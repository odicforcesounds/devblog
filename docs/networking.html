You may be a help-desk technician who resets passwords and now owns the switch closet. You may be a developer whose service “works locally” and dies behind a load balancer. 0.4 Lab safety and ethics

Everything in this manual can be practiced without touching a production network. Use Cisco Packet Tracer, GNS3, EVE-NG, containerlab, or a handful of cheap switches on your desk. The moment you practice on a network you do not own, you move from “student” to “incident”.

Non-negotiable

Do not run packet capture, ARP spoofing, deauthentication, port scanning, or “just testing” against systems you do not own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized access to a computer system is a crime in most jurisdictions, regardless of intent. Capture only your own traffic, in your own lab, on your own gear.

When you do lab, snapshot before you change. Label interfaces. Write the intended topology and addressing plan down before the first cable. The engineers who recover fastest are not the ones who memorize the most commands; they are the ones who can reconstruct what the network was supposed to look like.

1.1 What a network is

A network is a group of devices that have agreed on how to encode bits on a medium, how to identify each other, and how to decide who speaks next. That is the whole trick. Every protocol in this manual is a formal answer to one of those three problems, plus the policy layer humans add on top: who is allowed to talk to whom.

Two laptops on a desk with one cable between them are a network. So is a data centre with 40,000 servers. The difference is scale, and scale forces hierarchy: you cannot have 40,000 devices all hearing every message, so you introduce addressing, segmentation, and routing. Routing is how a network grows beyond what one device can remember.

Mental model

Think of a parcel. The street address is the IP address: it describes a location in a hierarchy that routers understand. The name on the door is the MAC address: it identifies the specific network card on the local segment. The courier’s route plan is the routing table. The packing slip is the transport header. If any one of those is wrong, the parcel does not arrive — and the tracking information lies to you in a different way each time.

There are two big families of delivery you need to keep separate in your head:

  • Connection-oriented (TCP): a phone call. You establish it, you acknowledge what you hear, you retransmit what was lost, you close it. Reliability has a cost: state, memory, and delay.
  • Connectionless (UDP, IP itself): a postcard. Fire and forget. Cheaper and faster; the application decides whether loss matters. DNS queries, voice, and video generally prefer this.

The OSI model is a teaching taxonomy with seven layers; the TCP/IP model is what the Internet actually implements, usually drawn with four or five. Neither is physics. The key consequence: a router normally cares only down to Layer 3, and a switch only down to Layer 2. Each one rewrites a different envelope.

Note what was proven and what was not. DNS and TCP/443 work. TLS, HTTP, authentication, or the application could still be broken. “Ping works” proves even less — ICMP may be permitted while the application port is blocked, and blocking ICMP does not mean the host is down.

1.4 Ethernet, media, and MTU

Ethernet is a family of Layer 1 and Layer 2 standards, not a single cable. The frame format you meet most often is Ethernet II:

The MTU is the largest payload an interface will send: 1500 bytes on standard Ethernet. That 1500 includes the IP header, so a TCP segment typically carries 1460 bytes of application data (1500 − 20 IP − 20 TCP) before options. A VLAN tag adds 4 bytes, so a tagged frame can be 1522 bytes on the wire while the IP MTU stays 1500. Jumbo frames (9000) exist in storage and data-centre fabrics but must be configured consistently end to end; one 1500-byte hop in the path silently breaks them.

Classic outage

Path MTU discovery relies on ICMP “fragmentation needed” (Type 3, Code 4). A firewall that blocks all ICMP creates an MTU black hole: small requests work, large responses hang forever. The symptom is famous — a website loads halfway, SSH connects and then freezes after login, VPN tunnels establish but no data passes. Fix the ICMP policy, or clamp TCP MSS on the tunnel, rather than lowering MTU on random hosts.

Copper twisted pair (Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6a) is cheap and limited to about 100 m for standard Ethernet. Fibre (multimode OM3/OM4 for buildings, single-mode OS2 for long haul) carries far more bandwidth over kilometres. What matters operationally: the optic must match the fibre, the wavelength, and the reach. A 10GBASE-LR optic will not light up over multimode patch leads, no matter how confident the installer is.

Duplex is the other silent killer. Full duplex means both sides can transmit simultaneously; half duplex means one at a time. Modern switched Ethernet is full duplex everywhere, and gigabit requires autonegotiation for duplex. When one side is hard-coded full and the other auto-negotiates, the auto side falls back to half — a duplex mismatch. The full-duplex side transmits whenever it wants; the half-duplex side detects “collisions” that are really the other side talking. Result: late collisions, CRC/FCS errors, terrible throughput, and a link that reports “up”.

The fix is boring: set both ends to speed auto / duplex auto, or hard-code both to the same values. Never hard-code one end only. And note that late collisions on a link that is supposed to be full duplex is not a cable problem — it is a configuration problem.

1.5 MAC addresses and ARP

A MAC address is 48 bits, written as six hex octets. The first three bytes are the OUI (vendor), the last three are assigned by the vendor. The least significant bit of the first octet distinguishes unicast from multicast; the second-least significant bit marks a locally administered address (common in virtual machines and privacy features). Broadcast is all ones: ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff.

ARP (Address Resolution Protocol, RFC 826) glues IPv4 to Ethernet. A host that needs to send to 192.168.10.1 on its own subnet broadcasts “Who has 192.168.10.1? Tell 192.168.10.10”, and the owner replies with its MAC. The result is cached for a few minutes. For a destination off the subnet, the host ARPs for the gateway, then puts the gateway’s MAC on the frame with the server’s IP inside.

ARP has no authentication. Any host can answer “that IP is me” and begin receiving traffic meant for the gateway. That is ARP spoofing, and it is the foundation of most on-path attacks in a flat LAN. Level 05 covers the defences (Dynamic ARP Inspection, DHCP snooping, port security, segmentation). For now, remember the design flaw: the protocol trusts whoever speaks first.

IPv6 does not use ARP. Neighbor Discovery (ND, RFC 4861) uses ICMPv6 Neighbor Solicitation and Advertisement, with Duplicate Address Detection built in. It is still unauthenticated by default; SEND (RFC 3971) exists but is rarely deployed, so ND spoofing is just as real.

1.6 What a switch really does

A switch is a Layer 2 bridge with many ports. It learns source MAC addresses into a CAM table (content-addressable memory), then forwards frames destined to a known MAC only out that port. Unknown destination, broadcast, and multicast frames are flooded to all ports in the same VLAN. That is the whole algorithm, and almost every Layer 2 mystery is a consequence of it.

  1. Learn — source MAC plus ingress port goes into the table, with an ageing timer (300 seconds by default on Cisco).
  2. Forward — destination MAC is known and not on the ingress port: send only there.
  3. Flood — destination is unknown, broadcast, or multicast: send everywhere in the VLAN except the source port.
  4. Filter — destination is on the same port it arrived from (common with hubs or a shared segment): drop.

A collision domain is the set of devices that share a medium and can collide. A switch gives each port its own collision domain. A broadcast domain is the set of devices that receive each other’s broadcast frames; by default, one VLAN equals one broadcast domain. A router (or a Layer 3 interface) is the boundary between broadcast domains. If you remember only one sentence about switches: they extend broadcast domains and break collision domains.

MAC flooding attacks exploit the CAM table: send thousands of frames with random source MACs until the table is full, and the switch falls back to flooding everything, letting the attacker capture traffic. Modern switches rate-limit learning and support port security, but the fundamental behaviour — “when in doubt, flood” — is why a compromised access port is a serious event.

1.7 Bandwidth is not latency

These are different quantities, and confusing them wastes money:

  • Bandwidth — capacity per second (Mbps, Gbps). It decides how long a large transfer takes.
  • Throughput — what you actually achieve, after overhead, loss, and protocol limits.
  • Latency — time for one bit to cross (RTT is round-trip). It decides how long a small request feels.
  • Jitter — variation in latency. It destroys voice and video, not file downloads.
  • Loss — dropped packets force TCP to retransmit and shrink its congestion window.

The bandwidth-delay product is the amount of data that must be “in flight” to fill a path: BDP = bandwidth × RTT. A 1 Gbps path with 80 ms RTT needs 80 Mb — about 10 MB — in flight to reach capacity. A single TCP flow with a small window cannot fill it, which is why long-fat networks need window scaling, parallel flows, or both. This is also why “we upgraded to 1 Gbps and the file transfer from the branch did not improve” is a normal, explainable outcome.

94 Mbps on a 100 Mbps link is healthy: Ethernet, IP, TCP, and TLS headers consume the rest. The same test across a 1 Gbps link that reports 94 Mbps is a red flag — check duplex, cabling, interface errors, and whether something is policing the port. Never accept “it’s the ISP” without a measurement at the demarcation.

1.8 Bad decisions at Layer 1 and 2

BAD

“We’ll just chain a few unmanaged switches through the office”

Someone needs three more ports in a meeting room, so a $25 unmanaged switch is daisy-chained off another unmanaged switch, off a wall port. Then someone plugs both ends of a cable into the same switch to “tidy up”.

Why it fails: unmanaged switches usually run STP, but they expose no controls, no logs, and no way to see the topology. A loop creates a broadcast storm that saturates every link in seconds; MAC tables thrash; the whole floor goes down and the only clue is “the network is slow”. There is also no VLAN separation, so a visitor’s infected laptop is on the same broadcast domain as finance.

Do instead: one managed access switch per area, uplinked once. Enable BPDU Guard and PortFast on edge ports, loop protection where unmanaged gear is unavoidable, and never create a physical loop “for redundancy” without STP verified end to end.

BAD

One flat /16 for the entire company because “routing is complicated”

A single 10.0.0.0/16 covers 65,534 hosts across offices, servers, printers, cameras, and guest Wi-Fi.

Why it fails: every broadcast — and there are a lot: ARP, DHCP, mDNS, NetBIOS — reaches every device. One infected machine can scan and attack everything. There is no meaningful place to put a firewall policy. Troubleshooting becomes “is it the LAN or the application?” with no segmentation to narrow it down. And a single subnet cannot span routers, so every remote site needs an ugly Layer 2 extension.

Do instead: a deliberate addressing plan: /24 per department or floor, /24 or /23 for voice, a separate guest VLAN, a separate management VLAN, and an IoT/printer VLAN. Route between them with explicit policy.

BAD

Hard-coding speed and duplex on one side only

An admin “locks the link down” on the switch with speed 100 and duplex full, but leaves the server NIC on auto.

Why it fails: the auto-negotiating side cannot detect the fixed duplex, so it falls back to half duplex. The link comes up and passes some traffic, which is worse than being down. Users report “slow” and the monitoring system reports “up”.

Do instead: leave both ends on auto for copper access ports; if policy requires hard-coding, hard-code both ends identically and document it.

Field heuristic

When a link is “up but terrible”, check in this order: interface errors and duplex, then MTU, then routing, then DNS, then the application. The physical layer lies less often than the layers above it, but it lies first.

IPv4 addresses are 32-bit unsigned integers, written as four decimal octets for human convenience. There is no magic in subnetting; there are eight place values and a mask. If you can add 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, and 1, you can subnet.

No — check it, because this is exactly the mistake that costs marks: 26 bits means 8 + 8 + 8 + 2 bits in the last octet, so the mask is 255.255.255.192 (11000000). The only formula you must internalise

Block size = 256 − mask octet. For /26 the interesting mask octet is 192, so the block is 64. Subnets start at 0, 64, 128, 192. For /28, mask 240, block 16. For /22 in the third octet, mask 252, block 4 — so networks are 10.0.0.0, 10.0.4.0, 10.0.8.0, and so on.

The * * * at hop 3 is not necessarily a failure. Many routers rate-limit or suppress ICMP Time Exceeded. Traceroute shows the path as ICMP replies reveal it, not a guaranteed map. Modern Linux defaults to UDP probes; Windows tracert uses ICMP Echo; both can be filtered differently.

2.3 Classes, CIDR, and why classful died

Early IPv4 used address classes: Class A 0–127 with a /8 mask, Class B 128–191 with /16, Class C 192–223 with /24, Class D multicast, Class E experimental. The class was encoded in the first bits, and routing protocols assumed the mask from the class.

This was catastrophic for allocation efficiency. A company that needed 300 hosts could not fit a Class C (254 usable), so it received a Class B: 65,534 addresses, of which 65,234 were wasted. The routing table also exploded, because every organisation’s block had to be advertised individually.

CIDR (Classless Inter-Domain Routing, RFC 4632, originally RFC 1519 in 1993) made the mask explicit in the route. That enabled two things: arbitrary prefix sizes (/26, /22, /19) and route aggregation, where one route covers many contiguous networks. It did not create more addresses — IANA exhausted its IPv4 pool in 2011 — but it slowed the routing-table explosion and made subnetting flexible.

Historical trap

Classful addressing still appears in old exam questions and in the default behaviour of ancient routing protocols such as RIPv1. When you see a question about “the class of this address”, answer it, then forget it for design work: modern networks are classless. The class of an address does not constrain your prefix today.

2.4 Masks, networks, broadcasts

Given an address and a prefix, three values matter:

  • Network address — all host bits zero. Not assignable to a host (traditionally).
  • Broadcast address — all host bits one. Reaches every host in the subnet. Not assignable.
  • Usable range — network + 1 through broadcast − 1.

For 192.168.10.130/26: the block is 64, so the fourth octet’s network is 128 (because 130 falls between 128 and 191). Network 192.168.10.128, broadcast 192.168.10.191, usable .129–.190, 62 hosts. The next subnet starts at .192.

With 172.16.35.200/20, the mask is 255.255.240.0. The interesting octet is the third: 240 → block 16. Multiples of 16 near 35 are 32 and 48, so the network is 172.16.32.0, broadcast 172.16.47.255, usable 172.16.32.1–172.16.47.254. Host bits total 12, so 4094 usable addresses.

Two exceptions worth knowing

/31 on a point-to-point link has two usable addresses and no broadcast (RFC 3021). /32 is a single host route — common on loopbacks and for advertising one server into a routing domain. Neither follows the “2h − 2” rule. If an exam question says “traditional subnet”, use the rule; if it specifies point-to-point or a host route, think before subtracting.

2.5 Subnetting the reliable way

People fail subnetting because they try to do it in their heads with formulas they half-remember. Use a fixed procedure. It is slower the first ten times and then it is faster than a calculator.

  1. Convert the prefix to a mask. Write all four octets. Do not skip the boring ones.
  2. Find the interesting octet — the one where the mask is neither 255 nor 0.
  3. Block size = 256 − that mask octet.
  4. Network = the largest multiple of the block that is ≤ the address octet.
  5. Broadcast = network + block − 1 in that octet (lower octets become 255).
  6. Usable = network + 1 → broadcast − 1. Host bits h give 2h − 2 usable addresses.

Worked example: carve 192.168.10.0/24 into four equal subnets

Four subnets means borrowing 2 bits: /24 + 2 = /26. Mask 255.255.255.192, block 64. The subnets are:

The second octet value is 18, not 200. 18 lies between 16 and 31, so the network is 172.16.0.0, broadcast 172.31.255.255. The 200 belongs to the third octet and does not affect the network boundary. Always identify the interesting octet before touching the numbers.

Speed drill

Practice with a timer: “How many usable hosts in a /27?” (30). “What is the network for 10.1.1.200/29?” (10.1.1.200? block 8: 200 is a multiple of 8, so yes, 10.1.1.200/29, broadcast .207). “Is 192.168.5.64/26 the same subnet as 192.168.5.100/26?” (Yes — both fall in .64–.127). Ten minutes a day for two weeks beats a three-hour cram session, because this must become automatic before the exam clock starts.

2.7 Summarisation and longest-prefix match

Four networks — 10.1.0.0/24, 10.1.1.0/24, 10.1.2.0/24, 10.1.3.0/24 — share the same first 22 bits. In binary, the third octet is 00000000, 00000001, 00000010, 00000011; the first six bits of that octet are identical, so the summary is 10.1.0.0/22. One route replaces four.

Routers choose the longest matching prefix, not the “best” or “most specific-looking” route. If a packet goes to 10.1.2.55 and the table contains both 10.1.0.0/22 and 10.1.2.0/24, the /24 wins because it is longer, regardless of metric. This is why a more specific route can silently override your carefully engineered path.

Summarisation can black-hole traffic

If you advertise 10.1.0.0/22 but only own and route .0/24 and .1/24, traffic for 10.1.2.x and 10.1.3.x arrives at your edge and is dropped. Summarise only ranges you fully control, and keep a discard/null route for the unused portion if the platform requires it. The same rule applies to IPv6 aggregation.

2.9 IPv6 in one sitting

IPv6 is 128 bits, written as eight groups of four hex digits, with :: replacing one run of zero groups. 2001:db8:acad:1::10/64 is a host in the 2001:db8:acad:1::/64 subnet. ::1 is loopback (not 127.0.0.1). There is no broadcast; multicast and anycast replace it.

A LAN is almost always a /64; a site commonly gets a /48 or /56 from its provider. That is not waste — 64 host bits are required for stateless address autoconfiguration (SLAAC) and are the basis of the addressing architecture (RFC 4291). Trying to “save addresses” with a /120 creates broken networks and tooling that refuses to cooperate.

Hosts configure themselves via Router Advertisements (RA). SLAAC builds an address from the /64 prefix plus an interface identifier; privacy extensions (RFC 8981) rotate that identifier so you are not tracked by a stable suffix. DHCPv6 can supply other options, and the M/O flags in the RA tell hosts whether to use it. In practice most enterprise networks run SLAAC + DHCPv6 stateless or stateful DHCPv6; both are legitimate. What is not legitimate is assuming “IPv6 is on” because the link-local address exists.

IPv6 has no NAT at the protocol level. NAT66 exists as a vendor feature but is widely discouraged: it breaks the end-to-end model, complicates multihoming, and provides no security benefit that a firewall does not provide better. The correct statement is: a firewall is the security control; NAT is an address translation mechanism (see RFC 4864). Level 05 expands on why “NAT hides us” is a dangerous belief.

Dual stack is a project, not a checkbox

Turning on IPv6 without updating firewall rules, monitoring, and ACLs creates a second, unaudited network. Hosts prefer IPv6 when it works (Happy Eyeballs), so a half-configured IPv6 path can look like random slow page loads: the browser waits for IPv6 to fail, then falls back. Either operate IPv6 deliberately or disable it deliberately — never leave it accidental.

2.10 Bad decisions in addressing

BAD

Using public address space internally “because it looks cleaner”

A team assigns 8.8.8.0/24 to a lab, or 172.32.0.0/16 to a branch because “it is practically private”.

Why it fails: those prefixes belong to real networks. Your hosts will believe 8.8.8.8 is local, your VPN will refuse to route the real destination, and your ISP may drop the traffic as spoofed or martian. Diagnosis is miserable because every tool reports plausible-looking addresses. RFC 1918 exists precisely to prevent this.

Do instead: use 10/8, 172.16/12, or 192.168/16 for internal addressing, and document the allocation. If you need globally unique space for mergers or cloud routing, get real assignments or use ULA for IPv6.

BAD

Every site uses 192.168.1.0/24, then someone builds a VPN

Head office, branch, and the new acquisition all use 192.168.1.0/24. It worked fine until the sites had to talk to each other.

Why it fails: a router cannot have two routes to the same prefix with different meanings. Hosts at both ends think the destination is local, so traffic never enters the tunnel. Workarounds (NAT-on-NAT, policy routing) add complexity and break logging, DNS, and inbound services.

Do instead: assign a unique block per site from a central plan: 10.10.0.0/16 for HQ, 10.20.0.0/16 for Branch A, and so on. If merging with an organisation that already overlaps, renumber deliberately or use NAT only as a documented, temporary bridge.

BAD

Static addresses inside the DHCP pool

A printer gets 192.168.10.50 configured manually. The DHCP server is configured to hand out .10–.200.

Why it fails: sooner or later the server leases .50 to a laptop. Two devices claim the same IP: ARP conflicts, intermittent connectivity, and a ticket that says “the printer works for some people”. Duplicate-address detection may catch it; often it does not.

Do instead: split the range: DHCP pool .100–.199, static/reserved .2–.99. Better: use DHCP reservations by MAC so the device is centrally documented. Exclude the gateway, switches, and servers from the pool explicitly.

3.1 VLANs: separation without new cables

A VLAN is a Layer 2 broadcast domain implemented in software on a switch. Ports assigned to VLAN 10 behave as if they were on a separate physical switch: broadcasts stay inside, and traffic between VLAN 10 and VLAN 20 must be routed. VLANs are identified by a 12-bit ID, so usable values are 1–4094; 0 and 4095 are reserved, and 1002–1005 are legacy defaults on some Cisco platforms.

The design value is not “security” by itself — it is fault and policy boundaries. A broadcast storm, a MAC flood, or a misbehaving IoT device stays inside its own broadcast domain. You can apply different DHCP scopes, ACLs, QoS policies, and monitoring to each VLAN. That is why the standard enterprise pattern is one VLAN per department, one for voice, one for management, one for guest, one for IoT/OT, and one for servers.

Two details in that snippet are deliberate. Ports are set to access mode explicitly; never trust the default, because a port left in dynamic mode may negotiate a trunk with a device that asks nicely. And the native VLAN is moved to an unused VLAN (999) so untagged frames cannot leak into a production VLAN.

Unused ports deserve the same care. Best practice is to put them in a “black hole” VLAN, shut them down, and enable port security. An unused live port in VLAN 1 is an open door in a conference room.

3.2 Trunks and 802.1Q

A trunk carries multiple VLANs over one link by tagging frames. IEEE 802.1Q inserts a 4-byte tag after the source MAC: a 16-bit TPID (0x8100), a 3-bit priority code point (PCP) used for QoS, a 1-bit drop eligible indicator (DEI), and a 12-bit VLAN ID.

The native VLAN is the one VLAN whose frames are sent untagged on an 802.1Q trunk. Both ends must agree. A mismatch produces the classic symptom: some traffic works, some VLANs silently leak into the wrong segment, and CDP logs “Native VLAN mismatch discovered”. If one side tags VLAN 10 and the other expects untagged frames in VLAN 20, you have built an accidental bridge between two broadcast domains.

DTP (Dynamic Trunking Protocol) is a Cisco proprietary negotiation that lets two ports decide to become a trunk. It is convenient in a lab and dangerous in production: an attacker who plugs in a laptop that speaks DTP can convince an access port to become a trunk and receive every VLAN. Disable it on user-facing ports with switchport mode access and switchport nonegotiate. If you need negotiation between switches, use it there only, and restrict the allowed VLAN list.

VLAN hopping has two classic forms. In switch spoofing, the attacker negotiates a trunk and joins all VLANs. In double tagging, the attacker sends a frame with two 802.1Q tags; the first switch strips the outer tag (the native VLAN) and forwards the inner-tagged frame into another VLAN. The mitigation is boring and effective: no dynamic trunking on access ports, a dedicated unused native VLAN, and an explicit allowed VLAN list.

3.3 Inter-VLAN routing

VLANs are isolated until something routes between them. There are two mainstream ways to do it.

Router-on-a-stick

One physical router interface carries a trunk, with a subinterface per VLAN. Cheap, simple, and limited by the router’s single link — every inter-VLAN flow shares it.

Switched virtual interfaces on a Layer 3 switch

A multilayer switch routes in hardware. Each VLAN gets an SVI with an IP address, and ip routing enables routing between them. This is the standard campus design: access switches at Layer 2, a core pair doing Layer 3.

The SVI is up only if at least one port in that VLAN is up (or the VLAN is explicitly configured and active). That trips people up: “the SVI is down” often means “no access port in this VLAN is up”, not “the switch is broken”.

Which to choose? Router-on-a-stick for a small branch or a lab; SVIs for anything with meaningful east-west traffic. A router forwarding 2 Gbps of inter-VLAN traffic over one 1 Gbps link is not a design, it is a bottleneck.

3.4 Spanning Tree, the loop police

Ethernet has no TTL. A broadcast frame in a loop circulates forever, and because switches flood broadcasts, each copy spawns more copies. Within seconds the links saturate, CAM tables thrash, and the network is down. Spanning Tree Protocol prevents this by electing a root bridge and blocking redundant ports so the active topology is a tree.

Root election

Every switch sends BPDUs containing a bridge ID: priority (2 bytes) + MAC address (6 bytes). The lowest bridge ID wins. On modern Cisco switches the priority field carries an extended system ID equal to the VLAN ID, so the effective value is priority + VLAN (32768 + 10 = 32778 for VLAN 10 by default). Ties are broken by the lowest MAC. The root bridge is not chosen by “the best switch” — it is chosen by configuration and, failing that, by hardware lottery.

Root placement matters because all traffic between VLANs tends to flow toward the root and then back out. If the root is a forgotten access switch in a closet with a 100 Mbps uplink, every VLAN’s traffic may take that path. Configure the core pair as primary and secondary root, and use Root Guard on ports where a superior BPDU must never arrive.

Classic 802.1D convergence can take 30–50 seconds (blocking → listening 15 s → learning 15 s → forwarding). RSTP (802.1w) converges in seconds or less and is the default on current Cisco hardware. MSTP (802.1s) maps groups of VLANs to instances, which matters on large networks where per-VLAN STP does not scale.

The protections that matter

  • PortFast — skips listening/learning on edge ports so a PC can DHCP immediately. Only on ports that will never connect to a switch.
  • BPDU Guard — if a PortFast port receives a BPDU, err-disable it. This stops a user from plugging in a switch and becoming root.
  • Root Guard — on ports facing other organisations or access layers, blocks superior BPDUs; the port goes root-inconsistent.
  • Loop Guard — if BPDUs stop arriving on a non-designated port (unidirectional link), prevents it from moving to forwarding and creating a loop.
  • UDLD — detects unidirectional fibre links that STP cannot see.

Never “just disable STP”

Disabling spanning tree on a switched network with redundant links is not a performance optimisation. It is a scheduled broadcast storm. If STP is causing a problem, find out why — a misconfigured root, a blocked uplink, an unexpected BPDU — instead of removing the only thing standing between you and a total outage.

3.5 EtherChannel

EtherChannel bundles up to eight physical links into one logical link. STP sees one link, so no ports are blocked; traffic is hashed across members per flow. It increases bandwidth and redundancy, but it does not make a single TCP flow faster — one conversation follows one hash and therefore one member link.

3.6 Port security

Port security limits which source MAC addresses may appear on an access port. It is a blunt but useful defence against MAC flooding, rogue devices, and a user unplugging a desk phone to attach a personal laptop.

  • protect — silently drops offending frames. No log, no counter. Useless for diagnosis.
  • restrict — drops and logs, increments the violation counter, sends SNMP. Good default.
  • shutdown — err-disables the port. Loudest and safest, but requires a recovery process.

Recovery after a shutdown violation: fix the cause, then shutdown / no shutdown, or enable errdisable recovery cause psecure-violation with a timer. Do not enable automatic recovery blindly — it turns a security control into a flapping nuisance.

3.7 DHCP snooping, DAI, IP Source Guard

These three features form a chain, and they are only as good as the trust boundaries you configure.

DHCP snooping

A rogue DHCP server can hand out a default gateway that points to the attacker. DHCP snooping classifies ports as trusted (toward legitimate DHCP servers and uplinks) or untrusted (access ports). DHCP server messages arriving on an untrusted port are dropped. The switch also builds a binding table of MAC, IP, VLAN, port, and lease time, which the other two features consume.

no ip dhcp snooping information option is a real-world compatibility fix: some DHCP servers drop requests with option 82 inserted by the switch. Only disable it if you have verified that behaviour; option 82 is useful for identifying the originating port.

Dynamic ARP Inspection

DAI intercepts ARP packets on untrusted ports and compares them against the DHCP snooping binding table. An ARP claiming an IP/MAC pair that does not match is dropped. This blocks the classic ARP poisoning attack used to become the man in the middle. It requires DHCP snooping to be healthy; static hosts need an ARP ACL or a static binding.

IP Source Guard

IPSG adds a port ACL that permits only the IP (and optionally MAC) bound to that port. It stops a host from using an address it was not assigned, including spoofing the gateway. It depends on DHCP snooping bindings or static entries, so plan for servers and printers.

Sequence matters

Enable DHCP snooping and verify the binding table before enabling DAI or IPSG. Turning on DAI with a broken snooping table drops legitimate ARP and takes the VLAN down. In a production change, do one switch at a time, during a window, with a rollback command ready.

3.8 Discovery protocols and Layer 2 bad decisions

CDP (Cisco) and LLDP (IEEE 802.1AB) advertise neighbour identity, platform, software version, and port details. Between infrastructure devices they are invaluable. On user-facing ports they are free reconnaissance: an attacker learns your switch model and IOS version, then looks up the matching vulnerability. Disable them where they are not needed.

BAD

“VLAN 1 is fine for management — it’s already there”

Every switch, access point, printer, and camera stays in VLAN 1, and management SVIs use 192.168.1.x.

Why it fails: VLAN 1 is the default on every port, the default native VLAN, and the default management VLAN. It is the first VLAN an attacker tries, and the one VLAN that is permitted on almost every trunk. A single misconfigured access port exposes the management plane to users. It also makes it impossible to apply a management ACL that actually separates operators from ordinary traffic.

Do instead: a dedicated management VLAN (e.g. 99) with an explicit ACL, no user ports in it, and a non-default native VLAN (e.g. 999) that exists nowhere else. Disable HTTP and Telnet on the management plane; use SSH only.

BAD

Trunking every VLAN to every switch “so it just works”

The uplink is configured as switchport mode trunk with no allowed list, on every switch, forever.

Why it fails: every broadcast in every VLAN crosses every uplink. STP instances multiply. A misconfigured or compromised port in a distant closet can inject traffic into VLANs it has no business reaching. Troubleshooting becomes harder because the failure domain is the whole campus. And VTP, if left in server mode, can propagate a VLAN database mistake everywhere at once.

Do instead: prune explicitly with switchport trunk allowed vlan …, keep VLANs local where they belong, and run VTP in transparent or off mode unless you have a deliberate, authenticated VTPv3 design.

BAD

Leaving edge ports dynamic and unprotected

Access ports are left in the default dynamic mode, with DTP enabled, no PortFast, no BPDU Guard, no port security, and no unused-port shutdown.

Why it fails: a user can plug in a switch, negotiate a trunk, and become the STP root — intentionally or by accident. A loop in a meeting room takes down the floor. A rogue device can MAC-flood the CAM table. This is not a theoretical attack chain; it is a Tuesday.

Do instead: explicit switchport mode access, switchport nonegotiate, PortFast + BPDU Guard, port security where practical, unused ports shut down in a black-hole VLAN, and DHCP snooping/DAI on user VLANs.

4.1 The routing table and the decision

A router forwards by asking three questions in order. Memorise the order; most “why is it going that way?” arguments are really arguments about step two or three.

  1. Longest prefix match. Among all routes that contain the destination, the most specific (longest mask) wins. Always. Metric is irrelevant until prefixes tie.
  2. Administrative distance. If the same prefix arrives from multiple sources, the source with the lowest AD wins: connected 0, static 1, eBGP 20, EIGRP 90, OSPF 110, IS-IS 115, RIP 120, iBGP 200.
  3. Metric. Within one protocol, the lowest metric wins. If metrics tie, some platforms install multiple paths (ECMP) up to a configured limit.

The bracket is [administrative distance/metric]. The L routes are /32 host routes for the router’s own interface addresses — that is why a router can answer a ping to itself even though the connected route is the subnet. The O IA route is OSPF inter-area. The S* is the default route, marked as a candidate default.

A subtle operational point: the next hop must itself be resolvable. If a static route points at 10.255.0.6 and that address is not reachable via a connected or learned route, the static route is not installed. This is recursive lookup, and it is why “the static route is configured but not in the table” usually means the next hop is wrong.

Connected routes are not optional

A connected route appears when an interface is up with an IP and a mask. If you configure ip address 10.10.10.1 255.255.255.0 and the interface is administratively down, the connected route disappears. No routing protocol can rescue an interface that is shut down.

4.2 Static routes and default routes

Static routes are explicit, predictable, and do not adapt. That is both their strength and their failure mode. Use them for small networks, stub sites, and default routes; use a dynamic protocol where topology changes.

Prefer a next-hop static route on Ethernet. An exit-interface route makes the router ARP for every destination in the prefix, which is fine on a point-to-point link but pathological on a multi-access segment. The floating static is a useful pattern: it sits invisible at AD 200 until the OSPF route disappears, then it takes over. That is a deliberate, testable failover — but only if the primary failure is detected. A static route cannot detect a black hole.

The default route is a trust boundary

0.0.0.0/0 means “everything I do not know about goes here”. On an internal router, a default route pointing at the wrong interface is how traffic for internal networks ends up at the ISP, gets dropped, or loops. On an edge router, it is how a misconfigured BGP session can attract your entire table. Always know which device is originating the default, and filter it at boundaries.

4.3 OSPF without mysticism

OSPF is a link-state protocol. Each router floods information about its own links (router LSAs), every router builds an identical topology database, and each runs Dijkstra’s shortest-path-first algorithm to compute best routes. It converges by detection and flooding, not by rumour, which is why it scales to large networks — and why a broken area design can flood the whole domain with churn.

Neighbours, not just routes

OSPF forms adjacencies by exchanging Hellos. On broadcast segments it elects a DR and BDR to reduce the number of adjacencies; everyone else reaches 2-WAY with each other and FULL with the DR/BDR.

A mismatch in hello/dead timers, area ID, authentication, stub flags, or MTU prevents adjacency — and OSPF is polite enough to log almost none of it at default logging levels.

Cost, reference bandwidth, and modern links

Cost = reference bandwidth ÷ interface bandwidth. The Cisco default reference is 100 Mbps, so a 100 Mbps link costs 1, a 1 Gbps link costs 1, and a 10 Gbps link also costs 1 — OSPF cannot tell them apart. On modern networks set auto-cost reference-bandwidth 100000 (100 Gbps) on every router in the domain. Mismatched reference bandwidth is a silent path-selection bug.

passive-interface default plus explicit exceptions is the safest pattern: OSPF stops sending Hellos toward user VLANs and branch stubs, but still advertises their connected networks. An interface that should form an adjacency must be explicitly un-passived.

Areas and LSA types

OSPF uses a two-level hierarchy. Area 0 is the backbone; every other area must connect to it (directly or via a virtual link). Routers in an area share a detailed topology; between areas, only summaries are exchanged.

  • Type 1 — Router LSA: a router’s links within an area.
  • Type 2 — Network LSA: generated by the DR for a broadcast segment.
  • Type 3 — Summary LSA: an ABR advertising networks from one area into another.
  • Type 4 — ASBR Summary: how to reach an ASBR.
  • Type 5 — External LSA: routes redistributed into OSPF from outside.
  • Type 7 — NSSA External: external routes inside a not-so-stubby area.

Do not redistribute into OSPF casually. A single redistribution mistake can inject a default route or a huge external prefix list and black-hole traffic. Always use a route map, always set a metric, and always filter both directions.

4.4 First-hop redundancy

If a PC’s default gateway is a single router and that router dies, the PC is offline even though another router is available. First-hop redundancy protocols give hosts a virtual IP and virtual MAC that can move between devices.

4.5 DHCP and relay

DHCP gives a host four things it cannot guess: an address, a mask, a default gateway, and DNS servers. The exchange is four messages, remembered as DORA:

  1. Discover — client broadcasts “is there a DHCP server?” from 0.0.0.0 to 255.255.255.255, UDP source 68, destination 67.
  2. Offer — server proposes an address and options.
  3. Request — client formally requests the offered address (broadcast so other servers know).
  4. Acknowledge — server confirms the lease, including duration and options.

Routers do not forward broadcasts, so a central DHCP server would never hear a client on another subnet. DHCP relay (ip helper-address) makes the router forward the request as unicast to the server, with the ingress interface’s address so the server knows which pool to use. The relay also inserts option 82 (circuit ID / remote ID) so the server can identify the access port.

Lease timers matter. A client renews at 50% of the lease (T1), rebinds at 87.5% (T2), and gives up at 100%. If you set a two-hour lease on a network with 300 devices and a /24, you will exhaust the pool when people return from lunch. If you set a 30-day lease on a guest network, you will exhaust it with visitors who left. Size the pool for peak concurrency, not total headcount.

Two DHCP servers, one subnet

Running two independent DHCP servers on the same segment without coordination causes duplicate offers, address conflicts, and clients that keep a lease from the wrong server. Use a proper split (e.g. 80/20 scopes with exclusions), DHCP failover, or a single authoritative server with relay. “It usually works” is not a design.

4.6 DNS and name resolution

DNS is a distributed, hierarchical database. A resolver asks a recursive server; that server walks the hierarchy from the root, to the TLD, to the authoritative nameserver, and caches the answer for the record’s TTL. Every layer of that chain is a place where your application can fail while the network is perfectly fine.

DNS filtering is also a real security control — and DNS over HTTPS can bypass it if you allow arbitrary DoH endpoints.

4.7 NAT and PAT

NAT rewrites addresses (and sometimes ports) as packets cross a boundary. It was a scaling workaround for IPv4 exhaustion; it became a default because it also happens to hide internal addressing. Learn the vocabulary precisely, because vendors and exam questions use it inconsistently.

Figure 4-4 · NAT terminology, with a real example

Static NAT maps one inside address to one outside address permanently — useful for a server that must be reachable from outside. Dynamic NAT maps to a pool on demand. PAT (overload) multiplexes thousands of inside hosts onto one public address by rewriting source ports.

NAT is stateful and directional: the router must see both directions of the flow, and the inside/outside designation must be correct. Asymmetrical routing through two NAT devices breaks it. So do protocols that embed addresses in payloads — SIP, active FTP, some IPsec configurations, and many game consoles. ALGs (application layer gateways) exist to rewrite those payloads, and they are a perennial source of bugs and vulnerabilities.

PAT capacity is finite: roughly 64,000 port mappings per public IPv4 address per protocol. A large office behind one address can exhaust ports, especially with many short-lived connections to the same destination (think a call centre hitting one API). The symptoms are intermittent connection failures that look like the remote service is down. More public addresses, fewer connections, or IPv6 solve it.

4.8 Time, logs, telemetry, QoS

These are the services that turn a collection of devices into an operable network. They are also the first things beginners skip, and the reason their incident timelines are fiction.

NTP

Time is a security dependency. TLS certificate validation, Kerberos, 802.1X, log correlation, and forensic timelines all fail or lie when clocks drift. Use at least three reliable sources, authenticate where possible, and monitor stratum and offset.

Syslog, SNMP, NetFlow

  • Syslog (UDP 514, or TCP/TLS with a collector) carries event messages with severities 0 emergency through 7 debug. Send it to a central collector; local buffers rotate and disappear on reload.
  • SNMP polls counters and receives traps. Use v3 with authPriv. SNMPv1/v2c send community strings in clear text — “public” is not a password, it is an invitation.
  • NetFlow / IPFIX exports flow records (who talked to whom, how much, when) without full packet capture. Invaluable for capacity, incident response, and finding the one host scanning the Internet.

QoS in one paragraph

QoS is not “make the network faster”; it is deciding who suffers first when it is full. Classify traffic at the trust boundary, mark it with DSCP (EF 46 for voice, AF41 34 for video, AF21 18 for transactional data, default 0), queue it (priority queue for voice with a bandwidth cap, weighted queues for the rest), and drop or shape at the edges. Policing drops excess; shaping buffers and delays it. If you mark voice as EF on the phone but do not trust DSCP on the switch port, the marking is erased at the first hop and the entire design is decorative.

4.9 Bad decisions in routing and services

BAD

“We have NAT, so we don’t need a firewall”

A small business puts a router with PAT in front of the LAN and considers the network protected. Then it adds port forwards for remote desktop, a camera, and a NAS “so we can access them from home”.

Why it fails: NAT translates addresses; it does not enforce policy, inspect application traffic, or authenticate users. PAT accidentally blocks unsolicited inbound connections, which creates a false sense of security — and every port forward is an explicit hole punched through that accident. Once an internal host is compromised, outbound NAT lets it reach the Internet freely, which is exactly what command-and-control needs. There is no logging of “allowed because it was already established” and no concept of who the user is.

Do instead: a stateful firewall with deny-by-default between zones, explicit inbound rules only for services that must be public, outbound logging, and a VPN with MFA for remote access instead of exposing RDP. Keep NAT if you need it for IPv4 addressing, but stop calling it security. See RFC 3022 and NIST SP 800-41.

BAD

Redistributing routes between protocols without filters

“Just redistribute OSPF into BGP so the branches can see everything.” No route map, no metric, no prefix list.

Why it fails: you have just advertised your entire internal topology to a protocol designed to carry the Internet’s table — and imported the Internet’s table into OSPF. A default route can leak inward, metrics become meaningless across protocols, and a routing loop can form between two redistribution points. The failure is intermittent, depends on timing, and is brutal to debug.

Do instead: redistribute only explicit prefixes with a route map, set a seed metric, tag routes to prevent feedback, and filter at every boundary. Prefer a clean design where redistribution happens at one or two well-understood points.

BAD

Blocking all ICMP “for security”

The edge firewall has a rule: deny ICMP from anywhere to anywhere. It looks tidy on the audit report.

Why it fails: Path MTU Discovery depends on ICMP Type 3 Code 4 (“fragmentation needed”). Block it and large packets vanish silently, producing the classic “site loads halfway”, “SSH freezes after login”, and “VPN connects but nothing works”. You also lose the fastest diagnostic signal in networking and make traceroute useless.

Do instead: permit the ICMP types you need — echo request/reply where appropriate, time exceeded, destination unreachable including fragmentation needed — and rate-limit them. Security comes from policy and inspection, not from blinding the network.

5.1 Threat models and the CIA triad

Security is not a product you install at the edge. It is a set of decisions about what you are protecting, from whom, and what you are willing to spend. Without that sentence, “we need to be secure” is a wish, not an engineering requirement.

Confidentiality

Only the right people can read it

Encryption, access control, segmentation, least privilege. Failure looks like a data breach or credential leak.

Integrity

Nobody changed it unnoticed

Hashes, signatures, ACLs, configuration management. Failure looks like altered records, injected routes, or tampered firmware.

Availability

It works when needed

Redundancy, rate limiting, DDoS protection, capacity. Failure looks like an outage — and outages are security incidents too.

A useful threat model names assets (what has value), actors (outsider, insider, automated worm, careless admin), entry points (Internet edge, Wi-Fi, USB, vendor VPN, cloud API), and impact (data loss, outage, safety, regulatory). Then it asks what controls reduce that risk to an acceptable level. Frameworks such as NIST CSF and the CIS Controls give you a structure; they do not make the decisions for you.

Two assumptions make designs dramatically better. First, assume breach: design as if an endpoint is already compromised, so that lateral movement is hard. Second, least privilege: every user, device, and API gets the minimum access required, for the minimum time. These are the core of zero trust (NIST SP 800-207), and they are mostly about segmentation and identity, not about buying a new appliance.

5.3 Firewalls, ACLs, and default deny

A stateless ACL evaluates each packet independently. A stateful firewall remembers flows and can allow return traffic for an established session. Both are policy engines; the difference is whether “related return traffic” is implicit or must be written explicitly.

Cisco ACLs are evaluated top-down, first match wins, and there is an implicit deny all at the end. Every ACL you write therefore ends with a deny you did not type. If you want to log what the implicit deny drops, add an explicit deny ip any any log at the end — but be aware that logging every dropped packet can melt a device under attack.

Notice what is not there: no permit ip any any, no “temporary” rule for a vendor, no management access from the whole Internet. The wildcard mask is the inverse of the subnet mask: 10.10.99.0 0.0.0.255 means the whole /24. A wildcard of 0.0.0.0 matches one address; 255.255.255.255 matches everything. Getting this backwards is a classic way to build a rule that permits far more than intended.

ACL placement is a design decision

Standard ACLs match only source addresses, so place them close to the destination. Extended ACLs match source, destination, protocol, and ports, so place them close to the source to drop bad traffic early. Putting an ACL on the wrong interface, in the wrong direction, is one of the most common “the firewall is blocking it” tickets — and the fix is usually show ip access-lists plus a counter check, not a new rule.

On modern platforms, zone-based firewalls replace interface ACLs with security zones and policies. Traffic between zones is denied until a policy permits it; return traffic is stateful. This maps better to intent (“guest cannot reach servers”) than a pile of interface-specific rules. The same principle applies to cloud security groups and network security groups: default deny, explicit allow, log the denies.

For device administration, TACACS+ is usually the better fit because it can authorize individual commands and log them. For network access control, RADIUS is the standard because 802.1X is built around it.

Always configure a local fallback account — and protect it. If the AAA server is unreachable, a switch with no fallback is a brick in a closet. If the fallback account is shared, it becomes the audit hole. Prefer a break-glass credential stored in a sealed vault, rotated after use.

802.1X: authentication at the port

802.1X is port-based network access control. Three roles: the supplicant (the device), the authenticator (the switch or wireless controller), and the authentication server (RADIUS, often backed by Active Directory or an identity provider). Before authentication, the port allows only EAPOL traffic. After success, the RADIUS server can return a VLAN, a downloadable ACL, or a posture decision.

The common enterprise pattern is EAP-TLS with device or user certificates, or PEAP-MSCHAPv2 with domain credentials. EAP-TLS is stronger because there is no password to phish or crack, but it requires a certificate lifecycle. PEAP is easier and still common; it is only as strong as the credentials and the server certificate validation on the client. If clients are configured to “trust any certificate”, an attacker with a rogue AP can proxy the authentication.

Deployment advice

Roll out 802.1X in monitor mode first. You will discover printers, cameras, and industrial devices that cannot authenticate. Use MAB (MAC Authentication Bypass) as a documented exception for those devices, and put them in a restricted VLAN — not in the same VLAN as authenticated users. Then enforce.

5.5 IPsec, TLS, and certificates

IPsec protects IP packets at Layer 3. It has two main protocols: ESP (Encapsulating Security Payload) for confidentiality and integrity, and AH (Authentication Header) for integrity only, without encryption. AH is rarely used because it cannot traverse NAT (it hashes the IP header, which NAT rewrites).

The security depends on authentication (MFA), patching the VPN appliance, and not exposing management interfaces alongside it.

TLS is not optional decoration

TLS provides confidentiality, integrity, and server authentication. In TLS 1.3 the handshake is one round trip and the certificate is encrypted, but the model is unchanged: the client validates a certificate chain to a trusted root, checks the name, checks validity dates, and checks revocation information. Fail any of those and the connection should fail — which is why “click through the warning” is a security incident, not a convenience.

HSTS (Strict-Transport-Security) tells browsers to refuse plaintext HTTP for a domain and, with includeSubDomains and preloading, closes the downgrade window. Certificate Transparency logs make mis-issuance visible. CAA records restrict which CAs may issue for your domain. None of these replace correct configuration, but together they make interception much harder.

TLS inspection is a trade-off, not a free win

An inline appliance that decrypts and re-encrypts traffic breaks end-to-end encryption, requires a private CA on every endpoint, and creates an extremely valuable target. It also breaks certificate pinning, some application updates, and client-certificate authentication. If you deploy it, scope it narrowly, protect the keys, log access to decrypted content, and document the legal/privacy implications. “We inspect everything” is usually a statement about ambition, not capability.

5.6 Segmentation and zero trust

Segmentation is the highest-leverage network security control you have. It does not prevent the first compromise; it prevents the first compromise from becoming the last one. The goal is to make lateral movement expensive: an infected laptop in the user VLAN should not be able to reach the hypervisor, the switch management plane, the backup server, or the OT network.

Zone design

Group by trust, not by cable

Users, servers, management, guest, IoT/OT, and DMZ. Each boundary is a firewall policy, not just a VLAN ID.

Least privilege

Allow the flow, not the subnet

“Users → app on 443” is a policy. “Users → servers” is a wish. Use application-aware rules where possible.

A VLAN by itself is only a broadcast domain. If a Layer 3 switch routes between all VLANs with no ACL, you have divided the network for STP and broadcasts while giving attackers a fully routed path between segments. Segmentation without policy is topology, not security. This is why “we have VLANs” is not an answer to “how do you stop ransomware from spreading?”

For guests, the minimum is: separate VLAN, firewall to the Internet only, no access to internal RFC 1918 ranges, client isolation on Wi-Fi so guests cannot attack each other, and a captive portal that does not leak credentials. For IoT and OT, assume the devices are unpatched and cannot run an agent: put them on their own zone, allow only the specific flows they need (often to a broker or controller), and monitor for new destinations.

Harden the management plane

The management plane is the shortest path from “a switch” to “the whole network”. A compromised management interface can create VLANs, change routes, mirror traffic, and disable logging. Treat it as a high-value zone:

  • Dedicated management VLAN, reachable only from an operations subnet or a jump host.
  • SSH only — transport input ssh, no Telnet, no HTTP server. Disable ip http server; keep HTTPS only if you truly need it, and restrict it.
  • AAA with individual accounts, MFA at the jump host, and command accounting. No shared enable password in a wiki.
  • Local fallback with a strong secret, stored offline, rotated after every use.
  • exec-timeout, login banners, and rate-limited login attempts.
  • Control-plane policing (CoPP) so a flood cannot starve routing protocols or SSH.
  • Disable unused services: CDP/LLDP on edge ports, PAD, BOOTP, proxy ARP where not required, unused SNMP communities.
  • Patch firmware on a schedule, and track end-of-support dates. An EOL switch with a known CVE is a permanent back door.

The management VLAN can be a trap

“Put management in its own VLAN” is only useful if the VLAN is actually reachable only by administrators. If the management SVI is routed from the user VLAN with no ACL, you have created a labelled target, not a boundary. Check the path, not the diagram.

5.7 Monitoring, logging, and response

Detection is a data problem. You need trustworthy time, centralized logs, flow data, and a baseline of what normal looks like. Without those, “we will investigate” means “we will guess after the fact”.

Practise the boring part

Run a tabletop exercise: “A laptop on VLAN 10 is beaconing to an unknown IP; what do you do in the first ten minutes?” If the answer requires finding a password, locating a console cable, or guessing which switch port the laptop is on, you have found your next project. Document the playbook before you need it.

5.8 Bad security decisions, explained

BAD

Managing devices with Telnet and HTTP “because it is faster”

The switch has Telnet enabled on the management VLAN, and the web UI is exposed on port 80. The reasoning: “it is internal, and SSH is slow to set up”.

Why it fails: Telnet sends every keystroke — including the enable password — in clear text. Anyone with access to the management path, a mirrored port, or a compromised jump host can capture it. HTTP has the same problem plus no integrity protection, so an on-path attacker can alter the page you are configuring from. “Internal” is not a security boundary: most breaches are internal by the time they reach the management plane.

Do instead: transport input ssh, no ip http server, SSHv2 only, AAA, and an ACL on the VTY lines. If you need a web UI, use HTTPS with a valid certificate and restrict source addresses.

BAD

SNMPv2c with the community string “public”

A monitoring system polls devices with SNMPv2c and the default read-only community. It has been that way since the last administrator.

Why it fails: SNMPv1/v2c send the community string in clear text, and the default strings are the first thing a scanner tries. Read access leaks interface inventories, ARP tables, routes, and sometimes running configuration. Write access — if the community is read-write — can change device configuration. This is not hypothetical; exposed SNMP is a recurring finding in Internet-wide scans.

Do instead: SNMPv3 with authPriv, per-device credentials, an ACL restricting pollers, and read-only views. If a device cannot do v3, isolate it and treat the community as a secret, not a label.

BAD

Port-forwarding RDP, SSH, or a camera/NVR directly to the Internet

“We need remote access, so we forwarded port 3389 to the office PC and 554 to the camera.” Sometimes it is a NAS, a PBX, or a building-management system.

Why it fails: you have published a service to every scanner on the planet. RDP and exposed cameras are among the most heavily attacked services in existence; a single unpatched box or weak password is enough. Cameras and NVRs are notorious for default credentials and abandoned firmware. Even if the service is patched today, you have created a permanent, unmonitored entry point that survives staff turnover.

Do instead: a VPN with MFA (or a zero-trust access proxy) for remote users, and a reverse proxy with authentication for services that must be public. Never expose management protocols. If a vendor insists on a cloud tunnel, scope it and monitor it.

BAD

One Wi-Fi password for 300 staff, no guest isolation

The office uses a single WPA2-PSK SSID. The password is on a whiteboard. Guests use the same SSID. “It is easier for everyone.”

Why it fails: the moment one device is lost, or one contractor shares the password, the credential is public and cannot be rotated without disrupting everyone. Every device on the SSID shares the same Layer 2 network — client-to-client attacks, ARP spoofing, and broadcast noise included. There is no per-user attribution in logs. A PSK network is a shared secret pretending to be authentication.

Do instead: WPA3-Enterprise (or WPA2-Enterprise with 802.1X) for staff, a separate guest SSID with client isolation and Internet-only policy, and a PSK only for a clearly bounded IoT SSID with its own firewall zone. Rotate credentials centrally; do not print them on walls.

BAD

“We have VLANs, so we are segmented”

Users, servers, and management are in different VLANs. The Layer 3 switch routes between them with no ACLs. The firewall only sits at the Internet edge.

Why it fails: any host can reach any other host through the router. A phished laptop can scan the server VLAN, reach the hypervisor, and authenticate to the management SVI. Broadcast isolation is not access control. This is the single most common gap between a network diagram that looks segmented and a network that behaves segmented.

Do instead: enforce policy at every zone boundary — firewall or ACLs on the Layer 3 switch, default deny, explicit flows, logged denies. Test it from a user VLAN with a port scan you are authorized to run. If the scan reaches management, the segmentation is decorative.

BAD

An “any-any” rule that was temporary in 2019

A vendor needed access for a migration. Someone added permit ip any any at the top of the ACL. The migration ended; the rule stayed. Nobody remembers who added it.

Why it fails: it silently invalidates every rule below it, so the firewall is now a router with logging. The audit still shows a long list of “security rules”, which creates false assurance. When an incident happens, the rule makes containment harder because there is no meaningful deny path to tighten.

Do instead: time-bound rules with an owner and an expiry date, change review, automated config diffs, and a quarterly rule recertification. Use a ticket reference in the rule description. “Temporary” without an expiry is permanent.

Authoritative reading

NIST SP 800-41 Rev. 1 (firewall policy), NIST SP 800-207 (zero trust), NIST SP 800-61 (incident handling), OWASP TLS Cheat Sheet, and Cisco’s zone-based firewall design guide.

6.1 RF fundamentals

Wi-Fi is a shared, half-duplex, contention-based medium. Only one device transmits on a channel at a time, and every device within range hears it. That single sentence explains most “Wi-Fi is slow” complaints: adding clients adds competition, not capacity per client. Ethernet switches give each port a dedicated collision domain; wireless gives everyone in earshot one.

Figure 6-1 · The three Wi-Fi bands in practice
BandChannelsBehaviourBest use
2.4 GHz1–13 (varies by regulator); only 1, 6, 11 are non-overlapping at 20 MHzLonger range, better penetration, heavily congested, only three clean channels.IoT, coverage edges, legacy devices. Not for dense user areas.
5 GHzMany UNII channels, including DFS channels shared with radarShorter range, more capacity, less interference. DFS requires radar detection and may cause channel changes.Primary band for laptops and phones.
6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7)Up to 59 channels at 20 MHz (regulatory dependent)Clean spectrum, no legacy devices, but shorter range and requires 6E-capable clients.High-density, modern clients, clean designs.

Signal strength is measured in dBm. −30 dBm is excellent; −67 dBm is a common target for voice; −72 dBm is workable for data; below −80 dBm is usually unusable. The noise floor in a quiet office might be −95 dBm; a microwave or a badly shielded power supply can raise it dramatically. SNR (signal minus noise) matters more than raw signal: −65 dBm signal with a −90 dBm noise floor is 25 dB SNR and is fine; −50 dBm signal with a −55 dBm noise floor is 5 dB SNR and is terrible despite the “strong” signal.

Channel width is a trade-off. Wider channels (40, 80, 160 MHz) carry more data for one client but consume more spectrum, reduce the number of non-overlapping channels, and increase the chance of co-channel interference. In a dense office, 20 MHz on 5 GHz is often faster in aggregate than 80 MHz, because more APs can reuse channels without stepping on each other. In a home with one AP and no neighbours, 80 MHz is reasonable.

MIMO and OFDMA do not repeal physics

MIMO uses multiple antennas and spatial streams; MU-MIMO serves multiple clients simultaneously in the downlink; OFDMA (Wi-Fi 6) divides a channel into resource units so small frames can share an airtime window. All three improve efficiency. None of them increase the total transmit power or make interference disappear. A bad channel plan with Wi-Fi 6 is still a bad channel plan.

Security, in order of preference

  • WPA3-Enterprise (802.1X) — per-user credentials or certificates, RADIUS, PMF required. The right answer for staff.
  • WPA3-Personal (SAE) — replaces the WPA2 PSK handshake with Simultaneous Authentication of Equals, which resists offline dictionary attacks. Still a shared secret, but a much better one.
  • WPA2-Enterprise — acceptable if WPA3 is not available, with PMF enabled where clients support it.
  • WPA2-Personal — only for bounded IoT/guest use, with a strong random passphrase and rotation.
  • Open / WEP / WPS — not security. WEP is broken; WPS PIN is brute-forceable; open networks require an overlay (VPN or OWE) to protect traffic.

Protected Management Frames (802.11w) prevents forged deauthentication and disassociation frames, which are otherwise a trivial way to knock clients off an AP. WPA3 requires PMF; enable it on WPA2 networks with capable clients. The old “hidden SSID” and “MAC address filtering” tricks are not security: the SSID appears in probe requests and beacons to anyone listening, and MAC addresses are trivially spoofed. They add operational pain and no meaningful protection.

Evil twin, not science fiction

An attacker advertises the same SSID with a stronger signal. Devices may roam to it automatically. If the network uses a shared PSK, the attacker can capture the handshake and attack it offline; if it uses Enterprise, a poorly configured supplicant that does not validate the RADIUS server certificate can be proxied. The defences are certificate validation, PMF, WIDS/WIPS that detects rogue BSSIDs, and user reporting — not “the SSID is hidden”.

6.3 Designing for capacity, not coverage

A single AP in the middle of an office can cover the floor with a usable signal. It cannot serve 150 laptops watching video. Coverage is a signal problem; capacity is an airtime problem. Design for the number of concurrent clients and their traffic, then place APs accordingly.

Roaming is a client decision, assisted by the network. 802.11k gives the client a neighbour report, 802.11v lets the infrastructure suggest a better AP, and 802.11r speeds up the key exchange when moving between APs. Each SSID usually maps to a VLAN. A common design: corporate SSID → VLAN 10 (802.1X), guest SSID → VLAN 30 (captive portal, client isolation, Internet only), IoT SSID → VLAN 40 (PSK, restricted destinations). More SSIDs mean more beacon overhead; keep the count small — four is already a lot of airtime.

6.5 WAN, MPLS, and SD-WAN

WAN design is an economics problem wearing a networking costume. You are buying distance, reliability, and sometimes a guaranteed latency — and every option trades one for another.

Figure 6-5 · WAN options compared

SD-WAN is not a protocol; it is an architecture. An overlay (usually IPsec) runs over one or more underlays (broadband, MPLS, LTE). A controller distributes policy. The edge measures the underlay continuously (BFD or proprietary probes), classifies applications, and chooses a path per flow or per packet. Voice goes over the low-latency link; bulk backup uses the cheap one; if latency or loss crosses a threshold, traffic moves automatically.

What SD-WAN fixes

Cost and agility

Cheap Internet bandwidth, direct cloud access, zero-touch provisioning, central policy, and encrypted transport without paying for MPLS at every site.

What it does not fix

Bad underlay

If both paths are congested, oversubscribed, or have 200 ms jitter, no overlay can create quality. You must measure and buy enough path diversity.

Two operational details are routinely missed. First, encryption adds overhead: IPsec reduces the usable MTU, so TCP sessions can black-hole unless you clamp MSS or adjust MTU. Second, QoS markings may not survive the Internet; your provider might ignore DSCP, so voice quality depends on low utilization and good path selection, not on marking alone.

MPLS is still the right answer where you need deterministic latency, strict SLAs, or regulated private connectivity — hospitals, financial trading, industrial control. The mature pattern is hybrid: MPLS for the latency-sensitive core, Internet/SD-WAN for everything else, with policy deciding what uses which.

MPLS is private, not encrypted

An MPLS L3VPN separates customers logically, but the provider can see your traffic and the path is not cryptographically protected. If confidentiality matters — and for most data it does — run IPsec or MACsec over the MPLS service, or terminate your own encrypted overlay on top. “It is a private circuit” is a statement about isolation, not about encryption.

At the Internet edge, addressing and routing policy matter as much as bandwidth. A single-homed branch has a single point of failure regardless of speed. Dual-homed branches need provider-independent addressing or careful NAT, BGP policy that does not accidentally become a transit path, and inbound failover that is tested. The most common edge failure is not a cable — it is a failover that was never verified.

6.6 Bad wireless and WAN decisions

BAD

“2.4 GHz reaches further, so we will use it everywhere”

All APs are configured for 2.4 GHz only, 20 MHz, maximum power. The office has 40 APs and 300 devices.

Why it fails: 2.4 GHz has three non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) at 20 MHz. Forty APs cannot share three channels without co-channel interference. Every AP hears every other AP, airtime is consumed by management frames and retries, and clients see “full bars” while throughput collapses. Legacy 802.11b/g devices also slow the entire cell.

Do instead: use 5 GHz and 6 GHz as the primary capacity bands, reserve 2.4 GHz for IoT and coverage edges, lower transmit power, and disable low data rates to shrink the cell. Design for reuse, not for maximum reach.

BAD

80 MHz channels across a dense floor

Every AP is set to 80 MHz because the datasheet says it is faster.

Why it fails: an 80 MHz channel consumes four 20 MHz channels. In a dense deployment, APs end up overlapping and sharing airtime. The peak rate for one client looks impressive in a speed test, but aggregate capacity and latency get worse for everyone. DFS radar events can also force an 80 MHz channel to move, disrupting the whole cell.

Do instead: 20 MHz in high-density areas, 40 MHz in moderate density, and wider channels only where APs are far apart. Let capacity come from cell reuse.

BAD

One AP per floor, mounted in a closet

Facilities insists the AP be hidden in the electrical closet, next to the metal door and the concrete column. One AP per floor is “enough coverage”.

Why it fails: metal, concrete, and closed doors attenuate 5 GHz severely. Coverage becomes patchy, clients at the far end associate at low rates and consume more airtime, and the AP serves fewer clients well. Hiding infrastructure is a real constraint, but pretending it has no RF cost is how networks get a reputation for being “slow”.

Do instead: survey the actual mounting locations, use external antennas where needed, and plan capacity per area. If aesthetics are non-negotiable, budget for more APs to compensate for bad placement.

BAD

Using a shared PSK and calling it “Wi-Fi security”

Staff, contractors, and the guest network all use the same WPA2-PSK SSID. The password has not changed in four years.

Why it fails: a PSK is a shared secret, not an identity. One leaked password compromises every device on the SSID, and you cannot revoke one person without disrupting everyone. There is no per-user accounting, no posture check, and no way to segment by role. Captured handshakes can be attacked offline; weak passphrases fall quickly.

Do instead: WPA3-Enterprise with 802.1X for staff, a separate guest SSID with isolation and a captive portal, and a dedicated IoT SSID with a rotated random PSK. Use certificates or MFA where the client population allows it.

BAD

SD-WAN everywhere, with no underlay visibility

A company replaces MPLS with Internet-only SD-WAN at 80 sites. Nobody monitors latency, loss, or jitter per path; the controller is configured once and forgotten. Voice quality becomes “sometimes bad”.

Why it fails: SD-WAN can only choose among the paths it has. If both providers share the same congested local loop, or if no one watches the SLA metrics, the overlay faithfully delivers poor packets over the best of two bad options. Encryption and policy do not create bandwidth or reduce jitter.

Do instead: instrument every underlay (BFD, IP SLA, streaming telemetry), define SLA classes for voice/video/bulk, alert on threshold breaches, and keep diverse paths (different providers or media) where the business depends on them. Test failover quarterly.

Standards and guidance

Wi-Fi security: Wi-Fi Alliance security and NIST SP 800-153. Wireless LAN security: CISA wireless guidance. For CAPWAP itself, see RFC 5415.

  3. Q3A client sees −62 dBm signal and a −92 dBm noise floor. What is the SNR and is it usable?

    WhySNR = signal − noise = −62 − (−92) = 30 dB. Above 25 dB is generally healthy. Note that SNR is not the same as RSSI, and a strong signal with a high noise floor can still be unusable.

  4. Q4Why is 80 MHz usually a poor choice for every AP in a dense office?

    WhyWider channels raise the peak rate for one client but reduce the number of independent channels available. In dense deployments, APs overlap and share airtime, so aggregate capacity and latency worsen. Use 20 MHz where APs are close.

  5. Q5Which ports does CAPWAP use?

    WhyCAPWAP control uses UDP 5246 and data uses UDP 5247. UDP 1812/1813 is RADIUS, TCP 49 is TACACS+, and UDP 500 is IKE. Do not confuse the wireless control channel with the AAA protocol.

  6. Q6Which statement about MPLS L3VPN is correct?

    WhyMPLS L3VPN provides logical separation and traffic engineering, but the provider can see the packets. If confidentiality is required, add IPsec or MACsec over the service. “Private” and “encrypted” are different properties.

  7. Q7What is the main value of SD-WAN over a set of Internet links?

    WhySD-WAN builds an encrypted overlay across multiple transports, monitors each path, and applies per-application policy. It cannot guarantee zero loss on a best-effort underlay; it can only choose the best available path and fail over.

7.1 Why automate at all

Automation is not about replacing engineers. It is about making changes repeatable, reviewable, and reversible. A human typing 40 commands into 40 switches will make a typo; the only question is which switch and how long it takes to find. A script makes the same typo 40 times in four seconds, which is why automation without validation is worse than manual work — it scales mistakes too.

Idempotency

Run it twice, same result

The desired state is declared; the tool only changes what differs. Re-running a playbook should not add duplicate config.

Source of truth

One place that is right

Inventory, addressing, VLANs, and policy live in version control — not in six spreadsheets and one engineer’s memory.

Guardrails

Make the safe path easy

Templates that produce valid config, pre-change backups, diffs, and automated rollback. Speed is a by-product.

The maturity ladder is worth naming because teams skip steps and fail: document → script → template → pipeline → intent. Document the process, script the repeatable parts, template the configuration, run it through review and tests, and only then talk about intent-based networking. Most “automation projects” that fail are actually documentation projects that were never finished.

Three data formats dominate network automation:

REST APIs use HTTP verbs as intent: GET reads, POST creates, PUT replaces, PATCH modifies, DELETE removes. Secrets do not belong in URLs

API tokens in query strings end up in proxy logs, browser history, and monitoring traces. Use headers, store tokens in a secret manager or environment variable, scope them narrowly, and rotate them. The same rule applies to SNMP communities and TACACS+ keys — a secret in a Git repository is a secret that has already leaked.

7.3 NETCONF, RESTCONF, YANG, telemetry

CLI scraping is brittle: output changes with IOS version, locale, and terminal width. Model-driven interfaces replace text with structured data. YANG defines the data model; NETCONF (SSH, TCP 830) and RESTCONF (HTTPS) carry it. The device validates the payload against the model, so an invalid leaf is rejected instead of silently ignored as a mistyped CLI command.

The CCNA-level takeaway is not that you must write NETCONF clients. It is that configuration as data is the direction of the industry, and understanding the model is more durable than memorising a vendor’s CLI. 7.4 Python and Ansible, minimally

Python is the glue language of network automation. Libraries such as Netmiko and NAPALM wrap SSH and model-driven APIs; Paramiko is the lower-level SSH library. The important habit is not the syntax — it is collect, validate, change, verify, and log.

Notice the finally block. A script that leaves SSH sessions open will eventually exhaust the VTY lines on the device, and the next engineer will be locked out. Notice also that failures are printed and processing continues — one unreachable switch should not abort a 200-device audit. And notice that the script only reads. Read-only automation is the correct first project.

Ansible’s value is the declarative model and the inventory. The ios_config module compares the desired lines with the running config and only sends what is missing, which is what idempotency looks like in practice. But modules are not magic: if you template a full configuration and push it blindly, you can remove management access and lock yourself out. Always render to a file, diff it, and apply in a maintenance window.

Read-only first, then small writes

Build a script that backs up every device’s configuration nightly and commits it to Git. That single, boring automation gives you change history, drift detection, and a recovery path — and it cannot break the network. Then automate one low-risk change (NTP, syslog, banners) with a dry run and a diff. Earn the right to automate bigger things.

7.5 Version control and safe change

A network change without a backup is a bet that nothing will go wrong. A change without a rollback plan is a bet you cannot afford to lose. The mature workflow is unglamorous and repeatable:

  1. Capture the before state. Running config, routing table, neighbour table, and the specific show commands that prove the service works.
  2. Write the intended change in a template or playbook, not in a terminal history.
  3. Render and diff. Compare the generated configuration against the running configuration. Read the diff; do not skim it.
  4. Apply to a canary — one switch, one site, one non-critical device. Verify with the same evidence you captured.
  5. Apply to the fleet in batches, with a stop condition.
  6. Verify end to end from the user’s perspective, not just “the interface is up”.
  7. Record the change and update the documentation.

reload in is a crude but effective dead-man switch on devices that support it. It protects against the most common remote-change disaster: you change the management path and lose access. The timer reboots the device into its saved configuration and restores reachability. Use it, and remember to cancel it.

For fleet operations, tools such as Oxidized or RANCID poll and version device configurations automatically; Git gives you review, blame, and diff; a CI pipeline can run syntax checks, policy checks, and post-change verification. Emulation tools (containerlab, GNS3, EVE-NG) let you test a template against a virtual topology before it touches hardware. None of this replaces judgement; it makes judgement cheaper to apply.

7.6 A troubleshooting method that works

Troubleshooting is not “trying things until it works”. It is a controlled experiment under time pressure.

  1. Define the problem in one sentence. “Users on VLAN 30 in Building B cannot reach the payroll application via HTTPS, since 08:10 today.” Not “the network is down”.
  2. Establish scope and impact. One user, one VLAN, one building, or everyone? Is it constant or intermittent? What changed? A precise scope eliminates half the hypotheses for free.
  3. Gather evidence without changing anything. Counters, logs, routes, ARP, DNS, and a packet capture if possible. Changing first destroys the evidence.
  4. Form a hypothesis and predict what you would see if it were true. This is the step people skip, and it is the difference between diagnosis and guessing.
  5. Test the cheapest, least invasive hypothesis first. A ping beats a reboot. A route lookup beats a configuration change.
  6. Fix, then verify end to end. The user’s application working is the acceptance test, not “the interface came up”.
  7. Document the cause and the prevention. “Bad cable” is a cause. “No monitoring on access-port CRC errors” is a prevention.
Figure 7-3 · Three traversal strategies
StrategyStart atBest forRisk
Bottom-upPhysical / interface layer“Nothing works at all”, link-down reports.Slow if the problem is clearly application-level.
Top-downApplication / DNS / user symptom“The site is slow”, one app failing.Can miss a physical cause that mimics an app issue.

A fourth strategy is underrated: compare with a working case. If VLAN 10 works and VLAN 30 does not, diff the two configurations, routes, and switchport settings. The difference is usually the cause.

Do not shotgun

Rebooting devices, changing several settings at once, or “reloading the switch to clear it” destroys evidence, prolongs the outage, and may turn a partial failure into a full one. If you must reboot, do it deliberately, with a backup, and say why. “It worked after the reboot” is not a diagnosis — it is an unfinished investigation.

Reading interface counters

  • CRC / FCS errors — corrupted frames. Cabling, optics, interference, duplex mismatch. Layer 1 suspicion.
  • Runts / giants — frames below 64 or above the MTU. Often duplex or MTU issues.
  • Late collisions — collisions after the slot time. Almost always duplex mismatch.
  • Input drops — the device could not process traffic fast enough or a policy dropped it. Check CPU, queue drops, and ACLs.
  • Output drops — congestion on the egress queue. QoS or bandwidth problem, not a cable.
  • Resets / lost carrier — physical instability; check the cable and the port.

Incident A — “The new VLAN gets no DHCP”

Four independent faults stacked on one VLAN. This is normal in real networks built by change-by-change accretion. The method matters more than the commands: scope, evidence, hypothesis, fix, verify.

Incident B — “Wi-Fi drops every few minutes”

Users report disconnects in a meeting room. The AP shows high retries and 78% channel utilization. Clients are sticky to a distant AP because transmit power is at maximum and there is no 802.11k/v assistance. The DHCP lease is one hour for a /24 with 400 devices, so the pool is also exhausted at peak. Fixes: lower power, add an AP for capacity, enable 802.11k/v, widen the DHCP pool or shorten the lease appropriately, and check for a deauth source with WIDS. “Wi-Fi is bad” is not one problem; it was four.

Incident C — “The app loads halfway for remote users”

Small API calls work; large file downloads hang. Ping with a 1472-byte payload and DF set fails, while a 1200-byte ping succeeds. The VPN path has an MTU of 1400 but the firewall blocks ICMP “fragmentation needed”. Fix: permit the ICMP type, clamp TCP MSS on the tunnel (commonly 1360), and verify with a large transfer. A bandwidth upgrade would not have helped.

Incident D — “OSPF neighbour is stuck in ExStart”

OSPF database description packets must fit the link MTU; a mismatch leaves the routers unable to exchange databases. Fix the MTU consistently on both sides. ip ospf mtu-ignore exists, but it hides a real design inconsistency — use it only as a documented temporary measure.

7.9 Bad operational decisions

BAD

Changing production without a backup or a rollback plan

A quick ACL change is typed directly into a production switch at 16:55. No config copy, no scheduled reload, no second pair of eyes.

Why it fails: the change blocks the management path, SSH drops, and the device is now unreachable. The person who knew the console server password is on holiday. A five-minute change becomes a multi-hour outage and a physical site visit.

Do instead: copy the running config, schedule reload in 10, apply the change, verify, then cancel. Require peer review for production changes and keep an out-of-band path to every critical device.

BAD

Rebooting as the first troubleshooting step

“Have you tried turning it off and on again?” is applied to switches, firewalls, and VPN concentrators before any evidence is collected.

Why it fails: it destroys logs and counters, hides the cause, and may trigger a second failure during the boot (a config that only works with warm state, a firmware bug, a full flash). It also turns a contained problem into an outage for everyone on the device.

Do instead: collect evidence first. If a reboot is genuinely required, announce it, back up, and capture logs before and after. Then investigate why the reboot was needed.

BAD

Automating a broken process at full blast radius

A first automation project pushes a VLAN change to all 300 switches at once, with no dry run and no canary.

Why it fails: a template error or a wrong variable becomes a 300-device outage in under a minute. Automation removes the human’s natural pause and multiplies whatever the template does — including deleting the management VLAN.

Do instead: render, diff, dry-run, canary, batch, and verify. Use --check where available. Keep a tested rollback. Measure blast radius before you measure speed.

  2. Q2An API returns 403 Forbidden. What does it mean?

  3. Q3Which transport and port does NETCONF use?

    WhyNETCONF runs over SSH, by convention on TCP 830 (RFC 6241). RESTCONF uses HTTPS. UDP 161 is SNMP, TCP 49 is TACACS+, and HTTPS 443 is not the NETCONF default even though some implementations offer call-home variants.

  4. Q4Two routers are directly connected but the OSPF neighbour is stuck in ExStart. What is the most likely cause?

    WhyExStart is where database description exchange begins, and those packets must fit the link MTU. A mismatch (1500 vs 1400) leaves the routers unable to exchange databases. Area/auth mismatches typically prevent adjacency earlier, and duplicate router IDs cause other symptoms.

  5. Q5What should you do first when a production incident begins?

    WhyPrecise scope and preserved evidence are what make the rest of the investigation possible. Changing or rebooting first destroys the data you need and may expand the outage. Fixes come after a hypothesis, not before.

  6. Q6What is the purpose of reload in 10 before a remote configuration change?

    WhyThe scheduled reload is a dead-man switch: if your change breaks management access, the device reboots into its saved configuration and returns. It only helps if the running configuration was saved first, and you must cancel it after verification.

  7. Q7An access port shows growing CRC/FCS errors. Which layer do you investigate first?

    WhyCRC/FCS errors mean frames arrived corrupted, which is a physical/link issue: bad cable, damaged connector, failing optic, electromagnetic interference, or a duplex mismatch. Fix the physical cause; the upper layers are symptoms.

  8. Q8Why should API tokens and SNMP communities never be committed to Git?

    WhyRemoving a secret in a later commit does not remove it from Git history. Treat any committed secret as compromised, rotate it, and store secrets in a secret manager or environment variables injected at runtime.

8.5 Exam tactics that survive contact

Subnetting under the clock

You should not be deriving subnet math during the exam; you should be recognising it. Before the questions start, write the mask/block-size table from memory on the provided scratch material: /24 256, /25 128, /26 64, /27 32, /28 16, /29 8, /30 4, /31 2, /32 1. Then every question becomes a lookup plus one subtraction. If a subnetting question takes more than 60–90 seconds, flag it and move on.

Read the question like a contract

  • “Which two” means exactly two. Selecting three scores zero for that question on many item types.
  • “Best” and “most likely” mean there is a distractor that is technically true but does not answer the scenario.
  • Exhibits — read the topology and the output before the answers. The answer is usually visible in the output if you know which field to read.
  • Negations — “which is not” is where careful readers lose marks. Underline it mentally.

Configuration simlets

Read every task in the scenario first, then configure in an order that does not lock you out: addressing, VLANs/trunks, routing, services, security last. Verify with the same commands you would use in production — show ip interface brief, show vlan brief, show ip route, show ip ospf neighbor, show interfaces trunk. Save the configuration: on many simulation engines the running configuration is what gets graded, and forgetting to save is a self-inflicted wound.

Partial credit exists on multi-task simlets. If you cannot complete one requirement, do the others correctly rather than abandoning the item. If a command is rejected, check the mode prompt before assuming the platform is broken.

Time and psychology

120 minutes is enough if you do not donate ten minutes to one question. Work in passes: answer what you know, flag what you do not, then return. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a question blank. If you finish early, use the time to re-read simlet requirements — not to second-guess 40 correct answers.

Do not use exam dumps

Brain dumps violate Cisco’s certification exam policy and can invalidate your certification and your testing account. They are also frequently wrong or outdated, and they teach you to recognise a leaked question rather than to operate a network. The exam is expensive precisely because the skill is valuable; cheating the credential devalues it for everyone who earns it. Cisco publishes the rules — read them before you sit the exam.

Scheduling, identification requirements, online proctoring rules, and retake waiting periods are set by Cisco and Pearson VUE and can change. Confirm them on the official exam page when you book rather than trusting a study guide’s summary.

The CCNA is a foundation, not a destination. It is valid for three years; Cisco allows recertification by examination or by continuing education credits. More importantly, the badge does not make you senior — the labs, incidents, and documentation you accumulate do.

  1. Q1How long is the CCNA 200-301 exam?

    WhyCisco lists the CCNA exam as 120 minutes. Plan two passes: answer what you know, flag the rest, then return. Check the official page when you book in case the logistics change.

    WhyIP Connectivity is 25% in the published v1.1 blueprint, ahead of the two 20% domains. It covers the routing table, static routes, OSPFv2, and first-hop redundancy — all of which depend on addressing and Layer 2.

  3. Q3According to Cisco’s published transition schedule, when does CCNA v2.0 begin testing?

    WhyCisco lists v1.1 availability through 2 February 2027 and v2.0 beginning 3 February 2027, with the 200-301 code unchanged. Always re-check the official exam topics before booking; blueprints are revised.

    WhyPacket Tracer is Cisco Networking Academy’s official simulator. GNS3, EVE-NG, and containerlab are excellent third-party labs that run real or virtual network operating systems; they are not Cisco’s official simulator.

  5. Q5Why are exam dumps a bad study strategy, beyond the ethics?

    WhyDumps breach Cisco’s certification exam policy, can invalidate results, and teach question recognition instead of operational skill. They are also frequently wrong because they are recollections, not the live blueprint.

  6. Q6How many usable addresses does a /26 subnet provide?

    Why/26 leaves 6 host bits: 26 = 64 total addresses, minus the network and broadcast addresses = 62 usable. This should be automatic before exam day.

  7. Q7Which device stops a Layer 2 broadcast from crossing into another subnet?

    WhySwitches forward broadcasts within a VLAN; routers terminate broadcast domains at Layer 3. A Layer 3 switch does this with SVIs and routing, which is why “the VLAN is the boundary” is only true until you route it.

  8. Q8Which command verifies that an OSPF adjacency reached full state?

    Whyshow ip ospf neighbor shows each neighbour’s state (FULL, 2WAY, EXSTART, etc.). The other commands are useful, but only the neighbour table tells you whether the adjacency itself is healthy.

A.5 Five RFCs to read first

  1. RFC 791 — Internet Protocol. Read the header and the fragmentation rules; skip the obsolete parts.
  2. RFC 1918 — Address Allocation for Private Internets. Four pages that explain half of enterprise addressing.
  3. RFC 3022 — Traditional IP Network Address Translator. The vocabulary of NAT, from the source.
  4. RFC 9293 — Transmission Control Protocol. The current TCP specification; read the state machine and handshake sections.
  5. RFC 8200 — IPv6 Specification. The header is simpler than IPv4; the behaviour is not.
