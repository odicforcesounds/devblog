The Arithmetic of
Armed Violence

A documented accounting of how many people are killed by firearms and explosives — by civilians, police, soldiers, and children — and an evidence-based argument that weapons are not instruments of solution. It closes with the methods that measurably do work.

00 — Abstract

What this document argues, and what it does not

This dossier rests on a premise stated plainly: a gun is always a bad tool. That is a moral claim, and no table of figures can prove a moral claim. What the figures can do is something narrower and more useful: they can show what the tool actually produces when it is used, at scale, by every kind of person who uses it.

So the document proceeds in two movements. First, an accounting. We count the dead by category — homicide, suicide, accident, police action, war, bombing — and by the hand that held the weapon: a civilian, an officer, a soldier, a child. Second, an argument. From that accounting we ask a practical question: does the weapon solve the problem it was produced to solve? The evidence assembled here says no, and says it consistently enough that the burden of proof has shifted.

A third movement follows, because criticism without construction is cheap. If weapons do not solve conflicts between persons, what does? Here the literature is unusually clear and unusually ignored: organised nonviolence outperforms armed struggle by roughly two to one; street-level violence interruption has cut shootings in specific neighbourhoods by double-digit percentages; restorative conferencing measurably reduces repeat offending; purchaser licensing is associated with large reductions in firearm homicide. We report those, including the places where the evidence is weak.

A note on intellectual honesty

This is an advocacy document built on real data, and it says so. Where a widely-cited claim turned out to be unsupported — gun buybacks, for instance, or the idea that hospital-based violence intervention is a proven fix for adults — the claim is marked as unsupported rather than quietly dropped. An argument that only works when inconvenient evidence is hidden is not an argument. It is advertising.

01 — Method & definitions

How the numbers were assembled

Four rules governed the collection of every figure in this document.

  1. Every number carries its year and its dataset. A death count without a year is not a fact; it is a rumour with a comma in it. "44,447" means nothing until it is labelled CDC/NVSS, 2024, United States.
  2. Modelled estimates are labelled as models. The global figures in this document come from the Global Burden of Disease study, which estimates deaths where civil registration is incomplete. They are the best available global figures. They are not censuses, and they carry uncertainty intervals that this document reports.
  3. Casualties are not deaths. Bombing datasets usually count people killed and injured. Mixing the two produces inflated headlines, so each table below states which measure it uses.
  4. Different datasets are not interchangeable. Where two credible sources disagree — and on conflict deaths they do — both are shown rather than reconciled by force.

Firearm death

A death where a gunshot wound is the underlying cause, recorded by intent: homicide, suicide, unintentional, legal intervention, or undetermined.

Explosive violence

Harm from manufactured explosives — bombs, shells, grenades, mines, cluster munitions — as distinct from gunfire.

Casualty

One person killed or injured. Used only where the source does not separate the two, and flagged when so.

Civilian

A person not taking direct part in hostilities. Explosive-weapon datasets count civilians specifically because they dominate the toll.

02 — The global ledger

A quarter of a million people, every year

The most complete global breakdown of firearm deaths by intent comes from the Global Burden of Disease study covering 1990–2016. It puts the annual worldwide total at 251,000 firearm deaths, within an uncertainty interval of 195,000 to 276,000.

Two things about that table matter more than the total. The first is the suicide share: roughly one in four firearm deaths worldwide, and in the United States more than six in ten, is a person killing themselves. A weapon kept for protection is, statistically, far more likely to be used against its owner than against an intruder. The second is the homicide share. Two-thirds of the global toll is people killing other people — not states killing enemies, not soldiers killing soldiers, but ordinary homicide.

Wider violent death

The Small Arms Survey's Global Violent Deaths database counts intentional homicide, direct conflict deaths, and killings during legal intervention, and deliberately excludes suicide. Its 2022 update recorded 531,000 violent deaths in 2020, of which 211,000 were firearm deaths. A subsequent report describes approximately 580,000 violent deaths in 2021, of which about 45% were inflicted with firearms. [4] [5]

Read against the GBD figure, the implication is uncomfortable and worth stating directly: firearms are not one hazard among many. They are the single mechanism through which a very large share of the world's intentional killing is done.

Wars, and the soldiers in them

The military is the category in which the weapon is most formally justified: the state issues it, trains the holder, and directs its use toward a declared enemy. It is also the category with the largest single-event totals in human history.

The Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded 154,000 deaths from organised violence worldwide in 2023, down from 310,000 in 2022 — a measure broader than state-based war deaths alone, which also counted 20,900 deaths in non-state conflicts in 2023. For 2024 Uppsala reports nearly 160,000 deaths in organised violence, including roughly 76,000 battle-related deaths in Ukraine and about 26,000 across the Israel–Gaza and Hezbollah–Lebanon conflicts, of which 94% were civilians or people of unknown identity. [39] [40]

Zoom out, and the same pattern holds at civilisational scale. World War II killed an estimated 70–85 million people. One widely used museum estimate divides that as 15 million battle deaths and 45 million civilian deaths — civilians at roughly 75% of the listed total. The museum cautions that estimates vary widely and that civilian deaths in China alone may have exceeded 50 million, so this is a source-specific estimate rather than a settled ratio. [42] [43]

Read those two paragraphs together and a striking thing appears. The category where weapons are most organised, most professionalised, and most heavily regulated is also the category where the people killed are least likely to be the people holding the weapons. The soldier's weapon does not mainly kill soldiers. It kills the people who live where the fighting happens.

“About one in two homicides globally involves a firearm.”

WHO, Global Status Report on Violence Prevention, on 475,000 homicides in 2012 [3]

03 — The United States

The richest country, and its ledger

The United States is the most completely documented case in the world, because it counts its dead through a national vital registration system rather than a model. In 2024 it recorded 44,447 firearm deaths — 13.1 per 100,000 people. [2]

The suicide majority is the argument

Most public argument about guns concerns homicide, because homicide is what appears in the news. The data says the weapon's primary human output is different. Sixty-two percent of American firearm deaths are suicides. And suicide is, uniquely among these categories, a problem where the method determines the outcome: the difference between a suicide attempt with a firearm and an attempt by most other means is largely a difference between dying and surviving.

That is why the American toll is not primarily a story about crime. It is a story about a household object that converts a transient crisis into a permanent outcome. The person who dies is, in the overwhelming majority of cases, the person who owns the gun or lives with whoever does.

Police killings

The state's own use of firearms is a documented category, not an abstraction. Because no comprehensive federal database existed for decades, two independent projects filled the gap. Mapping Police Violence recorded 1,365 people killed by police in 2024 and 1,314 in 2025 — all fatal encounters, including shootings, tasers, physical force and vehicles, not shootings alone. The Washington Post's Fatal Force database counted 10,429 fatal police shootings across 2015–2024. [6] [7]

These are lawful killings, in the main, carried out by people authorised to carry weapons and trained in their use. They belong in the ledger precisely because they complicate the premise that a gun in trained hands is a categorically different thing from a gun in untrained hands. The same instrument produces the same outcome; only the paperwork differs.

04 — The smallest hands

Children, on both ends of the weapon

In 2021, 2,571 children aged 1–17 died from firearms in the United States — a rate of 3.7 per 100,000. Across ages 1–19 the count was 4,733, and firearms accounted for 20% of all deaths in that age group, against an average of less than 2% in comparably large and wealthy peer nations. [8]

The same KFF analysis found that the U.S. firearm death rate for ages 1–19 was more than 9.5 times that of Canada, the peer country with the next-highest rate. That comparison uses U.S. data for 2021 and peer data for 2019, which is worth knowing before quoting it.

The trend is not ambiguous. Pew's analysis of CDC data recorded 1,732 firearm deaths among Americans under 18 in 2019, rising to 2,590 in 2021 — a roughly 50% increase in two years. [9]

Beyond the United States, children are killed and maimed as a direct instrument of war. The United Nations verified 5,301 children killed and 11,649 killed or maimed in armed conflict in 2023. In 2024 it verified 11,967 children killed or maimed. Between 2005 and 2022 the UN verified more than 120,000 children killed or maimed in conflict. [15] [16]

Children are also the ones who find the weapon at home. The category the CDC labels “unintentional” is small in absolute terms — 450 deaths in 2024 — and disproportionately young. A tool designed to be lethal at a distance does not distinguish between an intended target and a four-year-old in the next room.

05 — Explosives

When the weapon is a bomb, the victim is a bystander

Bombs differ from guns in one decisive respect: they cannot be aimed with precision at a person. They are area weapons, and the area contains whoever happens to be in it. Every serious dataset on explosive violence reflects this, and the trend is worsening.

Casualties from explosive weapons roughly tripled in five years, and the civilian share climbed from 59% to 89%. This is not a coincidence of two independent trends. It is what happens when area weapons are used in populated places.

The United Nations puts the general figure at about 90% of casualties being civilians when explosive weapons are used in populated areas, against about 10% elsewhere. The ICRC makes the same point about urban warfare: civilians are an estimated 90% of casualties when wars are fought in cities. [17] [18]

Mines and explosive remnants

Explosive weapons also kill after the fighting stops, which no firearm does. Landmine Monitor recorded 5,757 casualties in 2023 — 1,983 killed, 3,663 injured, 111 with status unknown. Civilians were 4,335, or 84% of casualties where civilian or military status was known, and 1,498 of them were children, 37% of civilian casualties where age was recorded. The 2025 edition reports 6,279 casualties. [13]

The 2022 figures were 4,710 casualties, 85% civilian, with about half of civilian casualties being children. The direction of travel is upward, and the victims are not combatants.

A truck bomb outside a federal building. The FBI's case history records 168 dead and several hundred injured, in a building that housed a day-care centre. [19]

Four hijacked aircraft used as guided weapons. The figure is the victim toll, excluding the 19 hijackers. [20]

Three cases, three technologies, one structural feature: in each, the people killed were not the people with whom the perpetrator had a dispute. A bomb is a tool for converting a grievance into the deaths of strangers. That is what it is for. There is no version of the device that does something else.

06 — The defensive claim

The one argument that would matter, and what it shows

The strongest available defence of the weapon is not that it is pleasant, but that it is necessary: that a good person with a gun stops a bad person with a gun. This is the only claim that could rescue the tool from the ledger above. So it deserves the most careful examination in the document.

The FBI studied 160 active-shooter incidents between 2000 and 2013, in which 486 people were killed and 557 wounded, excluding shooters. In 21 incidents — 13.1% — unarmed citizens successfully restrained the shooter. [21]

RAND's analysis goes further into the mechanics. Bystanders or security personnel attempted to intervene in 65 cases. Of those interventions, 44 ended the shooting, 12 were partly successful, and 9 failed. Among the 56 successful or partly successful interventions, 45 — 80% — did not involve armed defence at all. RAND identified only two cases in which civilian bystanders used firearms to stop a shooter; most of the eleven cases where gunfire stopped a shooter involved security guards or off-duty officers. These findings do not prove that an armed bystander can never help; that would be a stronger claim than the data supports. What they show is that the scenario at the centre of the armed-defence argument — the armed civilian who saves the day — is rare, while the unarmed intervention is comparatively common. The tool's advertised purpose is not its observed function.

And the costs are not symmetrical. A weapon present in a confrontation raises the probability that the confrontation becomes lethal, whoever is holding it, and it does so in every category this document has counted: the domestic argument, the suicidal crisis, the police stop, the school hallway, the crowded street. The benefit is a rare, conditional good. The cost is an actuarial certainty.

Evidence quality note

RAND's intervention cases are selected from incidents in which someone chose to intervene, and the FBI's active-shooter category excludes gang- and drug-related shootings. Neither dataset is a controlled comparison of armed versus unarmed response, and no such experiment could ethically be run. Readers should treat this section as the best available observational evidence, not as a decisive causal result.

07 — Why the tool fails

Five structural reasons a weapon cannot solve a problem

The figures above are a symptom. The argument is about mechanism. A weapon fails as a problem-solving instrument not because it is used badly, but because of what it is.

01

It addresses the person, not the problem

Every human conflict has a structure: a disputed resource, a broken agreement, a wounded standing, a fear about the future. A weapon can remove a participant. It cannot remove any of those. The dispute survives the person, usually with more participants and less trust.

02

It is irreversible, and it is used in irreversible moments

The 27,593 American firearm suicides in 2024 are the clearest evidence. A decision made in a crisis of minutes becomes permanent because the method allows no interval for reconsideration. A tool whose defining property is that it cannot be taken back is a poor instrument for resolving situations that change.

03

It cannot discriminate, and it is used where discrimination is everything

Firearms miss. Explosives do not aim. In populated areas, the UN estimate is that 90% of casualties from explosive weapons are civilians. A tool that cannot reliably tell combatant from child is not a precision instrument, whatever the marketing says.

04

It escalates the conflict it is meant to end

The presence of a weapon changes the calculations of everyone in the room. It invites pre-emption, arms races, and retaliation. The historical record of armed campaigns is not one of decisive resolution: it is one of longer wars, more dead, and settlements that resume. The nonviolent record, examined next, is measurably better at reaching the stated goal.

05

It transfers the risk to the people it claims to protect

A weapon kept in a home is, statistically, far more likely to be used on a member of that household than on an intruder. The risk does not disappear; it is redistributed, and it is redistributed toward the people the owner loves. That is not protection. It is a transfer of liability dressed as security.

A tool that cannot distinguish a target from a bystander, cannot be recalled once used, and cannot address the dispute that caused its use is not a solution to conflict. It is a permanent escalation of it.

08 — What actually works

Documented methods that solve problems between persons

Criticism is easy. The useful question is what has been measured to work. The methods below are ordered by strength of evidence, and each entry states what was measured, in what setting, and what the result was.

Strongest evidence

Organised nonviolent resistance

The largest comparative study of how mass campaigns achieve their goals examined 323 campaigns between 1900 and 2006. Nonviolent campaigns achieved their stated goals 53% of the time; violent campaigns, 26%. Roughly twice the success rate, across a century of cases. [23] [24]

Scope: campaign-level success at political goals, not a claim that nonviolence halves the probability of violence in every dispute. The 53/26 comparison refers to the original NAVCO sample and period; the dataset has since been extended.

Strong local evidence

Violence interruption and street mediation

Cure Violence, formerly Chicago CeaseFire, treats shootings as a public-health contagion and deploys trusted community members to interrupt imminent violence and mediate disputes. The National Institute of Justice evaluation found decreases in shooting hot spots in six of seven sites, with declines attributed to the programme in four. An NIJ summary reported a 16–28% decline in shootings in four of seven sites. [25] [26]

A 2025 systematic review covering 13 papers, 27 sites and 83 findings found that 68.7% of findings indicated reductions, with 32.5% statistically significant. Those are proportions of findings, not effect sizes, and the review's authors are affiliated with Cure Violence Global — a conflict of interest that readers should weigh. [27]

Honest caveat: results are context-dependent. A university evidence review describes the evidence base as mixed, and some evaluations were inconclusive or negative.

Randomised evidence

Restorative justice conferencing

A Campbell Collaboration systematic review examined 10 randomised trials of face-to-face restorative conferences, with about 1,879 offenders and two-year follow-up. Nine of ten trials pointed toward less repeat offending. The pooled effect was statistically significant but modest: d = −0.155, p = .001, with larger effects for violent than property crime, and improved victim satisfaction. Across seven UK experiments, the value of crimes prevented was estimated at about eight times the cost of running the conferences. [28] [29]

A 2024 evaluation of San Francisco's Make-it-Right programme found that assignment to conferencing reduced the probability of rearrest within six months by 19 percentage points — a 44% reduction relative to the control group. That is a programme-specific estimate, not the pooled effect of the method. [30]

Policy evidence

Purchaser licensing

Connecticut's 1995 permit-to-purchase law was associated with a 40% reduction in firearm homicide over its first decade in a synthetic-control analysis, with no evidence of a reduction in non-firearm homicide — the pattern one would expect if the law worked through firearm access specifically. [31]

A later four-state synthetic-control analysis estimated a 27.8% decrease in firearm homicide associated with the same law, and found no consistent relationship between comprehensive background checks alone and mortality. [32]

RAND characterises the evidence for permit-to-purchase laws as limited, and notes design and causal-attribution concerns. These are promising findings, not a guarantee that the same percentage transfers to every jurisdiction.

Promising, not proven

Hospital-based violence intervention

Programmes that engage people hospitalised for violent injury, with case management and support, are widely used and intuitively sound. The evidence is genuinely mixed. A 2016 Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma review screened 71 articles and reviewed 10, concluding there was insufficient evidence to recommend adult-focused programmes, citing small samples and self-selection bias. [33]

A Johns Hopkins-led review of seven randomised trials and six observational studies found mixed evidence overall. One frequently cited youth study reported intervention youth were 70% less likely to be arrested for any offence and 60% less likely to have criminal involvement — outcomes that are not the same as reduced reinjury. [34]

Included here rather than omitted because a document arguing for evidence-based alternatives must be willing to report where its own preferred alternatives fall short.

Supporting practice

Dialogue, social cohesion, and mental-health support

The WHO's Global Health and Peace Initiative recommends conflict-sensitive health programming and, where appropriate, strengthening dialogue, social cohesion and resilience to violence. WHO authors describe pathways including equitable services that build trust between citizens and the state, programming that restores collaboration between conflict parties, and inclusive community dialogue to repair relations. [35] [36]

On the mental-health side, WHO estimates that 22% of people who experienced war or conflict in the previous ten years have depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia, and recommends community self-help, psychological first aid, evidence-based clinical care and referral networks. [37]

These sources establish need and practice, not a quantified violence-reduction effect. Dialogue and mental-health support are justified on their own terms; they should not be oversold as measured violence-prevention interventions.

What the evidence does not support

Gun buybacks. RAND's 2023 review concludes the evidence is limited and mixed at best, with little empirical support for measurable reductions in firearm violence. Voluntary buybacks may remove guns and raise awareness; neither is the same as fewer shootings. [38] A serious argument for alternatives has to include the alternatives that do not work, or it is just a different kind of faith.

09 — Limitations

What this document cannot establish

  • Global counts are models. The 251,000 global total is a GBD estimate with an uncertainty interval of 195,000–276,000. It should not be quoted to four significant figures, and it should not be compared directly with U.S. vital-registration counts.
  • Datasets measure different things. AOAV counts reported casualties from explosive weapons; Landmine Monitor counts mine and ERW casualties; UCDP counts battle deaths; Small Arms Survey excludes suicide. None of these can be added to another.
  • Under-reporting is systematic and biased. AOAV's reliance on English-language media undercounts casualties in places without press freedom. Conflict tolls are contested by the parties to the conflict.
  • Observational evidence cannot settle causation. The nonviolent-resistance and violence-interruption findings are comparative and observational. They are strong evidence of association and plausible mechanism, not proof of a universal causal law.
  • The core claim is moral, not empirical. That a gun is always a bad tool is a judgement about what human beings should be able to do to one another. The data can show the cost of the tool. It cannot, by itself, tell you what to value.

11 — Conclusion

The ledger, and the choice it implies

Two hundred and fifty-one thousand firearm deaths a year, globally. Forty-four thousand four hundred and forty-seven in the United States alone, most of them suicides, most of them committed by people using the weapon on themselves. Six thousand two hundred and seventy-nine mine and explosive-remnant casualties in a single year, a third of the civilian ones children. Sixty-seven thousand people killed or injured by explosive weapons in 2024, eighty-nine percent of them civilians.

These are not the numbers of a tool that solves problems. They are the numbers of a tool that reliably converts disputes, despair and carelessness into bodies. The rare case in which a weapon interrupts a killing does not balance a ledger in which the weapon is the killing.

What does solve problems between persons is slower, less cinematic, and better evidenced: organised nonviolence that wins roughly twice as often as armed struggle; community mediators who walk into the argument before the shooting; restorative conferences that put the person who caused harm in a room with the person they harmed and reduce the odds they do it again; licensing rules that keep the tool out of the moment of crisis; mental-health care that treats the despair before it finds a method.

None of these require a weapon. All of them have been measured. That is the entire argument.
