    00

    Orientation: what is actually being tested

    The exam is not a quiz. It is a work order with a clock.

    Almost everyone who fails a Red Hat exam fails for the same reason, and it is never “I didn’t know the command.” It is one of three things: they could not find the command they half-remembered, they made a change that did not survive a reboot, or they broke a service and lost twenty minutes recovering instead of moving on. All three are process failures. This manual attacks process first.

    Performance-based means the machine grades you

    Red Hat’s individual exams are hands-on lab exams. You are given a live system (or several, plus a scoring host), a list of tasks, and a time limit. There are no multiple-choice questions. An automated grader inspects the state of the system afterwards: does the user exist, is the volume mounted at boot, does the service answer on the port, is the firewall rule permanent. In many Red Hat exams your work is evaluated as a snapshot at the end, so a task you “did” with a command that did not persist is simply a task you did not do.

    The single most expensive sentence in the exam room

    “It works right now.”
    The grader is not watching you type. It evaluates the machine after you leave — often after a reboot. If your change lives only in the running kernel, the running firewalld, or a login shell’s environment, the grader sees nothing. Always test the reboot.

    The map of the track

    For years the answer was simple: RHCSA (EX200) then RHCE (EX294). That is still the most common route because Ansible is the lingua franca of RHEL operations. But the current catalog assigns credentials to specialisations, and publishes an Enterprise Linux engineer path built on EX342 instead. Both start at EX200. RHCA is not an exam — it is a designation earned by stacking three eligible specialist exams on top of an administrator-plus-engineer pair within the same specialisation.

    Read the catalog, not the folklore

    Forum posts still describe an RHCE that is “the second exam after RHCSA.” That is now only one of the two published routes. Check the certification catalog and the individual exam page for the credential you intend to hold, and note which RHEL release the exam is based on when you schedule — EX200’s public page currently identifies RHEL 10.

    Red Hat does not publish a fixed task count or a numerical pass mark on its public exam pages. The commonly cited threshold is 210/300 (70%); treat it as a planning heuristic, not a contract.

    How the scoring really behaves

    Tasks are weighted. Some are worth a handful of points, some a couple of dozen. That produces three non-obvious consequences:

    1. Partial credit exists, but only for finished sub-states. Half a RAID array scores nothing; a correct user account with a wrong supplementary group may still score the account.
    2. A broken system can zero adjacent tasks. If you disable SELinux to make httpd work, you have traded one task for three. If you corrupt /etc/fstab, the machine may not boot and every later task is unreachable.
    3. Finishing is a strategy. Nine complete tasks beat six perfect ones and four unfinished experiments. The exam rewards triage.

    What the exam room is actually like

    • No internet. Red Hat’s lab environments normally have local repositories instead of the CDN. If you have only ever installed packages from the public internet, practise with a local repo.
    • Documentation is usually available. man and info pages are part of the operating system, so man firewall-cmd is a legitimate research tool. --help is too. A search engine is not.
    • Remote delivery is proctored. Webcam, microphone, a cleared desk, no second monitor, no phone. Read the environment requirements before exam day, not at 08:55.
    • You may get a free retake on a failed first individual-exam attempt, subject to Red Hat’s current policy. Plan as if you will not.
    • Certifications last three years. Renewal is by retaking the highest exam, passing another exam at the same level, or advancing a level — check the current FAQ for your specific credential.
    Bad decision #1 — studying by watching

    Watching a four-hour video course produces the feeling of competence without the recall. The exam requires you to type a correct, persistent configuration from a cold start while a clock runs. The only practice that transfers is breaking your own lab and fixing it. Every level in this manual therefore ends with commands you must run, not concepts you must recognise.

    Bad decision #2 — memorising the flag, not the model

    An administrator who memorised firewall-cmd --permanent --add-service=http but not why --permanent exists will forget --reload and score zero. Learn the model — runtime versus permanent, desired state versus current state — and the flags become predictable. This is the difference between an operator and a certificate holder.

    The exam does not ask whether you have seen the command. It asks whether the machine, tomorrow morning, still agrees with what you intended today. Working principle of this manual

    Build your lab before you read Level 01

    1. Get RHEL legally, for free

      The Red Hat Developer program’s Individual Developer Subscription provides no-cost RHEL for up to 16 physical or virtual systems for development, testing, demos, and small production use — self-supported, and explicitly not an organisational subscription. Register at developers.redhat.com and download the DVD ISO.

    2. Pick a hypervisor and give the VM real resources

      KVM/libvirt on a Linux host, VirtualBox, VMware Workstation/Fusion, or Hyper-V. Two vCPUs, 2–4 GB RAM, a 20 GB disk for the OS, and a second 10–20 GB disk you can wipe repeatedly for the storage labs. Snapshots are the whole point: take one after installation and return to it.

    3. Clone it twice and build a network

      You need a second machine for SSH, NFS, firewalld, and Ansible labs. A host-only/NAT pair lets both VMs reach each other and the internet without exposing either to your LAN.

    4. Take a “clean install” snapshot

      Name it post-install-clean. You will destroy this system at least ten times. Reverting a snapshot takes twelve seconds; reinstalling takes twenty minutes you could have spent practising.

    01

    The shell as an instrument

    Files, streams, permissions, and the three seconds before you press Enter.

    Every later level assumes fluency here. Not “I can cd” — fluency means you can answer “where did that output go?”, “why can’t this process read that file?”, and “what will this command do to the other eleven files matching the glob?” before you run it. EX200 begins with an essential tools domain that is deceptively small: redirection, grep, regex, SSH, archives, links, permissions, documentation. Those objectives are worth roughly a fifth of the exam, and they are the ones people lose points on because they stopped thinking about them years ago.

    Streams: the plumbing you cannot skip

    A process gets three file descriptors. Standard input (0) is what it reads, standard output (1) is what it prints, standard error (2) is where it complains. Redirection is just reassigning descriptors, and the shell applies it before the command runs.

    streams.shshell
    # Send stdout to a file, truncating it
$ ls -l /etc > /tmp/etc.txt

# Append instead of truncate
$ ls -l /var/log >> /tmp/etc.txt

# Capture BOTH streams into one file (order matters)
$ dnf install httpd > /tmp/install.log 2>&1

# Discard noise, keep the signal
$ find / -name "*.conf" 2>/dev/null | wc -l

# Feed a file as stdin
$ sort < /etc/passwd

# Here-document: multi-line stdin inside a script
$ cat <<'EOF' > /tmp/motd
Welcome to rhel.lab.example.com
Authorised use only.
EOF
    The classic order mistake

    2>&1 > file and > file 2>&1 are not the same. In the first, stderr is pointed at the terminal (stdout’s current target) and then stdout is moved to the file, so errors still hit your screen. In the second, stdout goes to the file first and stderr is then duplicated onto it. Only the second captures both. Redirections are evaluated left to right, like assignments.

    Permissions: the model, then the syntax

    Mode bits are an octal triplet per class: owner, group, others. rwx is 4+2+1, so 750 means owner rwx, group r-x, others nothing. Two extra concepts decide most exam tasks: umask (default mode is 666 & ~umask for files, 777 & ~umask for directories) and the special bits.

    Special bits that appear in real incident reports
    BitOctalEffectLegitimate use
    setuid4000Runs with the file owner’s UIDpasswd, su, mount — root-owned, tiny, audited
    setgid2000File: runs with group. Directory: new files inherit the directory’s groupShared team directories — the correct fix for “my colleague can’t edit it”
    sticky1000Only the owner may delete their file in a shared directory/tmp, /var/tmp
    permissions.shshell
    # Symbolic and octal are equally valid on the exam
# chmod u=rw,g=r,o= /etc/motd
# chmod 640 /etc/motd

# Recursive ownership, then verify
# chown -R apache:apache /var/www/html
# ls -ld /var/www/html

# Shared directory: setgid + sticky, group-writable, no world access
# mkdir -p /srv/shared
# chgrp devs /srv/shared
# chmod 2770 /srv/shared

# Find everything world-writable outside volatile dirs — an audit staple
# find / -xdev -type f -perm -0002 ! -path '/tmp/*' ! -path '/var/tmp/*' -ls 2>/dev/null

# Find SUID binaries (an attacker's shopping list)
# find / -xdev -perm -4000 -type f -exec ls -l {} \; 2>/dev/null
    Bad decision #3 — chmod -R 777 to “fix” a permission problem

    It works, in the same way that removing the front door fixes a broken lock. World-writable web content lets any local user — including a compromised service account — replace your PHP or your static assets, and it usually breaks SELinux expectations at the same time. It also hides the real cause, which is almost always one of: wrong owner, wrong group, missing traverse (x) permission on a parent directory, or a bad umask. Diagnose with namei -l /var/www/html/index.php, which prints the effective permissions of every path component. Then fix the one that is wrong.

    Links: two different objects

    A hard link is a second name for the same inode. It cannot cross filesystems and cannot point at a directory. A soft (symbolic) link is a tiny file containing a path; it can cross filesystems, can dangle, and can point at directories — which is why every service configuration in /etc that “just points somewhere” is a symlink.

    links.shshell
    # ln /etc/hosts /root/hosts-hard        # same inode, survives deleting the original name
# ln -s /opt/app/current/release.conf /etc/app.conf

# Prove the difference
$ stat -c '%i %h %n' /etc/hosts /root/hosts-hard
$ ls -li /etc/app.conf

# A dangling symlink is not an error to the filesystem, only to the app
$ readlink -f /etc/app.conf

    Finding things, archiving things

    find-and-tar.shshell
    # find is a query language: path, test, action
# find /etc -type f -name '*.conf' -newermt '-7 days' -printf '%TY-%Tm-%Td %p\n' | sort
# find /var/log -type f -size +50M -exec ls -lh {} +
# find /srv -type d -name cache -prune -o -type f -name '*.log' -print

# Archives: c=create x=extract t=list f=file z=gzip J=xz j=bzip2 v=verbose
# tar -czf /var/tmp/etc-$(date +%F).tar.gz /etc
# tar -tzf /var/tmp/etc-2026-10-20.tar.gz | head
# tar -xzf /var/tmp/etc-2026-10-20.tar.gz -C /root/restore

# Preserve SELinux labels and ownership when archiving a whole tree
# tar --selinux -cpzf /var/tmp/srv.tar.gz /srv
# restorecon -Rv /srv
    Exam habit: verify, do not assume

    After every archive or mount task, list the contents or read the mount table. tar -tzf before you delete the source; findmnt /data after you mount it; ss -tlnp after you start a service. Verification is not extra credit — it is how you find out you scored zero while there is still time to fix it.

    Documentation: the only offline search engine you get

    man.shshell
    $ man 5 passwd            # section 5 = file formats
$ man 8 mount             # section 8 = admin commands
$ man -k selinux          # same as apropos: search descriptions
$ mandb                   # rebuild the index if man -k finds nothing
$ rpm -qd httpd           # list docs shipped by a package
$ ls /usr/share/doc/httpd/
    Useful habit

    Inside man: / searches forward, n jumps to the next hit, G goes to the end (where EXAMPLES and SEE ALSO live), q quits. The EXAMPLES section is where exam answers hide. man -k fails on minimal installs until mandb has run once.

    02

    Installation and first boot

    Anaconda, partitioning, subscriptions — and the twenty minutes that decide everything after.

    You will not be asked to click through the installer on the exam. You will be asked to produce the result the installer would have produced: a system that boots to a defined target, with the right filesystem layout, the right time source, the right hostname, and a working subscription or local repository. Learn the installer because it is the fastest way to internalise those results.

    Step 0 — get the media and verify it

    1. Register with the Red Hat Developer program and download the DVD ISO

      The Individual Developer Subscription is free and covers up to 16 systems; the download page is developers.redhat.com/products/rhel/download. You get the full product, not a “community edition” with features removed.

    2. Verify the checksum before you build anything on it

      A truncated ISO produces installer errors that look like hardware faults. Compare against Red Hat’s published SHA-256.

    3. Decide the installation source

      DVD ISO attached to the VM, a bootable USB for bare metal, or network boot (PXE) in a datacentre. The exam lab uses its own repositories; your home lab should use the CDN or a local repo mirror.

    A defensible partition layout

    Nothing in the exam requires a specific layout, but a layout you can reason about makes every storage task easier. This is the one used throughout this manual:

    If you use LVM with a separate /var

    Make sure /var and / are separate logical volumes inside the same volume group, and that you know how to extend /var online with lvextend -r. A container-heavy host will fill /var/lib/containers long before it fills /.

    Post-install configuration, in the order that avoids pain

    The first ten minutes after first boot are where a machine becomes maintainable or becomes folklore. Work in this order, because each step makes the next one possible.

    1. Identity and time

      Set the hostname to a fully qualified name, the timezone, and NTP. hostnamectl and timedatectl are the supported interfaces; they write the right files for you.

    2. Register the system (or configure a local repo)

      Without a valid subscription or a reachable repository, nothing else in this manual is installable. In the exam lab you will typically be given a repository URL instead.

    3. Update, then snapshot

      Update first so your snapshot matches the patched state you will actually work from.

    4. Install your working set once

      A minimal set that covers 90% of the labs: vim-enhanced, bash-completion, tar, rsync, tree, policycoreutils-python-utils, setroubleshoot-server, firewalld, nfs-utils, autofs, container-tools.

    5. Prove sudo works before you need it

      Open a second SSH session as the admin user, run sudo -v, and only then harden SSH.

    Subscriptions without a subscription

    Red Hat exams and many enterprise labs do not give you the CDN. They give you a repository. Knowing the difference between the three sources of packages is a genuine exam skill:

    CDN / subscription-manager

    Entitlement-based. /etc/yum.repos.d/redhat.repo is generated and managed by subscription-manager. Editing it by hand is pointless — it gets rewritten.

    Local file repo

    A DVD mounted at /mnt/dvd, with a .repo file pointing at file:///mnt/dvd/BaseOS. No network required. This is what the exam typically provides.

    HTTP repo

    An internal mirror or a lab server. Same .repo syntax with baseurl=http://…, and GPG checking is your only integrity control.

    /etc/yum.repos.d/local-dvd.reporepo file
    [local-baseos]
name=Local RHEL BaseOS
baseurl=file:///mnt/dvd/BaseOS
enabled=1
gpgcheck=1
gpgkey=file:///etc/pki/rpm-gpg/RPM-GPG-KEY-redhat-release

[local-appstream]
name=Local RHEL AppStream
baseurl=file:///mnt/dvd/AppStream
enabled=1
gpgcheck=1
gpgkey=file:///etc/pki/rpm-gpg/RPM-GPG-KEY-redhat-release
    Bad decision #4 — gpgcheck=0 because the key “was annoying”

    GPG checking is the only thing that distinguishes a signed Red Hat package from a tampered one on a mirror you do not control. Turning it off converts every future dnf update into a supply-chain gamble, and on an exam host it can mask a repository that is subtly wrong. Import the key properly (rpm --import /etc/pki/rpm-gpg/RPM-GPG-KEY-redhat-release) and keep the check on.

    Packages, repositories, and the supply chain

    dnf, RPM, AppStream, Flatpak — and why rollback is not a time machine.

    RHEL is a curated distribution: the vendor signs packages, publishes them in repositories, and supports the combinations it shipped. Your job is to install, query, verify, and audit that flow without breaking the curation. The exam tests the mechanics; production punishes misunderstanding them.

    History, undo, and the trap

    Every transaction is recorded. This is genuinely useful for auditing and for reversing a single package change. It is not a system rollback, and RHEL’s own documentation warns that downgrading core system packages through it — kernel, glibc, SELinux libraries — is unsupported.

    dnf-history.shshell

    Bad decision #5 — treating dnf history rollback as “undo my bad week”

    Three reasons this fails in production. First, the old versions may no longer exist in the repo, so the transaction cannot be solved. Second, it does not touch configuration files you edited — a bad sshd_config survives a rollback untouched. Third, it can leave the running kernel, SELinux policy, and userspace libraries mismatched, which is exactly the state Red Hat documents as unsupported. The correct tools are: revert the configuration from version control, restore the VM snapshot, or build a replacement host. Use history undo for a single package you just added; never for an operating-system downgrade.

    systemd is not an init script runner. Treat it as the state machine it is.

    Partitions first

    Device names are not stable across reboots, controller changes, or hot-plug. sdb on your laptop is not sdb on the server.     fstab rules that prevent outages
    • Use UUID= or LABEL=, not /dev/sdb1.
    • Add _netdev to NFS/SMB entries so boot does not hang waiting for a network that is not up yet.
    • Add nofail for non-critical mounts (removable media, secondary data disks) so a missing device does not drop you to emergency mode.
    • Always run mount -a and check the exit status before rebooting.
    • systemctl daemon-reload after editing fstab, because systemd generates mount units from it.
    Bad decision #10 — a typo in fstab, then a reboot

    A bad fstab entry drops the system to emergency mode with a read-only root. On a remote server that is an outage. If it happens: log in at the console, mount -o remount,rw /, fix the entry, systemctl daemon-reload, reboot. If it happens on the exam, you have burned ten minutes and possibly the tasks that needed the machine. Prevention is mount -a plus a typed confirmation of the UUID from blkid.

    firewalld: zones, runtime, permanent

    firewalld’s model has three axes and candidates confuse them constantly:

    • Zone — a trust level attached to an interface or a source network. Default is usually public.
    • Runtime vs permanent — runtime is what is active now; permanent is what the XML on disk says. A reload copies permanent into runtime. Permanent changes do not take effect until reload.
    • Service vs port — a named service (http = 80/tcp) versus an explicit port/protocol pair.

    This proves nothing you cannot prove in one command (ss -tlnp), and it leaves the host defenceless if you forget to start it again — which, under exam pressure, people do. Worse, a stopped firewalld also defeats the permanent configuration, so the grading snapshot sees a machine with no firewall at all. If a port does not answer, check the listen socket, then the zone, then the SELinux port label. Never disable the firewall to isolate a bug.

    Bad decision #13 — forgetting --reload after --permanent

    The command returns success and changes nothing in the running firewall. Under exam conditions this feels like a working solution until the grader checks. The habit that prevents it: finish every firewall task with firewall-cmd --list-all and read the output. If your service is not in the list, you did not open it.

    A useful mental shortcut

    Three commands answer “why can’t I reach this service?” in order: ss -tlnp | grep :80 (is anything listening?), firewall-cmd --list-all (is the hole open?), and curl -I localhost versus curl -I http://192.0.2.10 (is it a bind address problem rather than a firewall problem?). Most “firewall” tickets are actually services bound to 127.0.0.1.

    Beyond firewalld

    RHEL also documents nftables directly for complex, performance-sensitive rulesets, and network bonding for redundancy (teaming is deprecated). You do not need either for EX200, but you should know they exist and why: firewalld is a management layer that generates nftables rules, so “firewalld or nftables” is really “managed ruleset or hand-written ruleset”.

    07

    Security: SELinux, SSH, sudo, crypto

    The level where “it works” stops being an argument.

    SELinux is the single most feared topic on the exam and the least mysterious once you accept one sentence: traditional permissions answer “which user may access this inode”, SELinux answers “which program may access this label”. Both must allow the operation. Every SELinux problem is therefore one of four things: wrong label, wrong boolean, wrong port label, or a policy that genuinely needs extending — in that order of likelihood.

    Do not disable it

    Disabling SELinux to make a service work is an automatic loss of multiple exam tasks and a real-world regression that survives long after the incident. The exam objectives explicitly include setting enforcing and permissive modes, identifying and restoring contexts, managing port labels, and using booleans — because the expected answer is always “configure SELinux correctly”, never “turn it off”.

    It converts any compromised user account into instant, silent root.     Automation: shell scripts and Ansible

    EX294 is not a different exam. It is Level 01–07, written as code that must be idempotent.

    Everything up to here was “make this one machine correct”. Automation is “make forty machines correct, twice, without changing anything the second time”. That second clause — idempotence — is the whole discipline. A script that appends a line to a file every time it runs is not automation; it is an incident with a schedule.

    Shell scripting: the exam-level subset

    /usr/local/sbin/user-audit.shbash

# Refuse to run as non-root instead of failing halfway through

while IFS=: read -r user _ uid _ _ home shell; do
  if (( uid >= THRESHOLD )) && [[ $shell != */nologin && $shell != */false ]]; then
    printf 'interactive: %-12s uid=%-5s home=%s\n' "$user" "$uid" "$home" >> "$LOG"

    The :Z is not optional

    Bind-mounting a host directory into a container gives the process access to files whose SELinux label belongs to the host. :Z applies a private, container-specific label so SELinux can tell the two contexts apart. Without it you get a permission denied inside the container that looks nothing like a permission problem. :z is the shared variant when several containers need the same volume.

    Rootless containers and the port problem

    An unprivileged user cannot bind ports below 1024. Red Hat’s default net.ipv4.ip_unprivileged_port_start is relaxed on RHEL so rootless containers can use low ports, but do not assume that on other distributions — and do not “fix” it by running everything as root.

Each has a specific fix; --privileged is not one of them.

    Troubleshooting as a method, not a mood

    Under exam pressure people start guessing. A fixed procedure prevents that, and it is also what separates a senior administrator from a fast one. The method has five steps and you should be able to say out loud which step you are on.

    1. State the expected behaviour precisely

      “HTTP should return 200 on 192.0.2.10:80 from the client VM” is testable. “The web server is broken” is not. Most wasted time is spent debugging a symptom nobody defined.

    2. Reproduce it and bound it

      Does it fail from localhost as well as remote? For all users or one? After reboot or only immediately? Each answer eliminates half the stack.

    3. Walk the layers in order

      Link → IP → route → listening socket → firewall → SELinux → application config → application log. Stop at the first layer that fails; do not skip ahead because you have a theory.

    4. Read the primary source, not the summary

      journalctl -u, /var/log/, ausearch, the application’s own error. A stack trace names the file and line; a blog post names a guess.

    5. Change one thing, then verify, then record

      Two simultaneous changes make the result uninterpretable. Write down what you changed — in the exam, that note is how you undo a mistake; in production, it is the change record.

    Bad decision #20 — tuning before measuring, and “fixing” memory with swap

    Adding swap to a host that is short on memory converts an out-of-memory kill into hours of unpredictable latency. Increasing vm.swappiness does the same thing with extra steps. Measure first: free -h, vmstat, and the application’s own metrics. A tuning profile that improves a benchmark and degrades the actual workload is not an improvement, it is a change with a story attached.

    Exam craft, and the catalog of bad decisions

    How to spend three hours, and the twenty mistakes that cost people the certificate.

    Knowledge without a plan loses points. This level is the operational layer: how to read a task, how to sequence the work, how to verify, how to recover, and how to study in the weeks before. It closes with the consolidated list of bad decisions from Levels 01–09, because patterns are easier to remember than individual rules.

    The first ten minutes

    1. Read every task before touching anything

      Ten minutes of reading saves an hour of rework. Tasks interact: one may say “do not restart the service”, another may depend on a repository you have not configured yet, and a third may be worth far more than its apparent difficulty.

    2. Write the task list on paper, with points if given

      Cross them off. A visible list prevents the classic failure of finishing nine tasks and forgetting the tenth existed.

    3. Sequence by dependency and by confidence

      Repositories and networking first (everything else needs them), then users and storage, then services and firewall, then SELinux refinements, then automation. Do the tasks you are certain about early — they bank points and calm you down.

    4. Set checkpoints at 60 and 120 minutes

      At each, stop and verify what you have already done instead of starting something new. Verification catches the --permanent-without---reload class of error while there is still time to fix it.

    5. Reserve the last fifteen minutes for reboot-and-verify

      If the exam allows it, reboot and confirm every service, mount, and scheduled job comes back. This single habit is worth more than any command you could memorise.

    How to read an exam task

    Exam tasks are written in a specific register, and every clause is there for a reason. Consider a typical one:

    Task, as written

    “Configure the system so that the directory /srv/reports is a 2 GiB XFS filesystem on a new logical volume in volume group vgdata, mounted persistently at boot, and writable by members of group reporting. The change must survive a reboot.”

    Every clause maps to a command
    ClauseWhat it forces you to do
    “2 GiB XFS on a new logical volume”lvcreate -n … -L 2G vgdata then mkfs.xfs
    “in volume group vgdata”Do not create a new VG; check vgs first
    “mounted persistently at boot”/etc/fstab by UUID, then mount -a
    “writable by members of group reporting”chgrp, chmod 2775, consider setgid
    “survive a reboot”fstab, not just mount

    If you can decompose a paragraph into commands in under a minute, you are ready. If a clause is ambiguous, pick the interpretation that is persistent and least destructive — that is almost always what the grader checks.

    Appendix: distributions that certify their administrators

    Where the credential comes from, what it costs, and which ones are marketing rather than proof.

    A Linux certification is only as meaningful as the organisation that issues it and the practical work it requires. There are three legitimate models: the vendor performance exam (Red Hat, SUSE, Oracle), the vendor qualification (Canonical), and the distribution-neutral credential (LPI, CompTIA, Linux Foundation). Knowing which model you are buying explains why two certificates at similar prices have wildly different weight in a hiring conversation.

    Vendor certifications

    Red Hat Enterprise Linux
    RHCSA · RHCE · RHCA

    The reference standard for enterprise Linux, because the exams are hands-on and the failure rate is real. EX200 is the gate; EX294 (Ansible) and EX342 (Enterprise Linux) are the two engineer paths; RHCA stacks three specialists on top. Valid three years.

    redhat.com/en/services/certifications
    SUSE Linux Enterprise
    SCA · SCE

    Multiple-choice but product-specific: SCA in SLES 16 is 70 questions in 90 minutes, 70% to pass, US$149; SCE in SLES 15 is 80 questions, 90 minutes, 70%, US$195, and requires an SCA. Cheaper and faster than Red Hat, and correspondingly narrower in signal.

    suse.com/training/exam/sca_sles16
    Ubuntu (Canonical)
    Canonical Academy SysAdmin

    Canonical’s current credential is a qualification assembled from exams in Linux Terminal, Ubuntu Desktop, Ubuntu Server, and DevOps Principles, mixing performance and scenario tasks. The older “UCP” label is no longer the current offering — treat any course advertising it as dated.

    canonical.com/academy
    Oracle Linux
    OCP · 1Z0-106

    Oracle Certified Professional, Oracle Linux 8 System Administrator. Functionally close to RHEL (it is RHEL-compatible, with its own kernel), so RHCSA study transfers almost entirely. Beware the code: 1Z0-1066 belongs to a cloud planning product, not to Linux.

    mylearn.oracle.com
    AlmaLinux · Rocky Linux
    No vendor exam

    These are RHEL-compatible rebuilds with no certification programme of their own. Their administrators certify through Red Hat (or the neutral bodies below), and the command-level skills are identical. If a course claims an “official Rocky certification”, verify it at the source.

    almalinux.org
    Fedora
    No certification

    Fedora is the community upstream where RHEL features appear first. There is no Fedora administrator exam; it is the place to watch what RHEL 11 will contain, not a credential path.

    fedoraproject.org
    Debian
    No vendor exam

    Debian has no certification body. Administrators demonstrate competence through the distribution-neutral credentials below, or through Debian-specific consulting reputation. Debian’s packaging and apt/debconf model differs enough from RHEL that RHCSA study does not transfer wholesale.

    debian.org
    Amazon Linux / AWS
    Cloud ops, not Linux

    AWS’s operations credential (CloudOps Engineer – Associate, SOA-C03, US$150) validates running workloads on AWS, not Linux administration. Useful adjacent proof; do not present it as a Linux certification.

    aws.amazon.com/certification

    Retired and renamed credentials

    LFCE was retired in May 2022. GIAC GCUX is retired. Katacoda is gone. “Linux Academy” no longer exists as a product. Ubuntu’s UCP label has been superseded by Canonical Academy qualifications. When a job posting or a course page names one of these, that is information about the posting, not about you.

    Which one should you actually take?

    If your job runs RHEL, CentOS Stream, AlmaLinux, Rocky, or Oracle Linux → RHCSA (EX200). Nothing else carries the same signal for that stack, and the study transfers to every rebuild.
    If you need a credential fast, cheaply, and your employer’s procurement recognises it → SUSE SCA (US$149, 90 minutes) or CompTIA Linux+.
    If you work across Debian and RHEL and want proof of breadth → LPIC-1, then LPIC-2. Neutral, modular, five-year validity.
    If you want a hands-on exam but cannot justify Red Hat’s price → LFCS, taken on the distribution you actually run.
    If you already hold RHCSA and your organisation automates → EX294. If it troubleshoots complex estates, look at EX342. Then three specialists for RHCA.
    Avoid any certificate whose exam can be passed by watching a video at 2× speed, and any “certification” issued by a bootcamp with no proctoring. The market has learned the difference.
