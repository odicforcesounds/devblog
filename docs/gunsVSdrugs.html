01

Legality is not a measurement of danger

It is a measurement of who is allowed to sell — and who is expected to bear the risk.

Start with a question that sounds naive and isn't. If a government's job were to minimise the harm caused by psychoactive substances, would it have produced the legal landscape we currently live in?

Two drugs kill millions of people every year and are sold with a barcode. A third — a plant that has been cultivated for at least three thousand years — is, in much of the world, a matter for police. The difference between them is not toxicology. Ethanol is a cytotoxic solvent that causes cancer, cirrhosis, hypertension, violence and fatal withdrawal. Nicotine is one of the most dependence-forming substances known. Both are lawful and taxed. Meanwhile a gram of resin can end a person's career.

That is not an argument that cannabis is harmless. It is an observation about what the law is actually measuring. And once you see it, the whole architecture becomes legible: prohibition is not a safety system. It is an authorisation system that pretends to be one.

This essay makes four claims, in order:

  1. The route and the dose determine the effect — not the reputation of the molecule. The same cannabis can be a mild evening tea, a six-hour panic attack, or a daily habit that costs someone their memory and their sleep.
  2. Control requires information — identity, purity, dose, setting and follow-up. Every one of those is something a licensed supply chain can provide and a street market structurally cannot.
  3. Compulsion, not chemistry, is the main engine of harm — and compulsion is a health problem that responds to treatment, not to a prison sentence.
  4. The moral asymmetry is real — a weapon projects harm onto a body that never consented; a drug's harm lands first on the person who chose it. A state may regulate both. It cannot honestly treat them as the same kind of thing.
A state that licenses who may sell a rifle but not who may test a gram of cannabis is not managing risk. It is managing a story about who deserves to be trusted.

“Drugs” here means psychoactive substances other than alcohol and tobacco, unless they are named. Nothing on this page is medical advice or an instruction to consume anything. 02

What cannabis actually does

Four ways to take one plant, four different drugs. The delivery route changes the onset, the duration, the metabolite, and the risk.

Cannabis is the right test case, because almost everyone has an opinion and there is now enough data to check it. The plant contains more than a hundred cannabinoids; the two that matter most are Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), which is intoxicating, and cannabidiol (CBD), which is not. In fresh plant material much of the THC exists as THCA, an acidic precursor that does not get you high until heat removes a carboxyl group — the reaction that gives decarboxylation its name.[14]

THC is fat-soluble and poorly soluble in water. That single chemical fact explains most of what follows: why a water infusion is weak, why a butter infusion is not, and why an edible can arrive ninety minutes after you have decided it isn't working.[13]

Oral THC passes through the liver before it reaches the brain, where it is converted to 11-OH-THC — a metabolite that is more potent and enters the brain more readily. The cup is chemistry, not tradition. No one has published a reliable human pharmacokinetic profile for cannabis tea. What exists is extraction data: a 2024 laboratory study found that a water-only infusion transferred roughly 0.4–1.9% of the plant's THC into the drink, while adding cream raised transfer to 53–64%.[13] That is extraction, not absorption — but it explains the folklore. Water pulls out almost nothing; fat pulls out a great deal. And because most plant material holds THC as non-intoxicating THCA, a tea made without heat is largely a tisane with a smell. The cultural belief that tea is “gentle” is really a belief that the dose is low and slow. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the cream was the dose.

In a 2025 Canadian sampling study, 48% of legal dried-cannabis samples measured below 80% of their stated THC.[20]

What it does, and what it costs

Acutely, THC raises heart rate, can drop or spike blood pressure, distorts time perception, impairs working memory and coordination, and — at high doses, in inexperienced users, or in the wrong setting — produces anxiety, panic and transient psychosis-like symptoms. It also relieves nausea, stimulates appetite, and is used clinically for chemotherapy-induced vomiting, multiple sclerosis spasticity and certain childhood epilepsies; cannabidiol is an approved medicine in much of the world, and synthetic THC has been a prescription product since 1985.

The costs are real and dose-dependent. The US CDC estimates that roughly 3 in 10 people who use cannabis have cannabis use disorder, with higher risk for people who start young and use often.[10] An earlier review using stricter criteria put lifetime dependence at about 9% of ever-users, 17% among those who start in adolescence, and 25–50% among daily users.[11] Different populations and different criteria — but the same direction: frequency and early onset are the multipliers.

Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome — cyclical vomiting and abdominal pain in long-term heavy users, sometimes relieved temporarily by hot showers — is a real and under-recognised consequence of chronic use.[21] On psychosis, the largest case-control study of first-episode psychosis found that daily use was associated with roughly three times the odds of a psychotic disorder, and daily use of high-potency cannabis (THC ≥10%) with nearly five times the odds.[12] These are associations, not proof of causation in any individual — but the dose-response pattern is consistent, and it has a policy implication that runs in the opposite direction from prohibition.

Cardiovascular signals point the same way. An observational analysis of 434,104 US adults found daily cannabis use associated with 25% higher odds of heart attack and 42% higher odds of stroke versus non-use — associations, not causation, and not route-specific.[22] And driving: a meta-analysis of nine observational studies estimated roughly double the odds of a crash after cannabis use, with substantial heterogeneity; later work puts the pooled increase closer to a third.[23]

Cannabis is not harmless. It is also not in the same risk class as the two legal drugs above it in the death tables. The argument for control is not that it is safe. It is that control is how you make an unsafe thing less dangerous.

Now look at what prohibition does with that information. It removes the label. It removes the potency cap. It removes the child-resistant lid. It removes the ability of a user to know whether the product in front of them is 4% or 70% THC, or whether it contains a pesticide that was banned in food. The black market sells concentrates because concentrates are value per gram, and nothing in a prohibition regime can stop that. A regulated market can cap potency, tax it, print it on the packet, and put the strong stuff behind a counter — the same way we handle every other dangerous but useful thing.

03

The pharmacy's hands

Almost every substance in the illegal market was, at some point, a product with a label, a dose, and a licensed manufacturer. The molecule never changed. The information did.

The premise that drugs are developed by people who know how to develop them is not a conspiracy theory. It is the history of the pharmaceutical industry, written in patents.

Heroin was a Bayer trademark. Introduced in 1898 as a cough suppressant and sold for respiratory illness, it was a pharmaceutical before it was a Schedule I narcotic. Diamorphine is still prescribed in the United Kingdom for severe pain and in Switzerland as supervised heroin-assisted treatment for people who have failed every other option.[24] Cocaine was the first effective local anaesthetic and is still used in ear, nose and throat surgery. Amphetamine was sold over the counter as an inhaler in the 1930s and is now a controlled prescription for ADHD. Ketamine is a battlefield anaesthetic that became an FDA-approved antidepressant in 2019. MDMA was used in psychotherapy before it was emergency-scheduled in 1985. Psilocybin is in phase 3 trials. Cannabis itself is already a prescription medicine in countries that simultaneously arrest people for possessing the plant.

  • 1898Bayer registers diacetylmorphine under the name Heroin, as a cough remedy.
  • 1933Amphetamine is marketed as Benzedrine. Within a decade it is in military kit bags on both sides of the Second World War.
  • 1985MDMA is placed in Schedule I in the United States. Therapeutic research stops for a generation.
  • 1994Switzerland begins prescribing pharmaceutical heroin to people with severe dependence.
  • 2018The FDA approves a purified cannabidiol medicine for severe childhood epilepsies.
  • 2024The FDA declines to approve MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, asking for more evidence on durability, safety and bias.[25]

Notice the shape of that timeline. Every step forward in knowledge required a licence. Every step backward required a law. The pharmacy did not make these molecules safe. It made them legible — and legibility is the precondition of safety. A prescription is not a promise of benefit; it is a bundle of information: what the substance is, how much of it, how pure, what it interacts with, who is responsible if it goes wrong, and who checks on you afterwards.

Prohibition deletes all five and keeps the molecule. Then it points at the resulting body count and calls it evidence that the molecule was always the problem.

The comparison is structural, not moral. A licensed chain can be held liable for a mislabelled product; a street dealer cannot be identified, sued, or recalled. Prohibition is the only regulatory regime in which a consumer product has no serial number, no dose, no purity test and no liable seller.

04

The military's hands

States are the largest drug distributors in history, and they have never needed a prescription.

Between April and July 1940, German forces were issued more than 35 million tablets of Pervitin — methamphetamine — to sustain wakefulness during the invasion of France.[26] Germany was not exceptional. Allied forces used amphetamines too; the British distributed Benzedrine to troops, and the American military studied and adopted stimulants for aircrew.[27] The distribution figure is a supply statistic, not proof that every soldier took the drug or that it explains any particular battle — but the scale is not in dispute.

The practice did not end in 1945. The US Air Force has issued dextroamphetamine — “go pills” — to aircrew as a fatigue countermeasure. On 17 April 2002, an F-16 attack at Tarnak Farms in Afghanistan killed four Canadian soldiers and wounded eight. The coalition investigation attributed the incident to the pilots' failure to exercise proper flight discipline, along with command failures; both pilots had reportedly taken amphetamines shortly beforehand, which does not establish that the medication caused the attack.[28] That distinction matters: it is the same distinction this essay asks for everywhere else — correlation is not causation, and a drug is not a scapegoat.

What the military example establishes is narrower, and harder to argue with. The state has always been willing to issue powerful psychoactive substances to nineteen-year-olds when the operational benefit was clear. It did not wait for a decade of phase 3 trials. It did not require that the substance be harmless. It required a dose, a protocol, a medical officer and a reason.

The same institution will hand a nineteen-year-old a stimulant and a rifle — and will not trust an adult with a tested gram of a plant.

And the institution is now moving toward the drugs this essay's reader is most likely to be curious about. In January 2024 the US Department of Veterans Affairs announced it would fund research into MDMA- and psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans, and the FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act opened the door to Department of Defense research on psychedelics in military populations.[29] The interest is medical, not recreational. It is also an admission that the conventional toolkit is not enough.

This is where the strongest version of the DMT claim belongs — and where it has to be handled honestly. The evidence that psychedelics can help trauma is promising and early, not settled:

  • MDMA for PTSD. The confirmatory phase 3 trial reported a between-group effect of Cohen's d ≈ 0.7 on PTSD symptom severity.[31] In August 2024 the FDA nonetheless declined to approve the application, asking for more work on durability, safety and bias.[25] A resubmission has been reported since; no approval has been granted. Contested
  • Psilocybin for depression. A Johns Hopkins randomised trial in 24 completers reported very large effects against a waiting list,[32] and phase 3 programmes have reported modest but statistically significant symptom reductions. Company-reported topline results are not the same as a published, independently reviewed paper. Mixed
  • DMT and ayahuasca for trauma. The clearest PTSD-specific human evidence is a case series of eight military veterans attending an ayahuasca retreat: five of the seven with follow-up data showed reliable symptom improvement, maintained at three months. Uncontrolled, tiny, self-selected, and confounded by setting and expectation.[30] Early

So the honest statement is not “DMT fixes soldiers.” It is: the hypothesis is serious, funded, and under formal investigation — and that investigation is only possible inside a regulatory system that can supply a known dose to a monitored patient. Prohibition is not the friend of that research. It is the reason the research is thirty years late.

05

Compulsion, not chemistry, is the engine

Most people who try a drug do not become dependent on it. The ones who do are concentrated where dose, frequency, potency and untreated distress converge.

This is the part of the argument that cuts against both sides. It is not true that a molecule is harmless because it is natural, and it is not true that a molecule is catastrophic because it is illegal. What the epidemiology shows is that the transition from use to dependence is the exception, not the rule — and it is predictable.

Criteria and populations differ from the CDC's current 3-in-10 cannabis estimate, which uses broader definitions; the two should not be averaged. What replicates across both: dependence is a minority outcome, and frequency and early onset raise it sharply.[33][10]

The chart is not a licence to experiment. It is an argument about where policy should aim. If most use does not become dependence, then a policy that treats every user as a future addict is not only cruel — it is inaccurate, and inaccurate policy wastes the credibility it needs for the cases that are real. And if the minority who do become dependent are concentrated among early-onset, high-frequency, high-potency users with untreated mental health problems, then the highest-yield interventions are the boring ones: delay onset, cap potency, treat depression and trauma, provide housing, and keep people alive long enough to recover.

The lethal mechanism in the current overdose crisis is not a molecule's inherent deadliness. It is unpredictability. Illicitly manufactured fentanyl is roughly 50 times more potent than heroin and 100 times more potent than morphine; a dose measured in milligrams can be fatal, and it is pressed into counterfeit pills that look like ordinary pharmaceuticals.[34] A person taking a pill has no way to know whether it contains 5 mg of oxycodone or 2 mg of fentanyl. That is not a pharmacology problem. It is an information problem, manufactured by the policy that makes information illegal.

The user's instinct — that compulsive consumption is the thing that destroys, not consumption as such — is supported by the data. The corollary is less comfortable for both sides: compulsion is a health condition, and health conditions are treated. Opioid agonist therapy roughly halves mortality for people with opioid dependence; naloxone reverses overdose; supervised consumption keeps people alive through the period in which they are not yet ready to stop. None of those interventions require the user to deserve them first.

06

The accounting

Yes, more Americans die of drugs than of guns. That is the indictment, not the rebuttal.

Here is where a lazy version of this argument dies. In 2024, 79,384 people in the United States died of drug overdose — a 26% fall in the age-adjusted death rate compared with the previous year, and still nearly twice the number killed by firearms.[2] If the case were “drugs are safer than guns”, the numbers would end it.

But that is not the case being made. Look at how each number is produced.

US age-adjusted overdose death rate fell 26.2% between 2023 and 2024, from 31.3 to 23.1 per 100,000.[2] Firearm totals include unintentional, legal-intervention and undetermined deaths, so suicide and homicide do not sum to the total.[1]

Two things in that chart deserve to be said out loud.

First: the drug deaths are an information failure. They are overwhelmingly poisonings — people taking a substance whose dose and identity they could not know. A pharmacy that sold a pill with no dose, no purity check and no liable manufacturer would be shut down in a week and its executives charged. The illegal market does exactly that, at industrial scale, and we call it policy. When fentanyl entered the supply, the death rate did not rise because Americans suddenly became more reckless. It rose because the product changed and nobody was allowed to tell them.

Second: the gun deaths are an engineering success. A majority of them — 27,593 of 44,447 — are suicides. A firearm is the most lethal common method of self-harm, which is precisely why means restriction works and why “the person would have found another way” is empirically weak: method matters enormously to whether an attempt becomes a death. A product designed to be instantly lethal is doing exactly what it was designed to do. The difference is that the gun's lethality is engineered on purpose, while the drug's lethality is manufactured by ignorance. We regulate the first with serial numbers and licensing, and the second with handcuffs.

And the money tells you what the state actually believes is worth managing. Global military expenditure reached $2.887 trillion in 2025, up 2.9% in real terms; the United States alone spent $954 billion. In 2021–25 the United States supplied 42% of major arms transfers.[6][7] There are roughly one billion firearms in the world, 85% of them civilian; US civilian holdings alone are estimated at 393 million in internationally comparable data, and industry figures put the accumulated total above 500 million.[8][9] The money and the supply chains exist. What is missing is a decision to spend a fraction of them on treatment, testing and supervision instead of on enforcement.

Governments do ban some weapons: chemical weapons, cluster munitions for states party to the convention, landmines for the 160-odd signatories of Ottawa. The point is not that the world is indifferent to harm from weapons. The point is that the threshold for regulating an object is not danger. It is whether the object is already inside the system of legitimate violence. Guns and munitions are. Drugs are not. Danger has almost nothing to do with it.

07

The consent asymmetry

A rifle's harm lands on a body that did not agree. A drug's harm lands, first, on the body that did.

Strip away the chemistry and the culture, and the two objects differ in one way that matters more than any potency table: where the harm goes.

A gun is a device for projecting force across distance into a body that has not consented to receive it. That is what it is for. Its primary function is to make a stranger's body stop, from over there, without their agreement. A bomb does the same thing at scale. A drug is a device for changing the state of the person who takes it. Its primary function is self-directed. It can be misused to harm others — driving, violence, neglect of children, poisoning someone — and when it is, the law rightly intervenes. But the harm does not originate in the design of the object. It originates in the user's choice, and the object is a means.

This is the distinction John Stuart Mill drew in 1859, and it has never been improved on: the only justified exercise of power over a member of a civilised community, against their will, is to prevent harm to others. Their own good — physical or moral — is not sufficient warrant.[35] You do not have to be a libertarian to see the force of it. You only have to notice that the state already accepts it everywhere else: it does not imprison people for eating badly, skiing badly, or refusing to exercise, even though those choices kill far more people than cannabis ever will.

And the uncomfortable version, stated plainly, because it is the part that matters: if a person, in full knowledge, damages only themselves, the case for sending armed men to stop them is weaker than the case for sending a doctor. That is not because self-destruction is noble. It is not. It is because the justification for force is the protection of a second person, and when there is no second person, the justification must be built out of something else — paternalism, disgust, or the convenience of a slogan. Those are not nothing. They are also not a basis for a prison sentence.

If this is not philosophy for you

If you are the one holding the pipe, the needle or the bottle, and it has stopped being a choice, this is not a policy question. It is a medical one, and it is treatable. In the US, call or text 988. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential services by country. The argument on this page can wait. You do not have to.

The consent asymmetry does not make drugs safe, and it does not make prohibition irrational in every particular. It sets the burden of proof. If the state wants to criminalise a self-regarding act, it must show that the act reliably harms third parties in ways that a narrower rule could not address — and then it must write the narrower rule. That is what a serious drug policy would look like: not a war on a molecule, but a careful accounting of who is actually being hurt, and by what.

08

What control actually looks like

“Controlled places” is not a vibe. It is a list of specific, testable interventions — with very different levels of evidence behind them.

The phrase “drugs in controlled places are better” is doing a lot of work. It should be cashed out. Below is what a regulated regime actually consists of, and how strong the evidence is for each part. The honest answer is that some of it is well established, some is promising, and some is still an educated guess. A serious advocate says which is which.

Grades reflect the strength of the human evidence, not the political difficulty of the measure. A “mixed” grade is not a refutation; it is an instruction to monitor outcomes and adjust.

Two lessons follow. The first is that the interventions with the strongest evidence are the least ideological: naloxone, methadone, and a packet that tells you what is in it. The second is that decriminalisation alone is not a policy. Portugal's results came bundled with a large treatment and harm-reduction build-out; Oregon's Measure 110 was implemented late, underfunded, and then reversed. Removing a punishment is not the same as building a system. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

The strongest comparison is not between drugs and guns in the abstract. It is between two regulatory philosophies applied to the same problem: a licensed supply with a dose on the label, and a violent market with no label at all. Canada is the closest thing to a natural experiment. By 2024, 72% of past-year cannabis consumers reported usually buying from a legal source, against 4% in 2018; legal non-medical sales reached CAD $5.2 billion in 2023/24.[41][42] That is what taking market share from a criminal economy looks like. It took six years, a lot of money, and a great deal of boring regulation. Prohibition has had a century and has never once done it.

09

The strongest objections, answered

A case that cannot survive its best counter-argument is not a case. These are the five that actually matter.

“Legalisation normalises use, especially among the young.”

The evidence does not support a simple version of this. US national Monitoring the Future data for 2023 found past-year cannabis use at 8.3% of 8th graders, 17.8% of 10th graders and 29.0% of 12th graders — stable across all three grades and below pre-pandemic levels.[43] In the national Youth Risk Behavior Survey, past-30-day use among high-schoolers fell from 23.1% in 2011 to 15.8% in 2021, while state legalisation expanded.[44] Canada's survey data are more mixed: past-30-day use among 16–19-year-olds was 27% in 2024 versus 23% in 2018, statistically unchanged from 2023, with daily use flat at 8%.

But normalisation is real, and commercialisation is the danger. Legal markets do increase adult use and heavy use, and the tobacco playbook — advertising, flavouring, price competition — is a proven way to manufacture dependence. The lesson is not that prohibition protects the young. It is that legalisation without advertising bans, potency caps, plain packaging and price policy is just a new way to sell an old harm. The design is the policy.

“Cannabis causes psychosis. That is enough to ban it.”

The association is real and dose-dependent. In the EU-GEI study, daily use was associated with an adjusted odds ratio of 3.2 for psychotic disorder; daily high-potency use with 4.8. That is a serious signal.[12]

But prohibition maximises the exposure it claims to prevent. The illegal market selects for potency because potency is value per gram: concentrates at 68.7% THC, no ceiling, no label, no warning. A regulated market can cap THC, tax high-potency products, require warnings about psychosis risk, and keep the strongest products out of the hands of adolescents. Banning the plant while the black market sells 70% concentrates is not protecting anyone's dopamine system. It is protecting a business model.

“Look at Oregon. Decriminalisation killed people.”

The causal claim is genuinely contested. An early analysis estimated 182 additional overdose deaths in 2021 attributable to Measure 110. A later peer-reviewed reanalysis found no statistically significant association after accounting for the arrival of fentanyl in Oregon's supply — which happened in the same months the policy took effect (−0.51 deaths per 100,000 per half-year; p = 0.41).[40] A three-year Portland State review likewise found little evidence that the measure caused rising overdose deaths.

What is not contested is that implementation failed. The state's own audit found that by the time services went statewide, providers had spent only about a third of grant funds and the state lacked outcome data. Oregon recriminalised possession in 2024. The honest conclusion is not “decriminalisation works” or “decriminalisation fails”. It is that you cannot test a health policy by funding it late and measuring nothing.

“Edibles poison children. That is why it must stay illegal.”

This one is true, and it is the strongest concrete harm in the legalisation literature. CDC surveillance counted 539,106 cannabis-involved emergency department visits among people under 25 between late 2018 and early 2023, with paediatric visits rising sharply; US poison centres recorded a 1,375% increase in edible ingestions among children under six from 2017 to 2021.[45] Canadian data show the same pattern after commercial edibles arrived.

Notice what the fix is. Canada's 10 mg per-container cap, child-resistant packaging, plain packaging, and storage rules are all regulations, not prohibitions. The comparison is not “legal edibles versus no edibles”. It is “edibles with a dose cap and a child-proof lid” versus “edibles sold by a dealer in a bag with a cartoon on it”. The rise in paediatric poisonings is an argument for stronger product regulation — and, specifically, an argument against the laissez-faire version of legalisation that treats a packet of gummies like a bag of sweets.

“Drugs fund cartels and violence. Legalisation would hand them the market.”

Prohibition is the cartel's business model. A prohibited market has no lawful supplier, so violence becomes the enforcement mechanism for contracts, and potency becomes the competitive margin. The mark-up exists because the product is illegal, not because the plant is expensive to grow. Every enforcement seizure is a supply shock that raises the price for everyone still selling.

The only thing that has ever taken market share from a criminal drug economy is a legal one. Canada moved from 4% of consumers usually buying legally in 2018 to 72% in 2024.[42] That is not an argument that legalisation ends organised crime — it does not, and illegal supply persists alongside legal markets. It is an argument that a policy which leaves the entire market in criminal hands has no mechanism for reducing criminal revenue at all.

10

Where this case is weakest

An argument that hides its weak points is asking to be believed rather than tested. Here are the four places where this one bends.

1. Pharmacology does not settle politics. Knowing that dose and purity drive harm tells you what a good system would look like. It does not tell you that your government can build one. A state that cannot run a pharmacy, inspect a kitchen or collect a fine should not be handed a drug market and told to be careful. Regulatory capture is real: the alcohol and tobacco industries are proof that a legal market will spend decades optimising for consumption rather than health. Legalisation is a tool, not a virtue.

2. Third-party harms are real and easy to undercount. Children in a house with edible gummies. A driver who has taken a high-potency concentrate. A foetus exposed to a substance with no established safe threshold. Second-hand smoke. Violence in the illegal market that spills into neighbourhoods. The consent asymmetry sets the burden of proof; it does not make those harms disappear. Any honest version of this argument has to build the exceptions in — which is why the proposals above are about licensing, labelling, potency caps and supervision, not about removing the law.

3. The gun comparison is rhetorically strong and analytically imperfect. A firearm is designed to injure; a drug is not. Comparing raw death tolls across two categories with different exposure bases, different counting methods and different legal histories is not epidemiology. The defensible claim is narrower: both are dangerous consumer products, and the regime that manages the danger better is the one with licences, labels, testing and liability — not the one that pretends the danger is a moral failing. That claim survives the numbers. The sweeping version does not.

4. The decriminalisation evidence is genuinely mixed. Portugal's favourable trends are confounded by massive treatment investment; its overdose deaths rose 16% in 2023. Oregon's results are disputed and were reversed. Supervised consumption prevents on-site deaths but has not been shown to reduce population mortality in large-scale controlled analyses; heroin-assisted treatment improves retention but has not shown a statistically significant mortality benefit. Anyone who tells you the evidence is settled is not reading it.

None of that collapses the case. It sharpens it. The strongest available position is not “drugs are fine”. It is: the harms that kill people are mostly the harms of uncontrolled dose and untreated compulsion, and those are precisely the harms that a regulated, health-led system is built to reduce. Prohibition does not reduce them. It relocates them, out of sight, into a market with no labels.

11

The ask

Five things a government could do this decade that would kill fewer people than the current arrangement.

This is not a demand for a drug supermarket. It is a demand that the law stop pretending that absence of regulation is a form of regulation. In order of evidence, not ideology:

  1. 01 Treat compulsion as a medical condition.Naloxone without a prescription, methadone and buprenorphine without barriers, and treatment on demand. These are the interventions with the strongest evidence in the entire field, and they require no philosophical agreement about drugs at all.
  2. 02 Test the supply, legally.Drug-checking services, purity alerts, and legal tolerance for people who report adulteration. A consumer who cannot find out what is in a product is not being protected by the state; they are being kept ignorant by it.
  3. 03 Regulate adult supply like a pharmacy, not a sweet shop.Dose on the label, potency caps, child-resistant and plain packaging, no advertising, licensed sellers with liability, and revenue earmarked for treatment. Canada's experience shows this can take three-quarters of the market from criminals — slowly, expensively, and only if it is done properly.
  4. 04 Stop criminalising possession.Not because drugs are safe, but because a criminal record is a machine for producing unemployment, homelessness and relapse. Decriminalisation without a treatment system is a gesture; with one, it is a policy.
  5. 05 Apply the same logic to the other dangerous product.Licensing, serial numbers, safe-storage requirements, waiting periods, liability, and research into why most firearm deaths are suicides. A state that can register a car and license a driver can register a rifle. The fact that it does not is the clearest evidence that “danger” was never the criterion.
The question is not whether a society should manage dangerous things. It always does. The question is whether it manages them on purpose.

Guns and bombs are legal because they are useful to states, and because the people who make them are inside the system of legitimate violence. Drugs are illegal because the people who use them are not. Neither fact is a judgement about which object is more dangerous. Both are facts about power — and the first step toward a rational policy is to stop mistaking the distribution of power for the distribution of risk.

A drug in a controlled place is a dose. A drug on a street corner is a gamble. A gun in a safe is a risk someone chose to take. A gun in a drawer is a decision that has already been made for whoever finds it. The law currently regulates the second of each pair and calls the first one freedom.

What this page is, and is not

It is an argument, assembled from official statistics and peer-reviewed research, that the legality of a substance is a poor guide to its danger and a worse guide to how to reduce it.

It is not medical advice, a dosing guide, or an invitation to use anything. If you use drugs, the evidence in this page points in one direction: know the dose, know the purity, do not use alone, and do not confuse a policy argument with a personal permission slip.

A note on counting

Death statistics are not interchangeable. Firearm totals include unintentional and undetermined deaths, so suicide plus homicide does not equal the total. Overdose deaths can involve multiple substances and overlap by drug. Alcohol and tobacco figures are attributable-burden estimates, not counts of death certificates. Drug-disorder mortality from UNODC is a rounded estimate. Comparing them, as this page does, is a way of showing scale — not a substitute for a controlled epidemiological model.
