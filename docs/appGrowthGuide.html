Two Apps, One Phone: A Mobile Growth Guide

Civic Perks and Social Tool Kit both turn small acts of care into records that other people confirm. Neither spreads by itself: an empty map and an empty group look broken. This is the step-by-step plan to fill one neighbourhood first, run from the phone in your hand.

If you only do three things today

  1. 1Claim the names, the handles and the two QR codes before you post anything.
  2. 2Pick one neighbourhood and one partner group. Write the name down; it is the whole strategy.
  3. 3Shoot one fifteen-second before-and-after clip and publish it in three places.

01

What each app asks of a stranger

A campaign cannot promise what the product does not do. Before choosing a channel, write the one sentence a stranger must understand in ten seconds, and check that the first screen proves it without an account. Both products are social in the narrow sense that matters here: the value arrives when a second and a third person take part.

The same promise, two different rituals
QuestionCivic PerksSocial Tool Kit
EntryOpen the map, no account needed to lookGoogle account, then a group
Unit of actionA pin on a street: report, fix, confirmA filmed challenge carried by a group
Who decidesAnother scout, then a third oneSeven outside reviewers
RewardXP and Impact CreditsPoints that count views, not verified good
Empty-state fixCold-start rules keep a lone scout usefulOne seed group can publish the first proof
Failure modeA map with three pins in one streetA group nobody joins, so no video is rated

The Civic Perks ritual

The map is the product. A walker finds something that needs fixing or protecting, drops a pin with a photograph, and later confirms what a neighbour found. Until the map has a crowd, the application protects the first scout with cold-start rules: new pins are accepted but flagged for a back-check, and the number of pins in a day is capped. Validation and solving wait for a third scout to exist, so a new area does not punish the person who arrived first.

The Social Tool Kit ritual

Here the group is the product. A workspace forms around a task that one person cannot finish alone, someone films the proof, and outsiders rate it. Seven approvals publish the challenge, seven denials remove it, and seven reports send it to a moderator. The application is deliberately without a feed, followers or money, and its points measure how many people watched a proof rather than how much good it did.

That difference decides the campaign. Civic Perks can be tried by a stranger in one tap, so the job is to make the map look alive. Social Tool Kit asks for a group and a camera, so the job is to recruit one committed group at a time. The log of the build, the earlier call for groups and the challenge format all describe this second, heavier ritual. The sound behind the name is a track, and the formal rules sit in the wiki.

Write the pitch once. Civic Perks: “Walk your street, pin what needs fixing, and let a neighbour confirm it.” Social Tool Kit: “Do the small thing with your group, film it, and let seven strangers decide if it holds.” Everything else on this page is distribution for those two sentences.
02

Where this works, and where it cannot

Country-level launches are the most common way this kind of app dies. A civic map needs density in a few square kilometres, a phone culture that already shares photographs of the street, and permission to gather and film without fear. Those conditions are local, not national. The right question is not “which country has the most people” but “which six neighbourhoods can reach three scouts each within a month”.

Four tests before a market

  1. Cheap, common mobile data. The first screen must load on a mid-range Android on a prepaid plan.
  2. Walkable density. Enough people on foot within one square kilometre to confirm a pin the same week.
  3. Civic habit. Existing groups that already clean, plant, patrol or care: they supply the first reviewers.
  4. Platform access. The channels you can actually use, with accounts that will not be blocked or require a workaround.

Brazil passes the first two comfortably. The country had 185 million internet users and 150 million active social media identities at the end of 2025, according to DataReportal, and messaging is the default way people organise. The obstacles are not connectivity but attention and trust: civic space is rated obstructed, and internet freedom is only partly free in the 2025 assessment. A campaign that looks like unpaid municipal surveillance will be rejected in a week.

Mainland China is the opposite case, and it is worth naming plainly because it is often proposed as a growth market. Internet freedom scores 9 out of 100 and civic space is rated closed, according to Freedom House and the CIVICUS Monitor. Google services, including Firebase authentication, are unreliable or blocked, and Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp are unavailable; measurements of the authentication host show interference from inside the country on GreatFire. Social Tool Kit signs people in with Google and the current Civic Perks campaign assumes Meta channels. Neither can run there without rebuilding authentication on a local provider, filing domestically and living inside WeChat or Douyin. That is a product decision, not a marketing tactic.

Nextdoor, the one social network built around the block, is available in eleven countries only, and Brazil is not one of them; the current list covers Australia, Canada, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom and the United States. In Brazil the neighbourhood layer is WhatsApp groups, Instagram and Facebook groups, which is why the plan treats those as infrastructure rather than as optional extras.

Rule of the pilot: one city, one district, three partner groups, thirty days. If the map is still empty after that, move the district, not the message. A second country before the first district works is just a more expensive way to learn nothing.
03

The best people to recruit first

“Best” here does not mean the largest audience. It means the person most likely to produce one confirmed action inside seven days, and then bring one more person. Both products fail at the second participant, not at the first download, so the target is a person with a route, a group or a camera already in their life.

Test every candidate with one question: can this person cause a pin to be confirmed, or a proof to be rated, this week? If the answer is no, they are an audience member, not a seed. That distinction saves a month of posting into rooms that will never act.

One list, two fits
PersonCivic PerksSocial Tool KitBecause
Organiser of a recurring groupHighHighBrings five to ten people and instant legitimacy
Corner shopkeeperHighLowFixed point, all-day presence, QR on the counter
Daily-route walkerHighMediumSees the same street every day, no planning needed
Group that already films its workMediumHighThe camera habit is the hardest part, already solved
Open-mapping volunteerHighLowPrecise pins and patient validation
Young content makerMediumHighMakes the clip that recruits everyone else
Small NGO with a funder reportMediumHighNeeds a public proof, has seven outside reviewers

For Civic Perks, the person with a route

The dog walker at seven in the morning, the postal worker, the delivery rider, the parent on the school run: they pass the same twenty corners five days a week and can tell you which one floods. Ask for five pins on their usual route in one week and offer to walk it with them once. Repeat visits matter more than enthusiasm, because confirmation needs a second sighting.

The corner shopkeeper is the second recruit, for a different reason: a fixed point with a counter, a window and the trust of the street. Ask to leave one A5 sheet with the QR code, and offer to print it yourself. A shop that displays the code is also a place where a stranger can ask a question, which converts far better than a link in a feed.

Then the block organiser: the person who already runs the residents' chat, the condominium meeting or the parish social action. They bring the first five neighbours in one message. After that come the neighbours with memory, who know which wall was repaired twice and which tree was planted by a school class; ask them to show you three corners that changed, and pin the stories.

For Social Tool Kit, the person with a group

The unit is a leader of a recurring task: a cleanup crew, a food bank, an animal shelter, a scout troop, a sports club. They already have a task that one person cannot finish, a list of members and a reason to prove the work happened. Ask for one challenge, filmed, rated by seven people outside the group.

The second recruit is a group that already films its volunteers. The camera habit is the hardest part of this product, and a group that posts clips after every action has already solved it. A small NGO that reports to funders is the third: a public proof page is useful to them beyond the app, and they can supply the seven outside reviewers from their board and partners.

Workplace volunteer teams and supervised school clubs fit the same shape, with one caution: an organised one-day event produces a good proof and a weak habit. Treat it as a campaign asset, not as a group that will return next month.

Start with trust, not with tech

The tempting first recruit is the civic-tech volunteer: precise, fast and generous with feedback. They are excellent validators and terrible first seeds, because they will audit the concept before the map has three pins. Research on civic-tech volunteers finds that the main motivation is an issue they or their community already face, and that they want to see something ship and have impact, as a Canadian study of the field notes. Give them a finished map to improve, not an empty one to judge.

Open-mapping contributors are the same asset in a different form. They care about open geographic knowledge and the commons, and they are motivated by the greater good as much as by the tool, according to research on the community. Ask them to validate ten pins in an area they know, and name them in the weekly ledger. They will find the errors you cannot see.

The twenty-name list. Before posting anything, write twenty names in a note: three organisers, four shopkeepers, eight daily walkers, three reviewers, two content makers. If you cannot fill the list, the district is wrong, not the copy.

Who to skip, for now

  • Tourists and visitors: they cannot confirm a pin next week.
  • Remote supporters who praise the idea but live in another city.
  • App collectors who install everything and return to nothing.
  • National organisations that want a pilot before a district exists.
  • Partisan groups: the map would be read as a campaign, not a commons.
  • Anyone whose home or workplace must stay unlocated.
  • Minors without a guardian's written agreement.

There is also an equity risk in copying the audience of the platforms that work best. Nextdoor's American neighbourhoods are less dense, older, wealthier and more educated than the country as a whole, according to a study of the platform, and Pew's survey found its users concentrated among college graduates and higher-income households. If the campaign only recruits there, the map will describe one kind of street and miss the corners that need it most. Deliberately add a dense periphery, a market street and a bus terminus to the list, and onboard with WhatsApp and printed codes rather than email forms.

Three messages that get a yes

To the organiserI am mapping our street with a small app. Can I join your next walk and show five neighbours how to pin one thing? It takes two minutes and nobody has to sign up to look.

To the shopkeeperCan I leave one printed sheet with a QR code by the till? It opens a map of this street, where neighbours add what needs fixing. I will bring the sheet and take it away if nobody uses it.

To the group leaderYour group already does the work. Can we film one task and ask seven people outside the group to rate the proof? You get a public page that shows what you did, and I do the editing.

04

The phone test before promotion

Traffic is borrowed; the first screen is owned. Every person a campaign brings is one thumb away from leaving. The rules below are the difference between a mobile web app and a desktop page squeezed into a phone.

Non-negotiables, with the standard behind each
ItemTargetWhy
Body text18px, line height 1.6 or moreReadable without pinch-zoom in sunlight
Tap targets48px minimum, 8px apartApple asks 44pt, Material asks 48dp
LayoutOne column at 320px wideNo horizontal scrolling, ever
NavigationBottom bar, five items at mostThumb reach, not top-corner menus
WeightUnder 1MB for the first viewPrepaid data, old phones, weak signal
InstallManifest, icons 192 and 512, HTTPSNeeded for the add-to-home prompt
SharingSystem share button on every resultA confirmed pin is a shareable object
ThemeDark, high contrastNight walks and battery life

The 48px figure is the strictest of the three guidelines and the easiest to remember: Apple asks for 44 by 44 points and text of at least 11 points, Material for 48 by 48dp, and WCAG 2.2 sets an AA floor of 24 by 24 CSS pixels with a 44 by 44 AAA target, as the W3C explains. Designing for the largest number satisfies all three.

Installability has its own bar. Chrome looks for HTTPS, a manifest with a name, a start URL, a display mode and icons at 192 and 512 pixels, plus a little engagement, and then fires the install prompt; the exact criteria are short. On iPhone the prompt never appears: Chrome and Edge on iOS cannot install a web app at all, so the only route is Safari's Share menu and “Add to Home Screen”, as web.dev notes. Put that instruction in the page, with a picture, instead of waiting for a prompt that will not come.

The ten-minute test

  • Open the site on a 360px Android phone, not the desktop emulator.
  • Hold it in one hand and reach every control with the thumb.
  • Turn brightness to half, step outside, and read the smallest text.
  • Disable images and reload: the page must still make sense.
  • Tap the primary action with a thumb, not a fingertip.
  • Open the share sheet from a pin or a proof and check the preview card.
  • Go offline after the first load; the shell should say what happened.
  • Add to home screen on an iPhone and on Android, then open both icons.

The assets you make once

Promotion fails when every post starts with a decision. Build a small kit in one afternoon and reuse it for a month. Everything here can be produced on the phone, with the browser and the camera roll.

The kit, and where each piece is used
AssetSpecUsed on
Profile pictureSquare, 1024px, high contrastEvery account, same image
Cover imageOne street photograph, no textPage and profile headers
One-sentence pitchUnder 150 charactersBios, groups, directories
Demo clip15 seconds, vertical, captions burned inReels, TikTok, Stories
Three screenshotsMap, pin, confirmationStore-style pages, launch posts
QR codeOne per app, tested on paperPosters, flyers, partner desks
Link with UTMFive fields, lower case, no spacesEvery post and print piece

Keep one naming scheme for the campaign links, because without it the numbers at the end are folklore. A workable pattern is the app, the channel, the city and the date: ?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=reel&utm_campaign=saopaulo-nov&utm_content=pin01. The same string goes on the printed QR code, so a scan and a tap land in the same count.

The two destinations are fixed for the whole campaign: Civic Perks and Social Tool Kit. Never send a campaign to a page that asks for an account before showing the product. Pair the QR code with three words and a verb, not a paragraph: “Scan. Walk. Pin.”

Make the demo clip first. It forces the pitch to be concrete, and it becomes the pinned post, the Reel, the TikTok, the Story highlight and the thumbnail on every other channel.
06

Facebook: Page plus Groups

A Page is a noticeboard; a local Group is a room. The Page gives you a stable name, a contact button and a place to pin the instructions. The Group is where neighbours already talk, and where a post can travel without money. Use both, but do not confuse their rules.

Create the Page from the phone

  1. Sign in, open the menu, choose Pages, then Create.
  2. Pick the professional route and create a new Page.
  3. Name it exactly as the domain, and choose the closest category for a civic app or community.
  4. Add a bio under 255 characters, the website, and the city you are starting in.
  5. Upload the profile picture and cover, then set the action button to “Learn more” with the campaign link.
  6. Invite the ten people who will actually test it, not the whole contact list.

Meta publishes these steps for both computer and phone. Add two people you trust as Page managers before the first post, so the account is not a single point of failure.

The first week of the Page

  • Day one: pinned post with the 15-second clip, the three steps and the QR image.
  • Day two: the “before” photograph of the street you will walk, with the time and meeting point.
  • Day four: the “after” result and the number of pins confirmed, even if the number is three.
  • Day six: one neighbour's sentence about why the corner matters, with permission.
  • Day seven: a plain invitation to the next walk, one date, one place, no form.

Join three local Groups as your personal profile, read their pinned rules, and ask the admin before posting a link. A first contribution that answers a question already in the Group buys more attention than ten announcements. Create a Facebook Event for each walk: it gives people a date, a map and a reminder without another account. Answer messages from the Page inbox within a day; the first ten conversations teach more about objections than any survey.

Do not run the Page as a bot. Identical posts across five groups in one hour, tag-a-friend bait and “share to win” mechanics are what the platforms penalise and what neighbours recognise. One group, one relevant post, one reply to every comment.
07

One walk, four posts

Shoot once, cut four times. A single twenty-minute walk produces enough material for a week if the shots are planned: the problem, the hand doing the work, the map after the pin, and the neighbour's reaction. Vertical video, burned-in captions, no music you do not own.

Channel roles in one campaign
ChannelPostLengthCall
Instagram ReelsBefore and after of one corner10–20 seconds“Scan the code at the corner shop”
Instagram StoriesPoll: which corner first?One frameSwipe to the map
TikTokThe hand, the bin, the map pin8–15 seconds“Comment your street”
WhatsApp StatusSame clip, no link15 secondsReply to join the walk
NextdoorA local question about a cornerShort text plus photoAsk neighbours to confirm

Nextdoor is the closest thing to a neighbourhood feed, but it is not everywhere and it is strict about who may post. Business Pages and Business Posts are free, and posts go to a radius you choose; only local businesses may post into the newsfeed, and the platform's business pages explain the flow. The tone that works there is a neighbour asking a question, not a company announcing a product.

WhatsApp needs consent, not cleverness. A broadcast list or a community group only works if people opted in; add them by asking at the walk, with a QR code that opens the group, and say how to leave. Never scrape numbers from a flyer. A Status update is the cheapest reach you have and the least intrusive, because it stays inside people who already saved your number.

Five formats worth repeating

  • The thirty-second fix: one problem, one action, one confirmed pin.
  • The map zoom: start on the city, end on the single street, so the scope feels small.
  • The reviewer's sentence: a seven-second clip of someone explaining why they approved a proof.
  • The ledger: a screenshot of the week: pins added, pins confirmed, walks run.
  • The invitation: date, time, corner, duration. Nothing else.
08

Reddit, Mastodon, Bluesky, HN

These are rooms with hosts, not billboards. The win is not a spike of clicks; it is a thread where people argue about the idea and a handful of them live in the city you named. Read the rules of the specific community before typing, and assume a moderator will judge the account by its history, not by the post.

On Reddit there is no sitewide percentage that guarantees safety. Reddit's own help says promotional content is not automatically spam, that some communities ban it outright and others apply a ten-percent guideline, and that the community's rules decide; see the spam guidance. In practice: comment for two weeks before posting, write the story of the walk with photographs, put the link in a comment rather than the title, and answer every reply. City subreddits and civic-tech communities are the two rooms worth the effort; a national subreddit is usually a waste.

Mastodon and Bluesky reward identity and repetition rather than reach. Use one account with a real name, a bio that says the city, and two or three hashtags that people actually follow, such as the civic-tech and open-mapping tags. Post the same walk report you post elsewhere, then reply to the people who boost it. There is no algorithm to game, so consistency is the whole game.

Hacker News is the opposite extreme: a technical audience that punishes promotion and rewards a working thing. “Show HN” is only for something people can try, ideally without a sign-up, and the guidelines say to be present in the thread and to skip landing pages. Civic Perks can qualify because the map is visible; Social Tool Kit cannot until a visitor can see a published proof without creating a Google account. Post the architecture decision, not the campaign.

One account, one voice. Never create a second profile to agree with yourself, and never ask a group chat to upvote. Both are easy to detect and impossible to undo.
09

Launch days that do not waste the work

A launch is a deadline for the assets, not a substitute for them. Pick the day you can sit with the thread for eight hours, set one measurable goal, and tell the people who already used the app before you tell strangers.

Product Hunt works on personal accounts, forbids paid votes and asks makers to choose a goal before a date and then show up to answer comments. The launch guide is explicit that the day is won in the weeks before it, and the promotion rules are equally clear about what not to do. A realistic goal for a civic app is thirty real trials and ten pieces of feedback, not a badge.

Three launch surfaces, three different jobs
SurfaceGood forFails when
Product HuntEarly adopters, honest feedbackThe product needs a group to work
Show HNTechnical scrutiny, map and data questionsThe first screen is a sign-up
Local pressNeighbours who never open tech sitesThere is no named street or person

Local press is underrated and cheap: a neighbourhood blog, a parish newsletter, a university civic lab, a radio slot. The pitch is one paragraph with a photograph of a real corner and a quote from a real participant. Send it the day after a walk, not the day before a launch, because the story is the walk.

Whatever the surface, do not launch both apps on the same day. Test one promise with one audience, keep the second app for the next month, and write down what the first taught you. Two launches in one week split the only resource that matters: your attention in the comments.

The 30-day phone calendar

This calendar assumes twenty minutes a day and one walk a week. It is deliberately boring: the same four posts, the same three groups, the same map. Boring is what makes a district look alive.

Four weeks, one district
WeekFocusPostsGate to pass
OneKit, Page, first ten testersThree: pitch, clip, invitationTen people open the map
TwoFirst walk, pins, confirmationsFour: before, during, after, ledgerFifteen pins, five confirmed
ThreePartner group, second walkThree: partner, proof, thanksOne group brings five people
FourLaunch day, review, next districtThree: launch, feedback, resultsThirty activated walkers

The daily twenty minutes

  • Five minutes replying. Every comment and message, in the order they arrived.
  • Five minutes commenting. Two useful replies in other people's threads, no links.
  • Five minutes capturing. One photograph or clip from today's walk.
  • Five minutes logging. New users, pins, confirmations, and where they came from.
What a week of posts actually looks like

Monday: the week's corner and the meeting time. Tuesday: a ten-second clip of the problem. Wednesday: a screenshot of the map with the new pin. Thursday: a neighbour's sentence, with consent. Friday: the count of confirmed pins and an invitation for Saturday. Saturday: live from the walk, three frames. Sunday: nothing, or a thank-you.

The calendar is a floor, not a ceiling. If a walk produces a better story than the plan, post the story and move the plan. If a week produces nothing, post the empty result honestly; a ledger with three pins is more credible than a month of stock photographs.

11

The pilot that fills an empty map

An empty civic map is a design problem disguised as a marketing problem. The fix is a seeded pilot: a small number of people who agree to act in the same week, in the same place, so that the first visitor sees evidence of other humans. The product's own cold-start rules help, but they cannot invent a second scout.

Civic Perks: the three-scout week

Recruit three people who already walk the same route: a shopkeeper, a dog walker, a parent on the school run. Ask each for five pins from one street over seven days. The daily cap protects the map from a single enthusiast, and the third scout is what unlocks validation and solving in that area. The cap and the flag are described in the official rules page, and they set the recruitment target: three, not thirty.

Social Tool Kit: one group, one proof

Do not recruit individuals. Recruit a group that already has a task: a cleanup crew, a food bank, a school club, a scout troop. Help them publish one challenge, film the proof, and collect seven reviews from people outside the group. The published challenge is the campaign asset: it is the first thing a stranger can see that proves the ritual works. Seven is the number to chase, not a follower count.

A seven-day pilot runbook
DayActionOwner
OneMeet the partner, choose one street and one taskYou
TwoCreate the QR poster and the group inviteYou
ThreeWalk, pin, photograph, no editing yetPartner
FourConfirm each other's pins, capture the momentPartner
FivePublish the proof, ask seven outsiders to ratePartner
SixPost the result in the local group and the PageYou
SevenWrite down what confused people, fix one thingBoth

Give the partner a one-page kit: the pitch, the QR code, three screenshots, the walk time, and the name of the person who answers questions. Ask for one thing only: five people from their group on one walk. A partner who is asked for a mailing, a testimonial and a venue will do none of the three.

Five numbers, no tracking

A civic app that spies on its neighbours has already lost the argument. You can run this campaign without cookies, pixels or a third-party analytics script, and still know what works, because the product already records the actions you care about.

What to review every Sunday
NumberWhere it comes fromHealthy sign
Activated walkersNew accounts with one real actionRising week on week
Pins per streetMap data, grouped by areaThree or more in one street
Confirmation rateConfirmed pins divided by added pinsAbove half within a week
Seven-day returnPeople who come back onceA third of the first-timers
Source mixUTM tags on the landing linkTwo channels, not one

UTM parameters are read from the request itself, so no script is needed: the landing page can record the campaign label in its own logs or in a first-party counter. Search Console shows which queries already find the site, which is free research for the next post. Server logs show the pages that get shared, and the ones that get abandoned. All three are first-party.

Write the five numbers in one place every Sunday and compare them with the week before, not with an industry benchmark. Three consecutive weeks of flat pins in a district is the signal to change the partner or the street. A spike in visits with no pins is the signal to change the first screen, not the advertising. The privacy baseline is already described in this blog's privacy manual; the campaign should be able to say “no cookies, no third-party analytics” and mean it.

One dashboard, one page. If the weekly review needs more than a screen, it will not happen on a phone. Five numbers, one line of commentary, one decision.
13

Consent, faces and the law

Civic content is content about people in public space. That is exactly where a well-meaning campaign can do harm. The rules below are not legal advice; they are the minimum a small team can adopt without a lawyer on call.

  • Ask before a face is recognisable. A wide shot of a street is one thing; a close-up of a person picking up litter is another. Say where it will be published and how to have it removed.
  • Children need a guardian's yes. No exceptions, no “it was a public event”.
  • Never map a shelter, a clinic or a person's home. A pin that reveals where a vulnerable person sleeps is a safety incident, not a data point.
  • Strip the metadata. Photographs carry GPS coordinates. Remove them before publishing anything that shows a private address.
  • Name the moderator. One person, one inbox, a published response time, and a route for reports that is not a public comment thread.
  • Delete on request. A person who asks to be removed from a post should get an answer the same day.

Brazil's data protection law applies to personal data collected from people in the country, and the national authority publishes the law in English. In practice that means a lawful basis for the account data, a way to export or delete it, and a written answer to anyone who asks what is stored. If the app also serves the European Union, the same questions arrive under the GDPR; the answers should be the same.

Platforms add their own layer. Facebook and Instagram restrict how brands may tag people and contact them privately; Nextdoor requires a real-world name and a precise service area for a business page. A campaign that films first and asks later will lose the account and the neighbourhood's trust at the same time.

Do not publish a “worst streets” ranking. Shaming a street brings clicks and destroys the partnership that makes the next walk possible. Name the problem, not the people.
14

The first hundred users

One hundred activated people is enough to make a district look alive and enough to learn whether the idea travels. The plan below counts activation, not accounts: a person who pins, confirms or reviews once. Download counts are vanity, and this app does not need a download to be used.

Six weeks, four gates
WeekMoveCumulative activatedDecision
OneTen hand-recruited testers, one street10Fix the first screen
TwoFirst public walk, Page and group posts30Keep or change the corner
ThreePartner group brings five, second walk55Add a second partner
FourLocal launch, one channel, one goal80Double the best channel
FiveSecond district seeded by the first95Write the repeatable script
SixReview, thank, hand over to a local host100+Choose the next city

The numbers are targets, not forecasts. The point of the gates is to force a decision every week instead of waiting for a miracle. If week two ends at twelve activated people, the problem is the invitation, the corner or the partner, and the cheapest experiment is to change one of those, not to buy advertising.

The last act of the six weeks matters most: hand the district to someone who lives there. A local host, a shop, a parish or an association can keep the walks going when the founder's attention moves. Growth that depends on one person's calendar is not growth; it is a performance.

15

What not to do

  • Buying followers, reviews or upvotes. It corrupts the one number the product is built on.
  • Mass direct messages on Instagram or Facebook. The account gets limited before the campaign starts.
  • Posting the same link in ten groups in one day. Moderators talk to each other.
  • Launching in a country because it is large. Density beats population every time.
  • Promising verified impact for a point that counts views. The wiki says so plainly; the campaign must too.
  • Filming people without consent and calling it public space. It is a complaint waiting to happen.
  • Running two apps' campaigns at once with one pair of hands.
  • Adding a cookie banner and a tracking pixel to a product whose promise is privacy.
