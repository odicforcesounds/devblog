Japan: A Dossier on an Archipelago

An island country of 124 million people, four-fifths of it mountains, sitting on four tectonic plates, that has twice rebuilt itself from fire — once in 1868 and once in 1945 — and that has produced one of the most distinctive material cultures on earth. This dossier follows the same four threads as its companion on China: the state, the language, the image and the household, from the Jōmon potters to the empty villages of 2026.

The archipelago

Japan is not four islands and not one country in the way a continental state is. The recount of 2023 put the number of islands in Japanese territory at 14,125, of which about 430 are inhabited; four of them — Honshu, Hokkaido, Kyushu, Shikoku — hold 97 per cent of the population. The total land area is 377,975 square kilometres, smaller than California, and about 73 per cent of it is mountain forest. The habitable plains are narrow, the rivers short and steep, and the sea between the islands is a highway rather than a barrier: the Inland Sea was the country's first commercial artery.

The geology is the reason for almost everything else. The archipelago sits where the Pacific, Philippine, Eurasian and North American plates meet, which produces about 1,500 earthquakes a year that are felt, around 2,000 active faults, 111 active volcanoes and the hot springs that made bathing a national institution. Mount Fuji, at 3,776 metres, is a stratovolcano last active in 1707. The same subduction that builds the mountains also builds the tsunamis: the Tōhoku earthquake of 11 March 2011, magnitude 9.0, shifted Honshu two metres east and sent a wave into the coast that killed more than 18,000 people and destroyed the Fukushima Daiichi plant.

That instability shaped the culture in ways that are easy to sentimentalise and hard to overstate. Buildings were traditionally light and replaceable, not monumental; the great shrine at Ise is rebuilt every twenty years from new timber on an adjacent plot, which is both a ritual of renewal and a practical response to rot and fire. Cities have burned repeatedly — Edo burned in 1657 and took 100,000 lives; Tokyo and Yokohama burned again in 1923 and in 1945 — and each time the rebuilding was faster and less ornamental than what it replaced.

The political geography is now 47 prefectures, and the demographic one is a single corridor: the Tokaido belt from Tokyo through Nagoya to Osaka and Kobe holds more than half the population, with Tokyo's metropolitan area at about 37 million, the largest on earth. Outside that corridor, the country is emptying. There are around 8.5 million empty houses, akiya, and villages where the school, the post office and the bus have all closed. Japan, in the official view, is one nation with one language and one culture; the reality is a metropole and a periphery that no longer need each other.

Jōmon, Yayoi, Kofun

The Jōmon period (roughly 14,000 BCE to 300 BCE) is named for the cord marks pressed into its pottery, and that pottery is among the oldest in the world. These were hunter-gatherers, but not nomadic ones: they lived in pit houses, fished, gathered acorns and chestnuts, hunted deer and boar, and produced figurines — dogū, mostly female, some with goggles and swollen bellies — whose purpose no one knows. The famous flame-style vessels of the Middle Jōmon are the most exuberant ceramics any Neolithic culture made.

Yayoi (300 BCE to 250 CE) brought wet-rice agriculture, bronze and iron, and a population that arrived in waves from the Korean peninsula, mixing with the Jōmon descendants. Rice changed everything: paddies need cooperation, irrigation needs organisation, and stored grain needs defending, which is the standard recipe for ranked societies. By the first century CE Chinese histories describe a country of a hundred kingdoms, and the Records of Wei describe one of them, Yamatai, ruled by a shamaness named Himiko, who sent envoys to the Chinese court and was buried with a hundred servants.

The Kofun period (250–538) is named for the burial mounds: keyhole-shaped tumuli, some of them enormous — the tomb attributed to Emperor Nintoku near Osaka covers 32 hectares and is longer than the Great Pyramid is tall. The largest are imperial and cannot be excavated by law, which is convenient for a monarchy that prefers its origins mythological. By the end of the period the Yamato court, based in the Nara basin, had established itself as the dominant power, had a genealogy — the Kojiki of 712 and the Nihon Shoki of 720 — that traced the imperial line to the sun goddess, and had begun to import Chinese writing, Chinese administration and Chinese religion.

What is worth noticing here is that Japan's foundational myths are written down in the eighth century, in a script borrowed from a country whose culture was already a thousand years older. The archipelago had a rich oral tradition and no writing of its own. Its literature begins as a translation project.

The Chinese century

Buddhism arrived officially in 538 or 552, depending on which chronicle you follow, as a gift from the king of Paekche in Korea; by the end of the century it was the religion of the court and the engine of state building. Prince Shōtoku (574–622), regent and legend, is credited with the Seventeen-Article Constitution of 604 — a set of injunctions to officials, in which "harmony" is the first virtue — and with the adoption of the Chinese calendar, the Chinese rank system and the Chinese model of a capital. The Taika reforms of 645 went further: land nationalised, taxes on rice, a census, a conscript army. The project was to make Japan a Chinese-style bureaucratic empire with a Japanese ruling house at the top.

It did not last in that form. The Nara period (710–784) built a permanent capital, a grid city modelled on Chang'an, with the great temple of Tōdaiji and its bronze Buddha dedicated in 752, and it produced the Kojiki, the Nihon Shoki and the Man'yōshū, the first great anthology of Japanese poetry, in a script in which Chinese characters were used for their sound. Then the court moved to Heian-kyō — Kyoto — in 794 and, over the next four centuries, deliberately stopped looking at China.

The Heian period (794–1185) is the most refined court culture in Japanese history, and the least like the rest of the country. The Fujiwara family governed as regents, marrying daughters into the imperial line; the emperor often abdicated young to rule as a cloistered monk; the provinces were left to their own gentry. At court, the arts were calligraphy, incense, poetry and taste. Two inventions made it possible: the simplification of Chinese characters into kana, a syllabary that could write Japanese as it was spoken, and the habit of writing in it. The result includes the Tale of Genji, written by Murasaki Shikibu around 1010, often called the first novel — a long, ironic, psychological narrative about court love with no plot in the modern sense; and Sei Shōnagon's Pillow Book, a collection of lists and observations that is the ancestor of the blog.

Buddhism in this period went esoteric. Saichō and Kūkai travelled to Tang China in 804 and returned with Tendai and Shingon, two systems of ritual, mantra and mandala in which enlightenment could be reached through the body and the hand; Shingon's headquarters at Mount Kōya is still a cemetery town in the forest. The older Nara schools kept their scholarship, and the court kept both. The pattern — several Buddhist systems coexisting, each serving a different social function — never disappeared.

The warrior centuries

The Heian court lost control of the countryside in the twelfth century to the provincial warrior houses it had armed, and in 1185 Minamoto no Yoritomo defeated the Taira in the sea battle of Dan-no-ura and took power. In 1192 he was named shogun. The emperor remained in Kyoto as the source of legitimacy; the shogun governed from Kamakura, and the arrangement — a military government that derives its authority from a sacred but powerless court — lasted, with interruptions, until 1868.

The Kamakura shogunate (1185–1333) was a government of warriors and their retainers, codified in the Jōei Code of 1232, a set of rules about land, inheritance and justice that is one of the earliest warrior law codes. It survived the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 — the second fleet was destroyed by a typhoon the Japanese called kamikaze, a divine wind — but the cost of the defence bankrupted the loyalty system, and in 1333 the shogunate fell. The Ashikaga shoguns who followed ruled from the Muromachi district of Kyoto through a century of civil war that culminated in the Ōnin War (1467–77), which destroyed most of the capital and opened 150 years of Sengoku, the "country at war", in which dozens of daimyō fought each other and the emperor sold calligraphy to survive.

Out of that disorder came two of the country's most durable aesthetic forms, both products of Zen. The first is the Gozan system: the five great Zen monasteries ranked by the shogunate, which became the intellectual bureaucracy of the age and the channel through which Chinese painting, poetry and printing continued to arrive. The second is the aesthetic of restraint that runs from the tea room through the garden and the print: wabi, the beauty of the worn and the plain; sabi, the beauty of age; mono no aware, the pathos of things passing. Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591) formalised the tea ceremony as an exercise in humility — a small room, a rough bowl, a single flower — and was ordered to commit suicide by his patron Hideyoshi, which is the culture's own lesson about the relation between art and power.

Unification came from three men in succession. Oda Nobunaga (1534–1582) broke the military power of the monasteries and the autonomous leagues; Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537–1598) completed the conquest, disarmed the peasantry in the "sword hunt" of 1588, and invaded Korea twice; Tokugawa Ieyasu won the battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and founded the shogunate that would rule for 265 years.

The Tokugawa peace

The Tokugawa order was a machine for preventing war, and it worked. The daimyō were ranked by their relation to the ruling house, forced to spend alternate years in Edo under the sankin kōtai system, and bound by marriage and hostage rules; their wives and heirs lived permanently in the shogun's capital. Roads, post stations and a uniform currency made the country a single market. The frontiers were closed to most foreign trade from the 1630s — the sakoku policy — with exceptions for the Dutch at Dejima in Nagasaki, the Chinese, and Korea through Tsushima. Christianity, which had arrived with Francis Xavier in 1549 and grown to perhaps 300,000 adherents, was suppressed with extraordinary violence at Shimabara in 1637–38 and then driven underground, where a hidden church survived for two centuries.

Peace produced cities and a commercial culture. Edo grew to a million people by 1700, larger than London, and Osaka became the country's rice market, where futures contracts were traded from the 1730s. A merchant class with money and no political status spent it on pleasure: the licensed quarters, the theatre, the print. Ukiyo-e, "pictures of the floating world", began as advertisements for actors and courtesans and became, in the nineteenth century, one of the great achievements of world printmaking. Hokusai (1760–1849) made the Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji, including the wave; Hiroshige (1797–1858) made the road to Kyoto into a series that Van Gogh copied; Utamaro (1753–1806) made portraits of women that are also studies of the line.

Literature found its own forms. Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) took the seventeen-syllable haiku from a party game to a compressed meditation on time and travel, above all in Narrow Road to the Deep North. Ihara Saikaku wrote realistic novels of merchants and desire in the 1680s. Chikamatsu Monzaemon (1653–1725) wrote puppet plays about double suicides that are the Japanese equivalent of the Elizabethan stage. Literacy was high for a pre-industrial society — perhaps 30 to 40 per cent of men in the late period, more in cities — taught in thousands of temple schools.

And the country was being studied from inside. Rangaku, "Dutch learning", made Western anatomy, astronomy and medicine available to a small circle of scholars in Nagasaki; the first Japanese dissection, in 1771, was recorded in a book that circulated widely. When the American squadron under Commodore Perry arrived in 1853 and demanded that the country open, the intellectual groundwork for what followed had already been laid — and so had the knowledge that the gap was real.

Meiji, empire, defeat

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 was not a revolution from below; it was a coup by a coalition of southwestern domains that replaced the shogunate with direct imperial rule and then used the emperor's restored authority to do what the shogunate had refused. The Charter Oath promised deliberative assemblies and the abandonment of "evil customs of the past". The Iwakura Mission of 1871–73 took half the government around the United States and Europe for eighteen months to study constitutions, factories and schools. Conscription was introduced in 1873, a national school system in 1872, the Meiji Constitution in 1889, and a parliament in 1890.

The result was the fastest industrialisation of the nineteenth century. Textile mills, then shipyards, then railways; a state that built the infrastructure and then sold it to friendly businessmen, creating the zaibatsu; a land tax that squeezed the peasantry to pay for it. Japan defeated China in 1894–95, took Taiwan, defeated Russia in 1904–05 — the first defeat of a European power by an Asian one in the modern era — annexed Korea in 1910, and entered the First World War on the Allied side to take German concessions in China.

The 1920s were the country's democratic decade: party cabinets, universal male suffrage in 1925, a vibrant mass culture of cinema, radio, department stores and cafés. It ended with the Great Kantō earthquake of 1923, which killed about 105,000 people and was followed by massacres of Koreans by vigilantes, and with the collapse of export markets in 1929. The army, which answered to the emperor and not to the cabinet, took Manchuria in 1931 and turned it into a puppet state; junior officers assassinated politicians in 1932 and 1936; and from 1937 the country was at war with China in a conflict it could not end. Pearl Harbor in December 1941 opened the Pacific war. By 1945, sixty-six cities had been firebombed, Tokyo's March raid killed around 100,000 people in a night, and the atomic bombs fell on Hiroshima on 6 August and Nagasaki on 9 August. The emperor announced surrender on 15 August.

The reckoning with that period is incomplete and politically live: the Nanjing massacre, the comfort women system, forced labour, Unit 731 and the Yasukuni shrine, where convicted war criminals are among the enshrined. Official apologies have been issued and contested; textbook language is negotiated; the neighbours remember. A dossier that treats Japanese culture as pure aesthetics has skipped the century that produced the aesthetics' darkest patron.

The postwar settlement

The American occupation (1945–52) did five things that shaped the rest of the century. It dismantled the military and the colonial empire. It imposed a new constitution, drafted in a week by American lawyers and adopted in 1947, which made the emperor a symbol without political power, gave women the vote, and wrote Article 9: the renunciation of war as a sovereign right and of "land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential". It broke up the zaibatsu — partly, and then reversed course when the Cold War made Japan a forward base. It redistributed land to tenants, creating a conservative rural electorate that would keep the Liberal Democratic Party in power for most of the next seventy years. And it rebuilt the economy around a fixed exchange rate of 360 yen to the dollar, which made exports cheap.

The growth that followed was the largest sustained expansion in modern history: from 1955 to 1973 the economy grew at close to 10 per cent a year. The mechanisms are worth naming because they became a global template. The Ministry of International Trade and Industry coordinated credit, licences and targets. The keiretsu — bank-centred corporate groups — spread risk across suppliers. Lifetime employment for regular workers, seniority pay and enterprise unions bought loyalty. Quality control, taught by an American statistician named W. Edwards Deming, became the Toyota Production System: just-in-time inventory, kaizen, andon cords that any worker could pull to stop the line.

The high-water marks are visible: the Shinkansen opened in 1964, the year of the Tokyo Olympics; the bullet train, the transistor radio, the Walkman and the compact car made "Made in Japan" mean precision rather than imitation. By 1989 the Nikkei average reached 38,915 and Japanese banks held the largest deposits in the world; it was said that the grounds of the imperial palace were worth more than California.

The long stagnation

Then the bubble broke. The Bank of Japan raised rates in 1989–90, land prices fell, and the collateral behind the banking system evaporated. The Nikkei fell by more than 60 per cent over two years and did not regain its 1989 level for thirty-four years. What followed was not a depression but something less familiar: a slow, stubborn deflation, in which households postponed purchases because prices would be lower next year, firms repaid debt instead of investing, and the government ran deficits that kept the economy alive without restarting it. Economists call it a balance-sheet recession, and it became the reference case for the West after 2008.

The social costs accumulated quietly. The lifetime employment system survived for the core of large firms and was withdrawn from everyone else: around 37 per cent of workers are now non-regular — part-time, contract, dispatched — with lower pay and no security. The generation that entered the labour market in the 1990s is called the "employment ice age" cohort, and it never caught up. The birth rate fell to 1.20 children per woman in 2023, deaths exceeded births by more than 800,000 a year, and the population is projected to fall below 100 million by the 2050s. The 65-and-over share passed 29 per cent, the highest of any large economy.

Within that frame there are things that work better than outsiders assume. Health care is universal with a co-payment and no gatekeeping, and life expectancy is among the highest in the world. Trains run to the second. Violent crime is exceptionally low. The 2011 disaster produced a civic response that was studied worldwide. And the country's cultural production — manga, anime, games, fashion, food — has a global reach that its economy no longer has.

Politics has been stable to the point of inertia: the LDP has governed for all but four years since 1955, through factions, farm subsidies and a fragmented opposition, and the country has been slow to liberalise immigration (about 3.4 million foreign residents, roughly 2.7 per cent), slow to raise the retirement age, and slow to change the tax base. In 2015 the government reinterpreted Article 9 to allow collective self-defence, and in 2022 committed to raising defence spending to 2 per cent of GDP by 2027 — the largest shift in security policy since 1945, and one that is still contested. The country's problem is not that it cannot reform; it is that it reforms at the speed of consensus, and the demographic clock is faster.

A language with three scripts

Japanese is the surviving main branch of the Japonic family; the other branch is the Ryukyuan languages of Okinawa, which UNESCO lists as endangered and which the Japanese state has only recently begun to protect. Ainu, the language of Hokkaido's indigenous people, is a separate family, now spoken by a handful of elderly speakers and being revived by a young generation. The idea that Japan is linguistically homogeneous is a nineteenth-century political construction, and it was enforced in schools: Okinawan children were punished for speaking their own language until the 1940s.

The standard language, hyōjungo, is based on the speech of Tokyo's educated class and spread by compulsory schooling, radio and television. It distinguishes pitch accent — the difference between hashi "chopsticks", hashi "bridge" and hashi "edge" is the pattern of high and low — but not stress, and its rhythm is counted in morae, so a small tsu or a long vowel adds a beat. Grammar is subject–object–verb, with the verb last and the sentence's meaning held until the end; particles mark the role of each phrase — wa for the topic, ga for the subject, o for the object, ni for place or recipient, de for means. There is no grammatical number, gender or article, and no future tense: the same form serves present and future, and time comes from context or from the ending.

Politeness is grammar, not decoration. Keigo has three layers — polite, respectful (sonkeigo, raising the other) and humble (kenjōgo, lowering oneself) — with different verb forms for each, and the choice depends on the relative status of speaker, listener and subject. Getting it wrong is not a stylistic error; it is a statement about who you think you are. Foreign learners are forgiven, junior employees are not.

The writing system is the part that defeats outsiders and is actually simpler than it looks once the division of labour is clear. Kanji, the Chinese characters, carry meaning: there are 2,136 jōyō characters designated for general use, and a newspaper reader needs about 3,000. Hiragana, 46 signs, writes grammatical endings, particles and native words; katakana, the same 46 in a different style, writes loanwords, onomatopoeia and emphasis. A single sentence mixes all three, and the mixture is the language's aesthetic: the eye reads meaning in the characters, grammar in the hiragana and foreignness in the katakana.

Loanwords came in waves — Chinese in the first millennium, Portuguese and Dutch in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (pan from pão, biiru from bier), German in medicine, English since 1945 in a flood — and they are now so dense that a Japanese newspaper can be hard for an older reader. The country's relation to English is a separate study: compulsory from primary school, widely studied, rarely spoken, and often replaced by a vocabulary of wasei-eigo, English words coined in Japan that an English speaker would not recognise.

The line and the page

Calligraphy, shodō, came from China with the characters and became a distinct art in the Heian court, where the kana syllabary allowed a flowing, connected hand that Chinese writing could not. The three scripts — formal, running, cursive — are still taught in schools as a subject, and the annual New Year calligraphy contest is a mass ritual. What the tradition prizes is not legibility but the quality of the line: the pressure, the speed, the space between characters, and the fact that the brush records a body moving.

Poetry is the country's central literary form, and its history is a history of compression. The Man'yōshū of the eighth century collected some 4,500 poems in a plain, direct register. The Heian court turned poetry into a social accomplishment, with anthologies compiled by imperial command and competitions in which a couplet had to be finished on the spot. The waka, thirty-one syllables in five lines, remained the aristocratic form for a thousand years; the haiku, seventeen syllables in three lines, became the popular one, and Bashō's best verses are not descriptions but juxtapositions — an old pond, a frog, the sound of water.

Prose arrived later and late bloomed. The Tale of Genji and the Pillow Book are Heian; the war chronicles, above all the Tale of the Heike, are thirteenth-century, written for recitation by blind lute priests and full of the Buddhist lesson that the powerful fall. The Tokugawa period produced the first commercially printed popular literature — sharebon, kibyōshi, ninjōbon, the genres of the lending-library trade — and the modern novel begins with Futabatei Shimei's Ukigumo in 1887, written under the influence of Russian realism. The twentieth century's major names — Natsume Sōseki, Tanizaki, Kawabata, Mishima, Ōe, Murakami — are all, in different ways, writing about the encounter with the West.

The physical book matters here in a way it does not in Europe. Paper, binding and typography are crafts with their own guilds; a Japanese edition is often a designed object, with a dust jacket that comes off to reveal a second cover. The same care shows in the stationery, the notebook and the hanko seal that still replaces the signature on contracts — a persistence of the stamp over the autograph that surprises every foreigner who tries to open a bank account.

Image, ink, and woodblock

Japanese painting has two parents: Chinese ink painting, imported by monks and scholars, and a native tradition of flat colour and strong outline, yamato-e, used for narrative handscrolls of court life and battles. The tension between them is the history of the art. The Kanō school, founded in the fifteenth century, served the shogunate with a monumental Chinese-derived style; the Rinpa school, with Kōrin (1658–1716), went the other way, into design — gold leaf, poppies, irises, waves — and is the ancestor of Japanese graphic design.

The print is the form that changed the world. Ukiyo-e was a commercial product of Edo's entertainment districts: a designer drew, a carver cut a cherry-wood block for each colour, a printer pulled the sheet, and a publisher sold it for the price of a bowl of noodles. The division of labour is the point — it was an industry, not a studio practice — and its subjects were actors, courtesans, sumo wrestlers, famous places, ghosts and erotica. When Japanese prints reached Paris in the 1860s, their flat colour, cropped composition and unapologetic ordinariness reorganised European painting: Manet, Degas, Van Gogh and Toulouse-Lautrec all took from them, and the word japonisme was coined for the fashion that followed.

Photography arrived in the 1840s and by the 1930s had produced a distinctive modernist school; after 1945, Japanese photographers — Domon Ken, Moriyama Daidō, Araki — became some of the most exhibited in the world. The visual culture of the late twentieth century is manga and anime, which deserve to be treated as an industrial art form rather than a genre: Tezuka Osamu's cinematic page layouts in the 1950s established the grammar, and the production committees, voice actors, animators and doujin circles that followed make up a system as structured as any film industry.

Landscape has a special status, and the mountain is its emblem: Fuji appears in the Man'yōshū, in Hokusai's thirty-six views, in a thousand souvenir tea cups, and since 2013 on the UNESCO list as a sacred place and source of artistic inspiration. The Japanese word for landscape, fūkei, carries the sense of scenery as something seen and framed, which is why the garden, the print and the window are discussed in the same breath.

Clay, lacquer, steel, cloth

Japan's craft tradition is a matter of materials and of the organisations that kept them alive. Pottery runs from Jōmon cord-marked ware through the six ancient kilns — Seto, Tokoname, Echizen, Shigaraki, Tanba, Bizen — to the tea ceramics of the sixteenth century, when Shino, Oribe and Raku were developed for the tea room. Raku is the clearest case of an aesthetic choice: a low-fired, hand-shaped bowl, taken from the kiln red-hot and cooled in straw, deliberately irregular, made to be held. Arita in Kyushu produced the first Japanese porcelain in the seventeenth century, after Korean potters were brought back from Hideyoshi's invasion, and the export ware shipped from the port of Imari became the "Japan" pattern of European tables.

Lacquer, urushi, is the country's most laborious material: the sap of a tree, applied in layers, cured in humid air, decorated with gold powder (maki-e) or inlaid shell, and requiring months for a single box. It was used for armour, for temple sculpture, for the bentō box and for the chawan that a tea master might keep in a silk bag. The craft is now sustained by a small number of workshops, a designation system for "living national treasures", and a domestic market that is shrinking.

Steel is the other tradition. The Japanese sword is a folded, differentially hardened blade — a soft spine, a hard edge, a curvature produced by the quench — and its manufacture was a religious as well as a technical practice, with the smith purifying himself before the forge. The same metallurgy went into carpentry tools, which is why Japanese joinery is possible at all: a plane that takes a shaving of a hundredth of a millimetre, a saw that cuts on the pull stroke, a chisel hardened to hold an edge in oak.

Cloth completes the set. Silk, hemp and later cotton; indigo dyeing (aizome) with its fermentation vats; shibori resist binding; kasuri ikat; and the kimono, which is not a garment but a system of panels that can be cut from a single bolt of cloth about 36 centimetres wide and reassembled without waste. The kimono's decline after 1945 is one of the clearest cases of a whole material culture becoming ceremonial within two generations: today it is worn at weddings, funerals, the coming-of-age ceremony and, on a few streets of Kyoto, as a rental.

Timber, garden, tatami

Japanese architecture is a timber architecture with a modular discipline as strict as the Chinese, and a different conclusion. The module is the ken, the bay between posts, and the mat, tatami, is sized to it — a room is described by the number of mats it holds. Walls are sliding screens, fusuma and shōji, which move, and the house is therefore a set of rooms that can be one room. Floors are raised, shoes come off, and the boundary between inside and garden is a veranda, the engawa, which is neither.

Two building traditions run in parallel. The shrine at Ise, rebuilt every twenty years on alternating sites, is the purest statement of the idea that a building is a ritual repeated rather than an object preserved; the joining is elaborate, the wood is unpainted, and the whole compound is replaced by identical carpenters using identical tools. At the other end, the sukiya style of the tea house and the merchant machiya of Kyoto developed a refined, asymmetrical, deliberately imperfect architecture — a post left in its natural shape, a window placed where the light is good rather than where symmetry demands.

The garden is the art form that most rewards attention. The dry garden, karesansui, uses raked gravel and a few stones to stand for water and mountains, and Ryōan-ji's fifteen stones, arranged so that one is always hidden from any viewpoint, is the most photographed puzzle in the country. The stroll garden, kaiyū, is designed to be walked, with framed views, borrowed scenery and a path that reveals each composition in sequence. Both are exercises in mitate, the deliberate substitution of one thing for another, and both depend on maintenance: a garden is a practice, not an object.

The modern city broke with all of this. Postwar Tokyo is a low-rise sprawl of concrete, glass and narrow streets, rebuilt after 1945 and again after the bubble, with zoning that allowed almost anything and a construction industry that replaced buildings every thirty years. The result is ugly in the way that only rapid growth is, and it is also the reason the city works: density, mixed use and trains. Kyoto, Nara and Kanazawa kept enough of the old fabric to sell it, and the preservation of a wooden townscape is now a substantial industry.

Noh, kabuki, and the moving image

Noh is the oldest surviving theatre in the world and the most austere. A bare cypress stage with a painted pine, a chorus, two musicians and a masked principal who moves with glacial slowness; the plays are about ghosts and the dead, and the dramatic action has usually happened before the play begins. Zeami (1363–1443) wrote the theory as well as the plays, and his idea that the actor's task is to make the audience feel what is not shown — yūgen, the mysterious depth — is the closest thing Japanese aesthetics has to a doctrine. Between the plays, kyōgen farces send up the whole proceedings, which tells you the tradition knew what it was doing.

Kabuki is the opposite: colour, noise, acrobatics, and a stage that includes a revolving platform and a walkway through the audience, the hanamichi, so that an entrance is an event. It began in 1603 with a woman named Izumo no Okuni dancing in Kyoto, was banned for women and then for boys, and settled into an all-male form with onnagata specialists playing women. The mie, a held pose at a moment of tension, is the form's signature, and it exists to be photographed — the woodblock print and the theatre grew up together. Bunraku, the puppet theatre, is in some ways the most sophisticated: half-life-size puppets, each worked by three visible operators, with a chanter and a shamisen, performing plays by Chikamatsu about merchants, obligation and double suicide that are the Japanese equivalent of tragedy.

Music runs from gagaku, the ancient court ensemble of winds, strings and percussion imported from Tang China and preserved by the imperial household, to the solo meditations of the shakuhachi bamboo flute, played by wandering monks of the Fuke sect as a form of Zen practice. The koto zither and the shamisen lute accompanied the pleasure quarters; taiko drumming, once a village and temple practice, became a global concert form in the postwar period. Enka, the sentimental ballad genre, is the sound of the twentieth century's nostalgia, and its popularity tracks the decline of the places it sings about.

Film is where the country's visual culture went in the twentieth century. Ozu Yasujirō filmed family life from a fixed low camera and called it "nothing happening"; Kurosawa Akira's Rashomon won Venice in 1951 and opened the West to Japanese cinema; Mizoguchi Kenji's long takes and Mizoguchi's women are a separate education. Animation, from Tezuka's Astro Boy to Miyazaki's Spirited Away, became the country's most successful cultural export, and the dance of the Noh stage — slow, weighted, held — is still visible in how anime draws a character turning.

Shintō, Buddhism, and the Christian century

Shintō is not a religion with a founder, a scripture or a doctrine. It is a practice of relating to kami: powers or presences in a place, a mountain, a waterfall, a rock, an ancestor, a storm, a rice field. A shrine is where a kami is invited to dwell, marked by a torii gate; a priest purifies, offers food and recites; a festival carries the kami through the streets in a portable shrine so that the community and the power can meet. There is no moral code and no afterlife to speak of — the concern is this world, its fertility, its health and its pollution. The emperor was, until 1945, claimed as a living kami descended from the sun goddess Amaterasu, whose shrine at Ise is the tradition's centre.

Buddhism arrived in the sixth century and supplied what Shintō did not: cosmology, funerals, and a doctrine of salvation. The two were combined for a thousand years in shinbutsu-shūgō, in which kami were understood as local manifestations of Buddhas — a theological fusion so complete that most shrines had a Buddhist temple beside them. The Meiji state separated them by decree in 1868, an operation that was violent in places and that invented "Shintō" as a national religion distinct from Buddhism, then made it the ideology of the imperial state until the American occupation ordered the separation of religion and state in 1945.

The Buddhist schools that matter: Tendai and Shingon, the esoteric systems of the Heian court; Pure Land, which taught that salvation comes by faith in Amida's vow rather than by one's own effort, and which became the religion of the ordinary household; Nichiren, founded by a fiercely intolerant monk in the thirteenth century, which made the Lotus Sutra the sole truth and later spawned a political party; and Zen, in the Rinzai school of Eisai (1141–1215) and the Sōtō school of Dōgen (1200–1253), which taught sitting meditation as enlightenment itself. Dōgen's Shōbōgenzō is one of the most demanding works of religious philosophy in any language, and Zen's cultural influence — on the tea ceremony, the garden, the Noh, the martial arts and the koan — is out of all proportion to its numbers.

The emptiness at the centre of the Mahayana teaching is easy to mistake for nihilism and is not: it says that things lack independent existence, not that they lack effect. Japanese religion in practice looks nothing like a doctrine. A family may have a Buddhist altar with ancestral tablets and a Shintō shelf with a charm from Ise; the wedding is Shintō, the funeral is Buddhist, the New Year visit is to a shrine and Christmas is a commercial festival. Surveys that count adherents produce totals larger than the population, which is the clearest evidence that the question is badly put.

Christianity is the exception that proves the rule about belonging. Francis Xavier landed in 1549 and the mission grew quickly among the southern daimyō; then persecution, the martyrdom of twenty-six Christians at Nagasaki in 1597, the Shimabara rebellion of 1637, and two centuries of underground practice in which the prayers survived as folk songs. When the country reopened, a hidden community came forward at Nagasaki and was found to have kept a corrupted, oral version of the faith. Christians are now about 1.5 per cent of the population, but the image of the church — the wedding chapel, the Christmas cake, the mission school — is woven into the culture far beyond that number.

Alongside the two institutional traditions sit the practices nobody calls religion: the matsuri that closes a street and carries a god through it, the festivals of the agricultural year, the grave-visiting of obon, the annual zodiac animal that greets the New Year on every postcard, and the fortune slips tied to temple railings. The state is secular and the society is not, in a way that no survey can measure.

The group, the company, the household

The basic social grammar is the distinction between uchi, inside, and soto, outside. Who is inside depends on context — the family, the department, the company, the school — and the behaviour changes accordingly: the language is more polite outside, the opinions more guarded, the gifts more carefully wrapped. Honne, the true feeling, and tatemae, the stated position, are not a licence to lie but a way of protecting the group's surface, and foreigners who mistake the surface for hypocrisy misread what is being protected.

The household, ie, was the legal unit until 1947: a corporate body headed by the father, with an heir, a family business, an ancestral altar and a grave. The postwar civil code replaced it with the nuclear family of equal spouses, but the obligations survived in practice, especially the care of elderly parents by the eldest son's wife — a role that now falls on a generation of women who also work, which is one of the quiet engines of the fertility collapse.

The company took over the functions the household had: belonging, identity, welfare, a hierarchy of seniority and a calendar of rituals. The classic model — hire a cohort of graduates, train them, pay by seniority, retire at 60, expect loyalty — was always more ideology than universal practice, and it was always male. It has been dismantled from the edges: non-regular employment, performance pay, foreign acquisition, and the 2019 work-style reform law that capped overtime, which followed a series of karoshi cases — deaths from overwork — and a culture in which leaving before the boss was a career risk. Planning in the Japanese firm is consensus-based, bottom-up (nemawashi, root-binding, before any decision), and slow by design; it produces excellent execution and poor pivots.

The social costs are now the country's main subject. Hikikomori, young adults who withdraw from society for years, are estimated at over a million. The gender gap is the widest in the G7 on the World Economic Forum's measure, with women underrepresented in management and politics and a large part of the gap explained by the division of household labour. Same-sex marriage is not recognised nationally, though a growing number of municipalities issue partnership certificates and courts have begun to rule the ban unconstitutional. The imperial household, with one male heir of his generation, is running the same demographic arithmetic as the rest of the country, and the debate about a female emperor is really a debate about what the institution is for.

Immigration is the policy the country keeps almost making. The foreign resident population passed 3.4 million, the government created a "specified skilled worker" visa in 2019, and convenience stores, care homes and construction sites now depend on Vietnamese, Chinese, Filipino and Brazilian workers. Public opinion is cautious, the bureaucracy is slow, and the language requirement is a real barrier. Given the birth rate, the alternative to immigration is a much smaller country, and the political system has not yet said which it prefers.

Rice, dashi, and the seasons

The meal is built on rice, and everything else is arranged around it: a soup, a main, two or three side dishes, pickles. The broth, dashi, is kombu seaweed and dried bonito flakes steeped in water at a temperature below boiling, and the savoury taste it carries — umami, the word coined by Kikunae Ikeda in 1908 when he isolated glutamate from kombu — is the backbone of the cuisine. Miso, fermented soy, and soy sauce add depth; mirin and sake add sweetness; the grill, the steamer, the pot and the raw knife do the rest. The knife is the signature tool: sashimi is a test of the blade and the cut, not of the fish alone.

Several of the dishes now regarded as ancient are recent. Sushi as it is eaten today — a hand-pressed ball of vinegared rice with a slice of fish — was a fast-food innovation of 1820s Edo, sold from stalls; before that, sushi was fermented fish with rice. Tempura came with the Portuguese in the sixteenth century, and its name may come from tempora, the Ember days. Ramen is Chinese in origin and twentieth-century in its Japanese form, with regional styles — tonkotsu in Kyushu, shōyu in Tokyo, miso in Sapporo — that are fiercely defended. Curry arrived with the British navy through the Indian subcontinent and became a school lunch. What the cuisine does have is a very old sense of season: the menu changes with the shun, the peak moment of each ingredient, and a kaiseki meal is an argument about time as much as taste.

Washoku, the traditional dietary culture, was inscribed by UNESCO in 2013 — not for a recipe but for a practice: rice-centred, seasonal, shared, and bound up with New Year and the festivals. Sake is brewed from rice with kōji mould, polished to remove the outer grain, and the polishing ratio is printed on the bottle; shōchū is distilled, cheap and regional. Tea came from China with the monks, and the powdered matcha of the tea ceremony coexists with the leaf sencha that most people actually drink, and with the bottled tea that fills every vending machine.

The year is a sequence of foods. New Year: mochi, osechi boxes, toshikoshi soba on the last night. Spring: hanami under the cherry trees, with sakura mochi. Summer: cold noodles, eel on the day of the ox, watermelon. Autumn: matsutake, persimmons, rice wine and the moon-viewing festival. Winter: nabe, hot pots, and the kotatsu table with a heater under it, which is the most comfortable piece of furniture ever designed. The estimate that the country is running out of people is easier to believe when you notice that the shops close on the day the last farmer in the district stops picking.

How to keep reading

Four books. Murasaki Shikibu's Tale of Genji, in Royall Tyler's translation, for the eleventh century. Bashō's Narrow Road to the Deep North, for the aesthetics of travel. Natsume Sōseki's Kokoro, for the Meiji transition seen from inside. And Edward Seidensticker's Low City, High City, for the disappearance of the old Tokyo — a book about demolition written with the affection of a resident.

For the record, the Tokyo National Museum, the Kyoto National Museum and the Miho Museum hold the objects; Nara's Shōsōin, opened once a year, holds the eighth-century imperial collection; and the Peace Memorial Museums at Hiroshima and Nagasaki should be visited by anyone who wants to understand the country's postwar settlement. The economy, the shrinking population and the security debate are best followed in the annual white papers, which are published in English and are more honest than the newspapers.

The four threads followed here meet in a sentence as they did for China: a state that borrowed a script and then wrote its own literature in it, a language whose politeness is grammar, an art that prizes the unfinished and the worn, and a household that absorbed the company and is now watching the company fail. The difference from China is the sequence: Japan's borrowings were deliberate and fast, its modernisation was a decision rather than a collapse, and its decline — if that is the word — is happening in peace, in a rich country, with a very good train service. That is not a small thing, and it is not a happy one.
