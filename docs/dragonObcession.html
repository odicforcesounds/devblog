The Scale and The Laugh

1

The Dragon polished his first scale before sunrise.
The Monkey woke with a leaf stuck to his face and called it a mask.

One said: “If it is not perfect, it is not done.”
One said: “If it is done, let us eat.”

And the day began, as days do, between finishing and beginning.

2

The Dragon counted his teeth. One hundred and eight. If one was crooked, the river would notice.

The Monkey counted his bruises. Seven. Each with a story better than the last fall.

“You fall because you do not look.”
“I look at clouds. Falling is how ground says hello.”

The Dragon was angry for half a breath. The Monkey laughed for a whole one.

3

Perfection is a high mountain with clean snow.
Play is a low river with muddy feet.

The mountain says: come up, be pure.
The river says: come in, be cool.

The Dragon lived on the mountain and was thirsty.
The Monkey lived in the river and sometimes missed the view.

Each looked at the other and felt a thirst and a coolness.

4

The Dragon built a palace of straight lines. Every tile faced east. Every bell rang on time.

The Monkey entered and hung upside down from the bell. “Now it rings funny.”

“You break order.”
“I make order sing.”

They did not fight. The bell kept ringing, a little off, a little alive. The Dragon listened longer than he meant to.

5

The cup that is full cannot receive tea.

The Dragon’s cup was carved jade, full of excellent tea, no room for more.
The Monkey’s cup was cracked, leaking, always half-empty.

“Pour yours into mine, it leaks out, we both drink the floor.”
“Wasteful.”
“The floor is thirsty too.”

That night it rained. The floor drank. Both slept to the sound.

6

The Dragon practiced his fire until it was blue and silent. No smoke. No smell. Pure.

The Monkey burned his rice. Smoke everywhere. Smell of almost.

Villagers came to the smoke, not to the blue flame. They brought bowls. They laughed. They stayed.

The Dragon watched from above, perfect and alone. “Why do they go to the mistake?”
“Because mistake smells like hunger, and hunger is honest.”

The Dragon let one wisp of smoke escape his next flame, on purpose, to see. It curled like a question.

7

To hold tightly is to lose the bird.
To open the hand is to keep the sky.

The Dragon held his plans tightly: sunrise at six, rain at eight, wind from the west.

Wind came from the east, laughing. Rain came at nine, uncombed. Sunrise, as always, came without asking.

The Monkey opened his hand: an ant walked across. “See? I hold a world and it does not know.”

The Dragon loosened one claw. The wind thanked him by ruffling his whiskers out of place. He almost smiled.

8

They tried to become each other.

The Dragon tried to be careless. He threw a pearl into mud to prove detachment. Then stayed up all night wondering if the pearl was cold.

The Monkey tried to be perfect. He stacked stones straight for an hour. Then sneezed. Stones fell. He laughed until stones laughed.

“I cannot be you.”
“Good. I am taken. Be you who has napped.”
“I cannot be you.”
“Good. I am taken. Be me who has burned rice once.”

9

The bow that is always bent breaks.
The string that is always loose never sings.

The Dragon was bent bow. Even sleeping, one eye counted stars for correctness.

The Monkey was loose string. Even awake, he hummed off-key.

One evening they tuned each other: Dragon taught Monkey to aim — breathe, hold, release — and the pebble hit the bell dead center. Monkey taught Dragon to miss — breathe, laugh, release — and the second pebble hit the pond and made the moon wobble. Both sounds were music.

10

The Dragon wanted to carve his name in stone so time would remember.

The Monkey wrote his name in water so time would laugh.

“Water forgets.”
“Stone remembers too hard. It gets heavy. Look, moss covers your grandfather’s name and moss is soft. Maybe forgetting is a blanket.”

The Dragon carved smaller. Just one scale-shape. The Monkey watched until ripples settled. Both left a mark: one that lasts, one that lets go.

11

When the bell is perfect, no one hears it. When it cracks, everyone turns.

A crack came to the palace bell — thin as hair. The Dragon wanted to melt it anew.

The Monkey put his ear to the crack. “Listen, it hums two notes now.”

They kept the cracked bell. Travelers asked about the double tone. The Dragon said, “It broke beautifully.” It was the first imperfect sentence he had ever loved.

12

The Dragon meditated on still water to see his face clearly.

The Monkey threw a pebble in. Face shattered into light.

“Angry. Now I see nothing.”
“Now you see everything moving. Still face is mask. Broken face is dance.”

They sat until water calmed. Face returned, a little wetter, a little kinder. The Dragon bowed to the pebble. The Monkey bowed to the stillness. Bows met in the middle.

13

The more laws, the more thieves.
The more polish, the more dust is seen.

The Dragon made a rule for clouds: be white, be on time.

Clouds came gray and late, gorgeous. The Dragon fumed. The Monkey napped under gray and woke refreshed.

“Your rule makes rebellion. My nap makes weather.”
“Then what should I govern?”
“Govern your grip.”

That day the Dragon governed nothing but his own claw, opening and closing, opening. Clouds, unsupervised, put on a show of dragons and monkeys.

14

They argued about the moon.

“It should be round. Round is complete.”
“It is sometimes a nail clipping. Still moon. Still pulls sea.”

“Incomplete is failure.”
“Incomplete is breathing. Full moon cannot grow. Half moon is becoming.”

They watched the nail-clipping moon pull the whole sea. The Dragon was quiet a long time. Then: “Teach me to love the half.”
“Bite it. Like cheese.” And they shared the night like cheese, with holes.

15

The sage does by not doing.
The Dragon did by doing double. Double fire, double patrol, double shine.

Exhaustion came wearing his own face.

The Monkey did by napping in the path. Travelers stepped around, slowed down, noticed flowers. The path became famous for flowers no one planted.

“You achieve without trying. Unfair.”
“I try without grasping. Try it. Grasp water.”

The Dragon tried to grasp water. Water left. He cupped water. Water stayed. He stared at his cupped hands like a lesson.

16

The Dragon feared death: rust on scales, dim in eyes, name mossed.

The Monkey feared nothing, which worried the Dragon more than death.

“How will you be remembered?”
“As the laugh after the story. Laughs don’t need statues.”

They planted a tree together — crooked seed in straight hole. It will outlive polish and play. That was enough. That was everything.

17

Sharp tools dull fast. Soft water cuts stone.

The Dragon sharpened his judgments daily. Dull by noon from overuse.

The Monkey dripped jokes on hard days. Stone grew a hollow where laughter sat.

“Give me your softness.”
“Give me your edge, I dull it on oranges.”

Exchange made: one scale for one laugh-line. Both wore the other’s gift badly and proudly.

18

They sat back to back under the crooked tree.

No polishing. No falling. Just breath in, breath out — the oldest palace with no tiles.

Thoughts came like clouds: gray, late, gorgeous. They let them pass without governing.

A leaf fell on Dragon’s nose, off-center. He did not fix it. A moment of silence fell on Monkey’s tongue. He did not fill it.

Perfection rested. Play rested. Both, at last, present.

19

To become what the other is, without being —

The Dragon will never be Monkey. His joy will always arrive after order, like dessert after rice. Good. Dessert is sweet because rice was plain.

The Monkey will never be Dragon. His order will always arrive inside play, like seed inside fruit. Good. Fruit is eaten because seed hides.

So they bow, confused and clear:

“Your mess teaches my shine to breathe.”
“Your shine teaches my mess to see.”

Anger sometimes. Laughter often. Becoming always, without leaving.

20

The way that can be perfected is not the endless way.
The laugh that can be repeated is not the living laugh.

So leave one tile crooked. Leave one bell cracked. Leave one flame smoky. Leave one stone unstacked.

And leave one moment — this one — exactly as it is: Dragon breathing, Monkey dozing, tree crooked, moon half, tea leaking onto thirsty floor.

Enough. And therefore complete.
