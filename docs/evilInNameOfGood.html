Evil in the Name of Good

Doing bad in the name of the Good: how governments used soldiers and police, for centuries, under orders made not to be questioned.

1. The pattern

Almost every century repeats the same sentence: the cause is Good, so the act must be permitted. God, nation, order, security, revolution, peace. Under those words, ordinary men burned villages, tortured prisoners, beat crowds, deported families and silenced witnesses.

The excuse is clean. The act is dirty. That gap is the subject of this page.

I was only following orders has been said in every language that ever had an army or a police force.

2. Orders made not to be questioned

For centuries, disobedience was punished harder than cruelty. A soldier who refused could be flogged, jailed or shot. A policeman who refused could lose pay, rank and future. The system selected for obedience.

Drill taught the body to move before the mind asked why. Secrecy hid the full chain. Paperwork turned killing into procedure, transfer, pacification, cleansing.

To question was treason. To obey was duty. That is how bad became routine.

3. Soldiers through history

Crusades, colonial wars, world wars, counter-insurgencies. Young men far from home, told the enemy is less than human, told the order comes from above, told history will thank them.

Afterwards came the same sentence: we did what we were told. Nuremberg answered it once, clearly: following orders does not erase responsibility. Yet armies still teach obedience first and conscience second.

4. Police through history

Police did it closer to home: beatings in cells, false arrests, planted evidence, charges against crowds, protection money, silence about colleagues. The order here is rarely written. It is a nod, a radio code, a quota, a deal with it.

Because police write the report, they also write the story. That is why every serious police dossier returns to the same demands: visible identification, written records, independent complaints, cameras that cannot be switched off.

5. Governments and criminal activity

A large part of criminal activity in history was not done by outlaws. It was done by salaried men with stamps, files and medals: deportations, forced disappearances, death squads, torture programs, mass surveillance, aggressive wars sold as defence.

Prison guards, border guards, intelligence services and private militias used the same defence: I do not make policy, I apply it.

6. Today the abuse is less common

Courts, a free press where it still exists, phones with cameras and human-rights law made open abuse harder. A soldier or policeman today knows someone may be filming. That matters.

But the structure survived in softer forms: strikes decided from screens, mass data collection, pushbacks at borders, administrative detention, automated punishment where no human signs the order. Less blood in the street, same absence of questions in the office.

7. How to refuse a criminal order

An order is not a blank cheque. Ask four times: is it legal, is it necessary, is it proportionate, would you accept it done to you. If it fails, ask for it in writing, keep a copy, tell someone outside the chain.

Never hand your conscience to someone else to hold.
