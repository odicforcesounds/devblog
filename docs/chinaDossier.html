Dossier · the long country

China: A Dossier on a Civilisation

A country of 1.4 billion people that has been a single state for two thousand two hundred years, that writes in a script older than its emperors, and that has been invaded, split, starved and rebuilt more often than the word "continuous" suggests. This dossier follows four threads through it — the state, the language, the image and the household — from the Neolithic to the present, and names what each thread costs.

One name, many countries

The name the country uses for itself is Zhongguo, the "central states" or "middle kingdom", a phrase older than the empire: it first appears on a bronze vessel in the eleventh century BCE, where it means the royal domain among the other domains, not a nation with borders. That is the first thing to understand about China — the word has always been relational. You are central relative to someone. The edge moves.

The territory is 9.6 million square kilometres, the fourth largest after Russia, Canada and the United States, and it holds almost every climate a farmer can want: the loess plateau in the north where millet was domesticated, the Yangtze delta in the south where rice was, the Tibetan plateau at four thousand metres, the deserts of Xinjiang, the tropical coast of Hainan. Two rivers organise the story. The Yellow River carries so much silt that its bed rises above the fields beside it, which is why it has changed course dramatically more than twenty times in recorded history and why the Chinese state has always been, at bottom, a water authority. The Yangtze is the other half: navigable, slower, feeding a delta that produces a large share of the country's grain and almost all of its river commerce.

Politically the map is one state with thirty-four provincial-level units — twenty-three provinces, five autonomous regions, four municipalities (Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, Chongqing) and two special administrative regions, Hong Kong and Macau. The island of Taiwan is governed separately and its status is disputed; Beijing claims it as a province, and that claim structures the country's foreign policy. Administrative unity, however, is not cultural unity: the state recognises fifty-six ethnic groups, of which the Han are about 91 per cent. The largest minority nations — Zhuang, Hui, Manchu, Uyghur, Miao, Yi, Tujia, Tibetan, Mongol — each have their own languages, and several have their own scripts. A dossier that treats China as one culture is already wrong at the second sentence.

Nor is the geography kind to centralisation. The single time zone (UTC+8) spans what should be five; in Xinjiang, offices keep Beijing time while markets keep local sun time, and the gap between them is a daily reminder that the state's clock is a decision. Distance, dialect and terrain have always pulled the other way, and the history below is largely the record of which pull won in each century.

Before the emperors

Hominins were in the region early: the Yuanmou teeth are around 1.7 million years old, and the Peking Man fossils from Zhoukoudian, near Beijing, are roughly 700,000 to 400,000 years old. These are not ancestors of modern Chinese people in any simple sense — the population history of East Asia is one of repeated mixture and movement — but they are the deep layer that any claim of antiquity rests on.

The Neolithic is where the two great agricultural systems appear. In the Yellow River basin, Yangshao culture (roughly 5000–3000 BCE) grew millet, kept pigs and dogs, painted swirling red and black designs on pottery, and buried its dead in cemeteries that show the beginnings of unequal households. In the Yangtze basin, rice was domesticated by around 7000 BCE at sites such as Kuahuqiao and Hemudu, with paddies, wooden oars and piles of fish bones. By 3000 BCE the south had something the north did not: Liangzhu, a walled city near modern Hangzhou with earthen ramparts, a hydraulic system of dams and canals, and a jade industry of astonishing skill. Its cong — square tubes with a round bore — and bi discs are the first objects in Chinese art made for no practical purpose at all.

Then comes the part the textbooks once smoothed over. The Xia dynasty, traditionally 2070–1600 BCE, is still not attested in contemporary writing. What exists is a site: Erlitou, in Henan, with a palace complex, bronze foundries and a grid of roads, dated to the right window and plausibly the centre of a state. Whether it was the Xia of later legend is a live argument among archaeologists, and it is a good example of how Chinese prehistory is written — from the ground up in one direction and from the classics down in the other, with a gap in the middle that no excavation has closed.

The Shang (c. 1600–1046 BCE) closed it, because the Shang wrote. At Anyang, the last Shang capital, archaeologists have recovered more than a hundred thousand fragments of turtle shell and ox bone inscribed with questions put to ancestors and to a high god: will the harvest hold, will the king's toothache pass, will the campaign against the Qiang succeed, is the child the queen carries a boy. The answers are read in the cracks made by a hot bronze rod. This is the ancestor of every Chinese character — and it is the oldest readable writing in East Asia, from about 1250 BCE.

That date is worth holding next to a common confusion. Writing comes first, and by a wide margin. The boxing called taijiquan — the "fist of the Great Ultimate" — is documented only from the seventeenth century, in the Chen village lineage that credits Chen Wangting (1580–1660); the attribution to the Daoist immortal Zhang Sanfeng is a later story with no contemporary evidence. The philosophical phrase taiji, the Great Ultimate, is older: it belongs to the cosmological commentaries gathered around the Book of Changes, around the third century BCE. But the art that carries the name is early modern. So at 2000 BCE the region had cities, jade, silk, bronze and no writing; writing arrives about 750 years later, and the slow boxing about 2,800 years after that.

Shang bronze is the other great survivor. Vessels for wine and grain, cast by pouring molten bronze into ceramic piece-moulds — a technique that allows decoration in relief and gives Chinese bronzes their hard, incised look — were the ritual technology of the state. The ding, a three-legged cauldron, became the emblem of legitimate rule; a later story says that when the Zhou took power, they asked the weight of the Shang's cauldrons, and the answer they got was a lesson in how virtue, not metal, keeps a dynasty.

The Zhou and the invention of argument

The Zhou, a western marchland people, defeated the Shang around 1046 BCE and then had to explain why. Their answer lasted three thousand years: the Mandate of Heaven. Heaven does not choose a family; it chooses a ruler who governs well, and withdraws the mandate when he does not. Rebellion becomes a sign of misrule rather than a crime, which is a remarkable thing for a dynasty to put in writing, and it set the terms of Chinese political argument: the question was never whether there should be an emperor, but whether this one still deserved the job.

When the Zhou court lost real power in 771 BCE, the country broke into the Spring and Autumn period and then the Warring States — five and a half centuries of interstate competition in which the rulers of a dozen states needed administrators, strategists and legitimating theories. What they got was the most productive argument in Chinese intellectual history, the Hundred Schools.

Confucius (551–479 BCE) was a minor official from Lu who taught that the disorder of his time came from the decay of ritual and of the obligations between people. His answer was not law but character: the junzi, the noble person, who governs by example; ren, humaneness, the capacity to treat the other as one would wish to be treated; li, ritual propriety, the grammar of respect that makes a society legible. He claimed no revelation, wrote nothing himself, and said he was transmitting rather than inventing. The Analects, compiled by students, is a book of fragments, which is why it can be quoted to support almost any later position.

Around him, and against him, the others:

  • Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE) argued for impartial concern for all, against Confucian graded love of family, and for a pragmatic test of any doctrine: does it enrich the state and feed the people? He also wrote the earliest Chinese treatises on defensive warfare and on optics.
  • Mencius (c. 372–289 BCE) held that human nature is good, and that a ruler who starves his people forfeits the mandate and may be removed. Xunzi (c. 310–235 BCE), a generation later, held that human nature is bad and requires the shaping of ritual and teachers — the same institutions, the opposite anthropology, and both called Confucian.
  • Laozi and Zhuangzi, the Daoist line, doubted that government improves anything: the best ruler is barely noticed, and the sage prefers the uncarved block to the finished instrument.
  • Shang Yang (d. 338 BCE) and Han Feizi (d. 233 BCE), the Legalists, held that people respond to reward and punishment, that law must be public and uniform, and that the ruler should trust no minister. Their programme built the state that won.
  • Sunzi's Art of War, a manual of the fifth century BCE, argued that the supreme skill is to win without fighting and that all warfare is deception — the most translated Chinese book after the classics.
  • The Yin-Yang and Five Phases school reduced the world to two forces and five transformations (wood, fire, earth, metal, water), a scheme that would later organise medicine, music, dynastic legitimacy and cooking.

This is the period to keep in mind whenever someone says Chinese thought is "harmony". It is a period of fierce, technical, sometimes cynical argument about how to run a state, conducted by men who travelled from court to court looking for employment. The synthesis that later looks seamless — Confucian ethics, Legalist administration, Daoist cosmology — was assembled afterwards, by victors.

The machine that lasted two thousand years

In 221 BCE the state of Qin, having reformed its army and its taxation along Legalist lines, destroyed the last of its rivals and declared a single empire. Its king took a new title, Huangdi, "August Thearch", and is remembered as Qin Shi Huang. What he did in eleven years set the template: he standardised weights, measures, coinage, the axle width of carts and the writing system; he abolished the old feudal nobility and divided the realm into commanderies run by appointed officials; he conscripted labour for roads, canals and the northern walls that later generations would call the Great Wall; and he buried the alternative literatures, ordering in 213 BCE the burning of books outside the official and technical collections. His tomb, guarded by the terracotta army discovered in 1974, is a model of the cosmos with mercury rivers, according to Sima Qian, still unexcavated.

Qin lasted fifteen years. Its replacement, the Han (206 BCE–220 CE), kept the administration and dropped the cruelty, and in doing so invented the pattern that lasted: a Legalist bureaucracy wearing a Confucian face. Han Wudi (r. 141–87 BCE) made the Confucian classics the curriculum of office, sent Zhang Qian west in 138 BCE on the missions that opened the Silk Road, and expanded south and into Korea. Under the Han, Sima Qian wrote the Records of the Grand Historian, the first universal history in Chinese and the model for every dynastic history after it; Cai Lun is credited in 105 CE with the improvement of paper; and Buddhism arrived from Central Asia along the trade routes in the first century CE.

Then the machine stopped, for four centuries. The Han fell in 220 into the Three Kingdoms, and the country was divided — briefly reunified by the Jin, then split between north and south for nearly three hundred years, with non-Han dynasties ruling the north and a sequence of short-lived southern courts keeping the literary tradition alive. It is the period when Buddhism took root, when the cave temples at Dunhuang, Yungang and Longmen were carved, and when the aristocratic families that would dominate the Tang began to form. Reunification came in 581 with the Sui, who dug the Grand Canal — the north-south waterway that made the grain of the south feed the armies of the north, and still does.

The Tang (618–907) is the dynasty the Chinese themselves treat as the high point, and the reasons are visible in the poetry. Chang'an, the capital, was a walled grid of 108 wards with a population of about a million, the largest city on earth, with Zoroastrian, Manichaean, Nestorian Christian and Muslim communities, Japanese and Korean students at the academy, and a cosmopolitan elite that drank grape wine from the western regions. Li Bai wrote drunk and unemployable; Du Fu wrote the An Lushan rebellion from inside it, including the conscription of villagers; Wang Wei wrote landscape poems that read like paintings. In 845 the Tang state turned on the Buddhists, confiscating monasteries and returning their slaves to the tax rolls — the largest of four persecutions, and the moment Chinese Buddhism lost its economic base and became a religion of the gentry and the countryside.

The Song (960–1279) is the other high point, and a more modern one. Excluded from the northern pasture by the Khitan and later the Jurchen, it compensated with commerce: a money economy, paper money in Sichuan from around 1024, a merchant marine with the magnetic compass, and an iron industry that produced more metal than Europe would for centuries. Printing — woodblock, and movable type in clay by Bi Sheng around 1040 — made books cheap enough to create a reading public. The examination system expanded until the gentry class was defined by it. And Neo-Confucianism, above all in Zhu Xi's (1130–1200) synthesis, turned the classical tradition into a metaphysics: li, principle, in all things; qi, the stuff they are made of; the investigation of things as the road to moral clarity. It became orthodoxy for the next seven hundred years.

The Yuan (1271–1368) put Mongols on the throne and China inside a Eurasian empire; Kublai Khan's court received Marco Polo, or at least the story of him. The Ming (1368–1644) restored a native dynasty, moved the capital to Beijing, built the Forbidden City between 1406 and 1420, and sent Zheng He's fleets as far as East Africa between 1405 and 1433 — seven voyages with ships far larger than anything in Europe at the time, abandoned when the court decided the treasury could better spend the money on the northern frontier. The Qing (1644–1912), Manchu conquerors who kept the civil service and the exams and added a queue and a shaved forehead, doubled the territory, and presided over the longest peace in Chinese history — and then over the collapse.

Buddhism, canals, and the commercial turn

It is worth pausing on how Buddhism became Chinese, because it is the best case study of the country's relation to foreign ideas. It arrived as a foreign religion with a foreign script, a monastic rule that withdrew men from the family, and a doctrine of rebirth that sat badly with ancestor worship. Within four centuries it had a Chinese canon, Chinese schools and Chinese monasteries that owned land, ran mills and lent money. Kumarajiva's translations in the early fifth century made the sutras readable; Xuanzang's pilgrimage to India between 629 and 645 brought back hundreds of texts and produced the Journey to the West's historical core.

The schools that mattered: Tiantai, which ranked all the sutras into one system; Huayan, whose image of reality is a net of jewels each reflecting all the others; Pure Land, which made salvation available to anyone who called the name of Amitabha with a sincere heart — the practice of the ordinary believer, and the reason Buddhism survived persecution; and Chan, which taught that enlightenment is not a gradual accumulation but a direct seeing, transmitted outside the scriptures, and which produced the strange, funny, violent encounter dialogues that later became the koans of Zen.

The printing of the Diamond Sutra in 868, recovered from the Dunhuang library cave and now the earliest securely dated printed book in the world, is a Buddhist object before it is a technological one: it was commissioned "for free universal distribution" by a man named Wang Jie, to earn merit for his parents. That is the shape of the commercial turn in miniature — a technology developed by a religion for the sake of merit, and then used by the state for examination cribs and by merchants for almanacs, and finally by everyone for everything.

The Dunhuang caves, painted and sculpted over a thousand years and sealed around the eleventh century, held tens of thousands of manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Uyghur, Sogdian and Sanskrit — a library of a Silk Road oasis, and the single richest archive of medieval Central Asian life. Its dispersal, after Aurel Stein's visits in 1907 and 1914, is one of the scandals of early twentieth-century archaeology and one of the reasons Chinese museums now restrict foreign excavation.

The long fall

The nineteenth century is where the "continuous civilisation" narrative breaks. The Qing state was solvent and its army large, but the Opium War of 1839–42 exposed the gap: British steamers moved against the wind, the emperor's commissioners could not tell a frigate from a transport, and the Treaty of Nanking ceded Hong Kong, opened five ports, and fixed a tariff the Chinese government could not change. The second war, 1856–60, ended with the destruction of the Summer Palace.

Internally it was worse. The Taiping Rebellion (1850–64), a movement led by a man who believed himself the younger brother of Jesus, took Nanjing and held it for eleven years; the death toll is usually estimated in the tens of millions, making it the deadliest civil war in history. The Self-Strengthening Movement that followed tried to import Western guns and factories while keeping the Confucian order — "Chinese learning for the essence, Western learning for use" — and failed the test in 1895, when Japan, which had reformed its institutions rather than its arsenals, destroyed the Beiyang Fleet. The Hundred Days' Reform of 1898 lasted 103 days. The Boxer rising of 1899–1901 brought eight foreign armies into Beijing and an indemnity that bankrupted the treasury.

The dynasty fell in 1912, and the republic that replaced it was weaker than the empire. The May Fourth Movement of 1919 — students protesting the award of German concessions in Shandong to Japan at Versailles — became a cultural insurgency: the New Culture movement attacked the family, the classics and the written language itself. Lu Xun's "Diary of a Madman" (1918) read the Confucian tradition as a record of cannibalism; Hu Shi and Chen Duxiu argued that the classical written language had to be replaced by the vernacular if anyone was to be educated at all. They won. Within a decade, schoolbooks, newspapers and novels were in baihua, the spoken grammar written down — a change in the Chinese book as total as the change from Latin to Italian in Europe.

Then came the wars. The Nationalists under Chiang Kai-shek unified the country partially in 1928 and governed a modernising coastal strip for a decade while fighting the Communists in the countryside. Japan invaded in 1937; the Nanjing massacre that December killed an estimated 200,000 to 300,000 people, and the war that ended in 1945 killed millions more. The civil war resumed immediately, and in 1949 the Communists took the mainland and the Nationalists withdrew to Taiwan.

Revolution and reform

The People's Republic began with land reform — the redistribution of landlord property to peasants, and in the process the execution of perhaps a million landlords — and with the consolidation of a party-state that reached into every village. The First Five-Year Plan copied Soviet heavy industry. Then, in 1958, Mao Zedong launched the Great Leap Forward: backyard steel furnaces, communal kitchens, and agricultural targets set by cadre ambition rather than by soil. The result was the largest famine in history, with deaths estimated between 15 and 45 million depending on the method, and a political system that reported success while people starved.

The Cultural Revolution (1966–76) was the second catastrophe: schools closed, temples and libraries were destroyed, intellectuals were beaten and sent to the countryside, and factions fought with weapons taken from the army. Its own logic — that revolution must be continuous, that expertise is a form of privilege — is essential to understanding why the reforms that followed were framed as pragmatism above all. When Mao died in 1976 his successors arrested the Gang of Four and, in December 1978, at the Third Plenum, Deng Xiaoping turned the party's priority from class struggle to economic construction.

The reform sequence is worth knowing in order, because it explains the country's shape. Agriculture was decollectivised first (household responsibility, from 1978), which raised output fast. Special economic zones — Shenzhen above all, a fishing town turned city of 17 million — were opened to foreign investment in 1980, with tax terms and labour rules that did not apply elsewhere. Township enterprises absorbed rural labour; then, from the 1990s, the state sold off much of its housing stock, closed thousands of state enterprises, and let the surviving ones list abroad. China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, and the export machine that followed pulled 300 million people out of poverty by the World Bank's measure — the largest such reduction ever recorded, and the strongest argument the system has.

The costs were structural and are now due. The one-child policy (1980–2015) held down the birth rate so effectively that the population began to fall in 2022, for the first time since the famine, and the old-age dependency ratio is climbing faster than in any country that grew rich first. The hukou household registration system, which ties schooling and health care to the place of birth, created a class of migrant workers — nearly 300 million people — who build cities they cannot legally settle in. Property development, which at its peak accounted for around a quarter of GDP, is deleveraging painfully. And the political system has become more centralised, not less: the abolition of presidential term limits in 2018 and the consolidation of Xi Jinping's position have reversed the collective leadership that the post-Mao settlement was built on.

This is the honest summary of the last forty years: an extraordinary material transformation, delivered by a state that is competent at infrastructure and mobilisation, expensive in environmental and demographic terms, and unwilling to let the political question be asked. Whether the second half of the century repeats the first is the open question of the dossier, and no one — inside or outside — knows the answer.

One script, a family of languages

The most consequential fact about Chinese is that its speakers do not share a language. They share a writing system. The Sinitic family includes Mandarin (about 70 per cent of Han speakers), Wu (Shanghai and the lower Yangtze, roughly 80 million), Min (Fujian and Taiwan, roughly 70 million across its branches), Yue (Cantonese, roughly 60 million), Hakka, Gan, Xiang and Jin, and the branches are not mutually intelligible — a Cantonese speaker and a Mandarin speaker cannot hold a conversation, though they can read the same newspaper, because the characters carry meaning rather than sound.

That is the design. A character encodes a word, not a pronunciation, so 山 is "mountain" in Beijing, in Guangzhou and in Tokyo, with three different sounds. The price is that the script must be learned character by character, that it is enormous, and that it preserves a written language that no one speaks: for two thousand years the elite wrote wenyan, a compressed, allusive classical grammar, while speaking something quite different. The vernacular revolution of 1919 was therefore not a stylistic choice but a democratisation.

The grammar is simpler than European grammars in some ways and stranger in others. There is no inflection: no tense, no plural, no case, no gender. Time is carried by particles and adverbs — le for a completed action, guo for experience, zhe for duration. Nouns need measure words: you cannot say "three books", you say "three ben books", and the choice of classifier encodes shape and kind. Word order is subject–verb–object, but the topic can come first and the sentence is often organised around what is being talked about rather than who is doing it: "this book I have read". Mandarin has four tones plus a neutral tone, and the tones are lexical — ma can be mother, hemp, horse or to scold — which is why romanisation needs diacritics and why singing in Mandarin requires care.

The modern standard, Putonghua, was defined in 1955 on the phonology of Beijing with a vocabulary drawn from the northern dialects and grammar from the modern vernacular; it is the language of school, television and the army, and for hundreds of millions of people it is a second language learned at school. The 1956 simplification reduced the stroke counts of about two thousand common characters and replaced several with simpler forms; pinyin, the romanisation with tone marks, was promulgated in 1958 and became the standard input method, so that Chinese is now typed by spelling it. Traditional characters remain in Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan.

Literacy figures are worth treating carefully: the official rate for adults is above 96 per cent, measured by a standard of around 1,500 characters for rural and 2,000 for urban readers, which is enough for a newspaper but not for a classical text. The full standard table issued in 2013 lists 8,105 characters; the largest historical dictionary, the Kangxi of 1716, has 47,035. A university-educated reader knows perhaps five to six thousand.

The character and the brush

Chinese characters are built from a small inventory of strokes arranged in a fixed order, and from a set of recurring components. The traditional analysis, the six writings, distinguishes pictographs (a stylised sun, a tree), simple indicators (a stroke above a line meaning "above"), compound ideographs (sun plus moon = bright), and — overwhelmingly — phono-semantic compounds, where one half hints at meaning and the other at sound. More than ninety per cent of characters are of that last kind, which means the script is not a picture-language; it is a phonetic system with a semantic filing cabinet attached.

They are grouped under 214 radicals, the indexing keys of every dictionary since the eighteenth century. Learning to write means learning stroke order, proportion and, above all, the standard script — kaishu — in which each stroke is separate and legible. The other four scripts are historical styles: seal script, a rounded archaic form still used on seals; clerical script, the Han bureaucracy's hand; running script, the everyday cursive-connected hand; and cursive, which can be illegible even to a native reader and is the form in which calligraphy becomes an art of speed.

Calligraphy is the senior art of China, above painting and far above sculpture, for a reason that is theological as much as aesthetic: the brushstroke records the movement of the body and the quality of the mind that moved it. A person's character is said to be visible in the hand. The tools are four — brush, ink stick, paper, inkstone — and the practice is repetition: copybooks of the masters, thousand-character passages, the same stroke for years. Wang Xizhi (303–361), whose Preface to the Orchid Pavilion survives only in copies, is the canonical figure; the fact that the greatest work exists as traced copies tells you what the tradition values. It values the trace.

Because the state recruited by examination and the examination was written, the hand was also a career. A candidate whose characters were clumsy lost marks however good his essay; the palace examination was graded partly on presentation. The result was a society in which literacy was a ladder and a script was a uniform, and in which the same brush wrote the poem, the memorial to the throne, the shop sign and the letter of condolence.

Landscape as a moral form

Chinese painting is not a history of pictures of things. It is a history of a relationship between the painter, the world and the paper, codified in the sixth century by Xie He in six principles, the first of which — qiyun shengdong, usually rendered "spirit resonance, life movement" — has been argued over for fifteen hundred years because it names something that cannot be taught: the painting must have the pulse of the thing in it.

The materials shape the result. Ink on silk or paper, a brush that can go from a hair's width to a soaked mass in one gesture, no ability to correct without the correction showing. There is no fixed viewpoint and no vanishing point: a handscroll unrolls, and the eye travels up a mountain path and down a river as if walking. Empty space is not background; it is mist, water, distance and the place where the viewer's attention is invited to rest. Colours are few — ink, mineral blue and green for the archaic style, ochre, cinnabar — and the inscription and the red seal are part of the composition, not labels added afterwards.

The periods have distinct temperaments. Northern Song monumental landscape (Fan Kuan, Guo Xi) puts a tiny human in an overwhelming mountain, and the message is cosmological. Southern Song academy painting (Ma Yuan, Xia Gui) crops to a corner and leaves the rest empty, and the message is poetic. The Yuan literati, painting under Mongol rule, turned their backs on professional finish: Ni Zan's sparse trees and empty pavilions are a statement about refusing to serve. The Ming revival (Shen Zhou, Wen Zhengming) made the amateur ideal official, and Dong Qichang's theory of a Northern and Southern school of painting gave the literati a lineage. The Qing individualists — Shitao, Bada Shanren — painted with a freedom that looks, to a modern eye, like expressionism three centuries early.

What the tradition does not have is the European hierarchy that puts history painting first. The highest subject is landscape, because landscape is where the scholar retires from office and finds the order that politics cannot give him. A mountain is a moral position.

Bronze, jade, porcelain

Three materials carry most of Chinese art history, and each was first a ritual object. Bronze vessels held wine and grain for the ancestors; their decoration — the squared spiral ground, the taotie mask with its paired eyes — was cast, not carved, by assembling a ceramic mould around a model, which allowed the ornament to stand in relief and gave Shang and Zhou bronzes their weight of surface. The ding cauldron became the emblem of rule, and the phrase "to ask the weight of the cauldrons" still means to covet the throne.

Jade was older and more sacred. The Liangzhu bi discs and cong tubes, made around 3000 BCE from nephrite worked with abrasive sand and bamboo, were placed in graves in quantities that imply a cosmology: round for heaven, square for earth. Jade was credited with moral properties — warmth, purity, a sound that carries — and the Han dressed their dead in suits of jade plaques sewn with gold wire. It is still the stone of preference for a gift that must not say what it means.

Porcelain is the material that went global. Stoneware and proto-porcelain existed by the Han; true high-fired porcelain, white-bodied and translucent, by the Tang. The Song connoisseurs ranked five kilns — Ru, Jun, Guan, Ge and Ding — for glazes that look like water, sky and crackled ice, and the restraint of a Ru dish is the closest Chinese art comes to minimalism. Jingdezhen in Jiangxi became the imperial kiln centre in the fourteenth century, and under the Yuan the potters learned to paint cobalt blue under the glaze; blue-and-white was made for export to the Middle East, where the cobalt came from, and came back to China as a national style. Ming and Qing export porcelain — kraak ware, famille verte, armorial services — filled Dutch and Portuguese warehouses, and the word "china" in English still means the stuff.

Around these three, the rest of the material culture: silk, reeled from cocoons and woven into the fabric that gave the Silk Road its name and served for centuries as a currency and a tax; lacquer, tapped from the tree and built up in dozens of layers to make boxes, trays and armour; cloisonné, the Byzantine technique naturalised in the Ming; and hardwood furniture, whose Ming lines — spare, joinery-only, no metal — are the one Chinese design idiom that modern industrial designers keep returning to.

Timber, courtyard, wall

Chinese building is a timber system, and the system is modular. A building is a number of bays between columns, repeated on a platform, roofed with tiles on a frame of purlins and rafters, with the weight carried by posts and beams rather than by walls. The bracket set, dougong, projects the eaves and absorbs movement; it also became, in imperial building, a mark of rank — the more tiers, the grander the building. Walls are infill, which is why a Chinese building can be opened entirely to the garden and why fires and wars could remove whole cities while leaving their stone platforms. The Yingzao Fashi, a building manual of 1103, codified proportions for the state workshops and is the reason we can reconstruct the system at all.

The city is axial and walled. Beijing's layout, fixed under the Ming, runs a north–south axis from the bell and drum towers through the palaces of the Forbidden City to the Tiananmen gate, with the altar of heaven to the east and of earth to the west. The unit of ordinary life is the courtyard house, the siheyuan: rooms around a paved court, closed to the street and open to the sky, with the elder generation to the north and the servants to the south. The same plan, denser and taller, produced the lanes called hutongs, most of which have been demolished since 1990.

Siting was governed by feng shui, which at its technical level is a set of rules about orientation, drainage, wind and the relation of a building to water and hill; at its popular level it is a cosmology of auspicious direction that estate agents still price. Gardens, above all in Suzhou, compressed mountain, water, pavilion and borrowed view into a walled hectare, with windows framing what the visitor is supposed to notice and paths that refuse a straight line.

The Wall is the building everyone knows and the one most misunderstood. What survives is mostly Ming masonry, built from the 1470s onward along a frontier that had been fortified with rammed earth since the Warring States; the state survey of 2012 counted 21,196 kilometres of all periods, much of it ditch and natural barrier. It never kept an army out for long — the Mongols and the Manchus both came through — but it slowed raids, carried signal fires, and controlled the trade in horses and salt that the frontier actually ran on.

Opera, strings, screens

Chinese theatre is not realistic and has never wanted to be. A table is a wall, a bed or a mountain depending on how it is used; a whip is a horse; a walk in a circle is a journey of a thousand li. Peking opera, formed in Beijing around 1790 when Anhui troupes came for the emperor's birthday and stayed, is the best-known form: sung arias in two main styles, speech that is half-chanted, acrobatics, and four role types — the male sheng, the female dan, the painted-face jing and the clown chou. The percussion drives everything; the conductor is a drummer. Face paint is a code: red for loyalty, white for treachery, black for rectitude.

Kunqu, older and slower, was declared a masterpiece of oral heritage by UNESCO in 2001 and survives mainly through a handful of companies and a repertoire of Peony Pavilion and its peers. Hundreds of regional operas continue in the provinces, often with state subsidy and ageing audiences. The Mao years replaced most of the repertoire with eight model works in which the costumes were modern and the politics explicit; the fact that a generation can still sing them is one of the odder legacies of the period.

Instrumental music divides into the courtly and the popular. The guqin, a seven-string zither played with the fingers and stopped without frets, is the scholar's instrument, quiet enough that a listener must lean in; its notation, a written finger-by-finger tablature, is the oldest working instrumental notation in the world. The pipa lute came from Central Asia, the erhu two-string fiddle from the north, the sheng mouth organ is the ancestor of the European harmonica and accordion, and the dizi flute carries the sound most people abroad associate with China. Scales are pentatonic, but the claim that Chinese music has no harmony is wrong: it has heterophony, several instruments ornamenting the same line differently, which is a different idea of texture rather than an absence of one.

In the twentieth century the loudest exports were cinema and, more recently, contemporary art. Shanghai in the 1930s made a left-wing cinema of remarkable quality; the Fifth Generation — Chen Kaige, Zhang Yimou — arrived in the 1980s with Yellow Earth and Red Sorghum, and the Sixth Generation (Jia Zhangke) filmed the demolition of the old cities. Ai Weiwei, Cai Guo-Qiang and Xu Bing took the Chinese material vocabulary — porcelain, gunpowder, characters — into the international gallery system, and paid various prices at home. The one figure everyone in the country knows is Wukong, the Monkey King of Journey to the West, who has been opera, cartoon, television series, video game and, for a decade, a national brand.

Three teachings and a crowd of gods

There is no Chinese religion in the sense that there is a Chinese language. There is a field of practice in which three traditions — Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist — are used for different things by the same person, often on the same day, plus a local pantheon of gods, ancestors and ghosts that is older than all three. The phrase for this is sanjiao, the three teachings, and the traditional image is of three men tasting the same vinegar: one finds it sour, one bitter, one sweet. None of them is wrong.

Daoism begins with a text of about five thousand characters, the Daodejing, attributed to Laozi and probably compiled in the fourth or third century BCE, and with a book of stories, the Zhuangzi, that is one of the funniest works of philosophy ever written. Its central ideas are dao, the way things happen when nothing forces them; wu wei, action without coercion; and ziran, spontaneity, the self-so. Organised Daoism is much later: the Way of the Heavenly Masters, founded in the second century CE in Sichuan, and the Quanzhen and Zhengyi orders of the Song and Jin, with monasteries, liturgies, a canon and an internal alchemy that sought physical immortality through breath, diet and drugs — an effort that killed several emperors and advanced Chinese chemistry.

The Daoism of the temples is a religion of festivals, exorcism and local gods, and it is where the YinYang diagram does its work: two forces, one waxing as the other wanes, each containing the seed of the other. That figure organises cosmology, medicine, music and martial arts, and it is a better description of how Chinese thought handles contradiction than the word "harmony" is.

Buddhism supplied what the other two did not: a doctrine of salvation, a clergy, and an account of what happens after death. Its Chinese forms — Chan, Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan, and in Tibet the Vajrayana — were discussed in the previous section, but the doctrinal core is worth stating, because it is not theism: suffering arises from craving, craving from the illusion of a permanent self, and the emptiness of all phenomena is not nothingness but the fact that nothing exists independently. The koan — a question with no rational answer, used to exhaust the discursive mind — is the Chan method that travelled to Japan as Zen and is now, worldwide, the most popular export of Chinese religion.

Confucianism supplies the ritual and the ethics of the household and the state: the five relationships (ruler–minister, father–son, husband–wife, elder–younger, friend–friend), each with reciprocal duties; xiao, filial piety, which obliges the living to care for parents and the dead to be fed and remembered; and the state cult of Heaven, performed at the Temple of Heaven in Beijing at the winter solstice by the emperor alone, because he was the only one entitled to speak to Heaven on the country's behalf.

Around these, the popular pantheon: Mazu, the goddess of the sea, a tenth-century fisherman's daughter from Fujian whose cult followed emigrants across the ocean; Guandi, the deified general Guan Yu, patron of police, merchants and sworn brotherhoods; Caishen, the god of wealth, whose image hangs in shops; Zao Jun, the kitchen god, whose paper effigy is smeared with sweets and burned at New Year so that he reports favourably; and the City God, who audits the dead of each district.

Roughly half the population reports no religion; of those who do, Buddhists and folk practitioners are the largest groups, with Christians estimated between two and five per cent (Protestant congregations grew fastest in the 1980s and 1990s, and are now more regulated), Muslims around twenty-five million (Hui across the country, Uyghurs and others in Xinjiang, where restrictions on religious practice are severe), and a small Daoist clergy. The party's own membership is about a hundred million and its cadres are expected to be atheist, which does not stop the same officials from consulting a geomancer about a building's orientation.

The family, the examination, the city

The examination system is the institution that most shaped Chinese society, and it ran for thirteen hundred years: from 605, when the Sui began recruiting by written test, to 1905, when the Qing abolished it. In its mature form it had three tiers — prefectural, provincial, metropolitan — with quotas, a fixed curriculum of the Four Books and Five Classics in Zhu Xi's interpretation, an eight-legged essay form, and a pass rate in the low single figures. Success made a man a jinshi and an official; failure could be repeated for a lifetime. The effect was to make literacy the route to power and to spread a common classical culture to every county, at the cost of a curriculum that had not changed in centuries.

The family was the unit the system served. Xiao was not sentiment but obligation: support in old age, mourning leave of three years for a parent, the duty to produce a male heir to continue the sacrifices, and the authority of the eldest male over property and marriage. Lineages kept genealogies, built ancestral halls and held land in trusts. The 1950 Marriage Law outlawed arranged marriage and concubinage and gave women the right to divorce, and the collectives then broke the lineage's economic base — but the obligation survived in practice, and the 1980 one-child policy, combined with a preference for sons that the policy itself sharpened, produced a generation with a sex ratio at birth above 115 boys per 100 girls at its peak.

The hukou, introduced in 1958, tied a household to a place and to a category — agricultural or non-agricultural — and with it access to grain, schooling, housing and health care. Reform did not abolish it; it made it porous. Nearly 300 million people now work in cities where they have no right to settle, sending money home and returning for the New Year, a migration that is the largest in human history and the reason Chinese cities can build at the speed they do.

Schooling is compulsory for nine years, and the gaokao, the national university entrance examination, is the single most consequential morning in a Chinese teenager's life: a score, a province-level quota and a ranking determine the university, the city and much of the career. Around it has grown an industry of cram schools and a literature of schooling under pressure. Work has its own vocabulary — 996, nine in the morning to nine at night, six days a week, the norm in parts of the tech industry until the state began to discourage it; tang ping, lying flat, the refusal of that bargain by a generation that has done the arithmetic on housing prices.

Marriage rates are falling, the average age of first birth is rising, and the population fell for the first time in six decades in 2022. The estimate for 2025 is around 1.4 billion, with about 30 per cent over 60 by 2035 on current trends. No country has aged at this speed at this income level, and the pension and health systems were designed for a younger population with more children.

Rice, tea, and the year

The food map is the wheat–rice line. North of the Yangtze, flour: steamed buns, hand-pulled noodles, dumplings, pancakes, vinegar and lamb. South, rice: rice as grain, rice as noodle, rice as flour for dumplings, with freshwater fish, shellfish and greens. The eight great cuisines — Shandong, Sichuan, Guangdong, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Hunan, Anhui — are a late codification of regional styles, and the differences are real: Sichuan's chilli and numbing pepper (both New World and introduced in the late Ming), Cantonese's steaming and roast meats, Huaiyang's knife work and mild stocks, Hunan's dry heat without the numbing.

The technology behind the food is the wok, a thin-walled cast-iron vessel that became cheap in the Song when coal replaced charcoal in the foundries, and which makes possible the fast, high-heat tossing that Chinese cooks call bao. Chopsticks are a consequence of the kitchen having already cut everything into mouth-sized pieces. Tea was a medicine, then a luxury, then a monopoly, then a habit: Lu Yu's Classic of Tea in 780 fixed the vocabulary, the Song whipped powdered tea (the ancestor of Japanese matcha), the Ming switched to leaf infusion, and the Qing export trade to Britain restructured both economies. Green, oolong and pu-erh are the same leaf processed differently, and the gongfu ceremony is a way of making a small amount of tea last an afternoon.

The year runs on the lunisolar calendar, with the New Year falling on the second new moon after the winter solstice — between 21 January and 20 February — and it is the only holiday the whole country takes at once: the travel period, chunyun, is the largest annual movement of people on earth. Then Lantern Festival, fifteen days later, with lanterns and sweet rice balls; Qingming in April, when graves are swept and the dead are fed; Duanwu in the fifth lunar month, with dragon boats and leaf-wrapped rice, commemorating the poet Qu Yuan, who drowned himself in protest and whose body the boats were rowed out to save; Mid-Autumn in the eighth month, with mooncakes and family reunion; Double Ninth, when one climbs a hill for the sake of the number.

The twenty-four solar terms, inscribed by UNESCO in 2016, divide the year into agricultural fortnights with names like "awakening of insects" and "white dew", and they still appear in calendars and weather forecasts. The twelve animals of the zodiac cycle by year, and the older system of ten heavenly stems and twelve earthly branches combines into a sixty-year cycle that names years the way the West names them with numbers. The zodiac travelled: the Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese versions are recognisably the same twelve, with local substitutions — the dog in Japan, the cat in Vietnam. And the culture of the year is where the festivals of the wider region can be read as one family of practices with different calendars.

The body as a system

Traditional Chinese medicine is a complete theoretical system, not a collection of remedies, and it is worth understanding on its own terms before judging it. Its founding text, the Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon, compiled around the Han from older material, describes the body as a network of channels in which qi circulates, and health as the balance of yin and yang and the five phases. Diagnosis is by inspection of the tongue and face, listening, questioning and palpation of the pulse at three positions on each wrist, each read at three depths — nine pulse qualities per side, in the classical scheme. Treatment aims at the pattern, not the disease: two patients with the same complaint may get opposite prescriptions.

The pharmacopoeia is enormous and empirical in origin. The Compendium of Materia Medica of Li Shizhen (1596) lists 1,892 substances with preparation and indication, and its entries on ephedra, rhubarb and chaulmoogra are recognisably pharmacology. Acupuncture — fine needles at defined points, sometimes with heat from burning moxa — is the best studied part, and the evidence is mixed rather than absent: it performs better than no treatment for some pain conditions, roughly as well as sham acupuncture in most trials, and the gap between those two results is where the argument lives. The 2011 Nobel Prize for Tu Youyou's artemisinin, extracted from a plant named in a fourth-century recipe, is the strongest case for taking the materia medica seriously as a source of leads.

Institutions have moved faster than evidence. The WHO's country office has supported integration since the 1970s, and the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases, adopted in 2019, includes a chapter on traditional medicine — a classification of what practitioners diagnose, not an endorsement of efficacy, though it is often reported as one. Chinese hospitals commonly run both systems side by side, and patients choose. A dossier that either dismisses the tradition as superstition or promotes it as ancient wisdom is not describing the situation: it is a working hybrid with real pharmacology, unproven theory and a large placebo component, which is exactly what most of the world's medical traditions were before the randomised trial.

The body is also the site of practice rather than belief. Qigong, the slow movement and breath work that became a mass movement in the 1950s and was suppressed when it turned political, and taijiquan, the boxing discussed earlier, are the everyday forms: millions of people in parks at dawn, moving slowly, because it is good for the knees and because it is a way of being outside with other people. Whether it is medicine is a separate question from whether it is worth doing.

How to keep reading

Four books will take a reader further than any summary. Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, written in the first century BCE, is the foundation of Chinese historical writing and still readable in Burton Watson's translations. The Tang poets, above all Du Fu, are the country's emotional archive; Kenneth Rexroth's and Burton Watson's versions are good places to start. Dream of the Red Chamber, written in the eighteenth century by Cao Xueqin, is the novel that every educated Chinese reader has read at least once — a family chronicle that is also a theory of fiction. And Lu Xun's stories, from 1918 onward, are the shortest route into the twentieth century's argument with itself.

For the material record, the Shanghai Museum's bronzes and the National Palace Museum's painting collection are the obvious places; abroad, the British Museum, the Met and the Musée Guimet hold the objects that the nineteenth century removed. Treat any single book on China with suspicion, including this one: the country is large enough that a claim true of Sichuan is false of Gansu, and its historiography is contested at every level, from the date of the Xia to the death toll of the famine. The honest position is the one this dossier has tried to hold — describe the mechanism, name the evidence, and say which numbers are disputed.

The four threads followed here meet in one sentence: a state that recruited by examination, a script that carried meaning without sound, an aesthetic that put the mountain above the portrait, and a household that treated the dead as members. None of those four survived the twentieth century intact. All four are still visible in how the country builds, writes, eats and argues — which is why the word "continuous" is not wrong, only imprecise.
