Survival: how to stay alive, healthy and free when everything around you breaks

A complete, technical and honest guide: how water, fire, shelter, food and energy really work in the wild; how a city behaves when power, water and trust collapse; how to produce what you need; and the bad decisions that kill people who thought they were prepared. 0. Why survival is a skill, not a mood

Most people who die in a disaster did not lack courage. They lacked a sequence. They drank the first water they found, lit a heater in a closed room, walked toward a crowd because it looked like help, or waited for an official announcement that never came. Survival is the discipline of choosing the right thing in the right order while your body and your nerves are shouting at you to do something else.

This dossier has three parts. Part I covers the wilderness and rural survival that has been taught for a century and that still works. Part II covers urban survival during conflict, civil war, blackouts and riots, where the enemy is often other people, disease, and bad information. Part III covers production and new ideas: how to make water, food, light and communication when the grid is gone, and how communities protect each other.

Honesty rule. Every technique here is either a standard public-health or emergency-management recommendation, or a method used by wilderness schools, humanitarian agencies or field engineers. Where a technique is dangerous, the danger is written next to it. Nothing in this dossier explains how to make weapons or explosives, and no tactic here should be used against other people.
Sources, not vibes. Numbers come from the CDC[1], Ready.gov[2], the Sphere humanitarian standards[3], the National Center for Home Food Preservation[6] and the ICRC[8]. Local laws and local health authorities always override this text.

1. The Rule of Threes: what kills you first

Survival training uses a simple priority ladder. It is a rule of thumb, not a law of physics, but it puts the scarce resources in order. Watch the bar fill: the first two bullets are measured in minutes and hours, the last two in days and weeks.

3 min
without air (breathing)
3 hrs
without shelter in harsh cold, heat or storm
3 days
without water (varies with heat and effort)
3 weeks
without food (body reserves, not comfort)

Ready.gov recommends at least one gallon (about 3.8 litres) of water per person per day, for at least three days[2]. The Sphere minimum for emergencies is 15 litres per person per day for drinking, cooking and hygiene[3].

Bad decision: starting with food. People who are hungry panic about food. But a person who is wet, cold and without shelter at night can die of hypothermia within hours, long before hunger matters. Fix the minutes problem first, then the hours problem, then water, then food.

PART I. Outside the city

Wilderness, rural areas, flood zones, mountains and disaster sites with no power and no supply trucks. The threats: dehydration, cold, heat, injury, getting lost, and contaminated water.

1.1 Water: the first real priority

The body loses roughly 2 to 3 litres a day at rest in temperate conditions, and much more in heat or hard work. Surface water (rivers, lakes, ponds, rain runoff) almost always carries bacteria, viruses and parasites such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium. Clear water is not safe water. Treatment works in layers:

1. Settle / pre-filtercloth, sand, or a sediment bottle removes silt and large particles
2. Filterceramic or hollow-fibre filter removes most bacteria and protozoa
3. Disinfectboil, chlorine or UV kills viruses that filters miss
4. Storeclean container, lid, label, used within days

Bad decision: "It's a clean mountain stream." Streams in cattle country, below villages, or downstream of a dead animal are contaminated, and there is no visual test. Hikers have caught Giardia from crystal-clear water for decades. Better: treat everything, every time, unless you are boiling it anyway.
Bad decision: salt water or urine. Drinking seawater makes the kidneys excrete more water than you gain. Drinking urine re-concentrates the same waste you are trying to flush. Both accelerate dehydration. Better: a solar still or rain catchment, even if slow, plus rationing.
Good practice: the solar still. Dig a bowl, place a clean container in the centre, cover with clear plastic sealed to the ground, weight the centre with a stone so condensation drips into the container. Output is small (often under a litre a day) but it avoids contamination and it works when everything else fails. Treat its output as dubious until disinfected too.

1.2 Fire: heat, light, cooking and water purification

Fire needs three elements, the fire triangle: heat, fuel and oxygen. Once a fire is burning and chain reactions sustain it, you have the fourth side, which makes it a fire tetrahedron. Remove any side and the fire stops. Click a side in the animation to remove it.

Status: burning. Click a side (the coloured lines) to remove it.

Practical translation: heat = a spark or match and then kindling; fuel = dry tinder, kindling, fuel wood in order of size; oxygen = air flow. Smothering with a lid removes oxygen. Water removes heat. Removing the fuel (e.g. pulling burning wood out) removes fuel.

Fire-building, step by step. Prepare three piles: tinder (dry grass, bark shavings, dry pine needles, cotton wool with petroleum jelly), kindling (pencil to thumb-thick twigs), and fuel (wrist to arm thick wood). Build a teepee or a lean-to over the tinder, light from downwind, feed the smallest size first and increase gradually. Keep a supply of dry wood under cover before the storm, not during it.

Bad decision: a charcoal grill or generator inside a closed room. Each year people die from carbon monoxide poisoning after running grills, camp stoves, generators or car engines in closed garages, tents and homes. CO is colourless and odourless and produces headache, dizziness, confusion and death without warning[5]. Better: cook and heat only outdoors or in a ventilated space, and install a battery-backed CO alarm.

1.3 Shelter: controlling heat loss

In cold conditions you lose heat by four routes: conduction (touching the ground or wet clothing), convection (wind), radiation (to the cold sky) and evaporation (sweat, wet clothes). A good shelter cuts all four. The animation shows the heat escaping from an uninsulated shelter; toggle the insulation to see how the loss changes.

SHELTER
↑←→↓
  • Site: away from dead standing trees, avalanche paths, flood plains and dry riverbeds (flash floods). Prefer the lee of a ridge, under a stand of living trees.
  • Lean-to: a ridge pole on two supports, ribs of sticks, and 30 to 50 cm of debris on top. Face the opening away from the wind and build a reflector fire in front.
  • Insulate from the ground first: a thick layer of dry leaves or boughs. Ground contact steals more heat than the air.
  • Keep the clothes dry. Wet insulation loses most of its value. Change into dry layers before sleep.
Bad decision: sleeping on bare ground next to a fire in a tent. The fire warms the front while your back loses heat to the soil, and a small tent draws a dangerous amount of CO and sparks. Better: insulate beneath, keep the fire at a safe distance, and ventilate.

1.4 Food: calories, foraging, preservation and production

An adult at rest needs about 2,000 to 2,500 kcal a day; cold weather, heavy work and growth push that higher. Starvation takes weeks, but energy deficit in the cold accelerates hypothermia and poor decisions. Prioritise calorie-dense, low-spoilage foods: rice, oats, dried beans, nuts, oils, dried meat, hard cheese, honey and sugar. Store them dry and sealed from pests.

FoodApprox. energyStorage notes
White rice (dry)about 3,600 kcal/kgYears in sealed, cool, dry containers; oxygen absorbers help.

Bad decision: eating unidentified wild plants or mushrooms. Death-cap mushrooms (Amanita phalloides) look like edible species and cause liver failure days after eating, when the victim believes they have recovered. Water hemlock is one of the most toxic plants in North America. Rule: eat only what you can identify with certainty, using two independent field guides and a local expert, and never test-taste a plant you cannot name.
Good practice: production that works quickly. Sprouting seeds (mung beans, lentils) gives fresh vitamin C-rich food in 3 to 5 days using a jar and water. A container garden with kale, potatoes, beans and herbs on a balcony produces food within 60 to 120 days. A seed library (saved open-pollinated seeds, labelled and dried) keeps your garden alive for years.

Preservation. Drying (below 15% water content for most foods) and salting are reliable and need no power. Fermentation (sauerkraut, kimchi) is safe when salt ratios and anaerobic conditions are right. Canning low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, beans) requires a pressure canner. A boiling water bath cannot reach the temperature needed to destroy Clostridium botulinum spores; home-canned low-acid food is a classic cause of fatal botulism[6].

1.5 Navigation without a phone

Use a paper map (scale and grid reference), a compass, and time. Set the map to north with the compass, then take a bearing on your next landmark. Carry a whistle; three blasts is the international distress signal. Mark your route by breadcrumbs (cairns, tape) only where it is safe to return.

NSEW

Shadow-stick method (rough east-west)

  1. Push a straight stick into flat ground at sunrise and mark the tip of its shadow.
  2. Wait 15 to 30 minutes and mark the new shadow tip.
  3. The line from the first mark to the second runs roughly west to east. Left is west.

1.6 First aid in the field

The usual wilderness killers are severe bleeding, hypothermia, heat illness and sprains with delayed evacuation. Stop the Bleed training teaches the core steps[7]:

  1. Severe bleeding: press hard with a hand or cloth directly on the wound. If it keeps bleeding on a limb, apply a commercial tourniquet 5 to 7 cm above the wound, tighten until bleeding stops, and write the time on the patient's skin or clothing. Do not loosen it.
  2. Hypothermia (below about 35 °C core): shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, confusion. Remove wet clothes, insulate from the ground, use warm dry layers and warm sweet drinks only if the person is alert. Handle gently: rough movement can trigger dangerous heart rhythms in severe cases.
  3. Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, weakness. Move to shade, sip water with electrolytes, cool the skin. Heat stroke (confusion, hot dry or wet skin, collapse) is an emergency: cool aggressively, evacuate.
Bad decision: loosening a tourniquet after five minutes "to save the limb". Once the bleeding is controlled with a tourniquet, releasing it can cause the victim to bleed out. Tourniquet removal belongs to a surgeon, not to a frightened bystander. Better: tighten, mark the time, and get the patient to care.

Planning: the go-bag and the stay-kit

A go-bag is for leaving within minutes (documents, medicines, water for 72 hours, light, radio, copies of contacts). A stay-kit is for remaining home or in a shelter for 2 weeks (stored water, food, medicines, sanitation, fuel-free cooking, batteries). Keep both, rotate food and medicines, and practise the route out of the area once a year.[2]

PART II. Inside the city in conflict or civil war

In a city the disasters are layered: power and water fail, shops empty, police and armed groups compete for control, shelling or gunfire reaches residential blocks, and rumours spread faster than facts. Your priorities change: avoid being targeted, get reliable information, keep your family together, and keep basic services running.

2.1 Read the threat: a map is a decision tool

Conflict is not uniform. Districts differ by hour. Ask neighbours, aid workers and reliable local media what is happening, then classify your route and your neighbourhood. The map below is a demonstration: click each zone to see the response it calls for.

2.2 Information hygiene

In civil wars, false alarms and deliberate disinformation are weapons. Videos are re-labelled; old footage is presented as new; "evacuation" messages are sent to lure people into ambushes. Build a source ladder:

  1. Primary official channels: local emergency authority, hospital, the municipal water utility, and humanitarian bodies such as the ICRC and UN OCHA (via ReliefWeb)[16].
  2. Independent local reporters who name specific places, times and sources, and who correct themselves.
  3. Your own eyes and your neighbours, confirmed by two independent people before you act.
  4. Social media is the last step, never the first. Check whether an account is new, whether it posts constantly in several conflicts, and whether the image appears in older reports (reverse-image search).
Bad decision: moving on a single viral message. A message that says "water truck at the stadium, go now" can be a rumour, a lure, or simply old news. Before moving, confirm with two independent sources and at least one official one. Better: wait 20 minutes, ask a neighbour who went out, and go in a group with a plan.

2.3 Shelter inside buildings

The most common injuries in urban shelling are from glass, falling masonry and fragments. The safest location is the lowest level you can reach, in a room without windows or exterior walls, away from the side facing the threat. A basement or interior corridor with several walls between you and the outside is better than a sunny upper floor. Keep shoes, a torch and documents within reach. Do not stand at the top of stairs or on balconies to watch.

  • Tape or cover windows with mattresses or heavy cloth; move beds and furniture away from glass.
  • Keep a gap in the corridor for quick exit, and avoid crowding.
  • Know two exits from your building and a meeting point for the family.
Bad decision: going to the roof or the window to look. Curiosity about the sound of a blast gets people exposed. Curiosity is one of the most common reasons civilians are hurt by fragments and secondary blasts. Stay low, stay inside, listen to the radio.

2.4 Water, power and cooking in a city

When the network fails, water is still the first priority: store water before the crisis (bathtubs, clean food-grade containers), find the nearest distribution point, and use the same treatment steps as Part I. Electricity is rarely restored on a predictable schedule, so plan for no grid for at least 2 weeks. Cook on a stove only in ventilated spaces, and never bring generators, grills or gas heaters inside.

Good practice: the family water rota. Assign one adult per day to collect water from the distribution point, recording litres and the time. Use two containers that can be carried by hand. Keep a reserve for children and the sick. Share the rota with neighbours; a shared water rota is more reliable than a private one.

2.5 Movement and checkpoints

Checkpoints are dangerous because you are stressed and because the people controlling them are often frightened and under pressure. The safest approach is calm and predictable:

  1. Slow down well before the checkpoint. Do not rush toward it or try to turn around sharply.
  2. Keep your hands visible, on the wheel or in plain sight. Do not reach into pockets or bags without warning.
  3. Switch off the radio and lights as instructed, and tell the operator calmly what you are doing: "I am going to get my ID from the glove box."
  4. Have ID, the purpose of travel and a list of family members ready. Answer briefly and honestly.
  5. Do not photograph armed personnel or equipment, and do not argue. Follow instructions and leave when permitted.
  6. Carry nothing that could be mistaken for a weapon or a tool of a party to the conflict.
Bad decision: taking photos for social media at a checkpoint. Filming a checkpoint can be read as surveillance and can lead to detention, confiscation or worse. It also tells the people controlling it that you are a target. Better: document later, from a safe place, and share only with trusted contacts.

2.6 Civilian protection: know your rights and the limits

International humanitarian law protects civilians and civilian objects in armed conflict. Under the Fourth Geneva Convention and its Additional Protocols, civilians who are not taking a direct part in hostilities must be treated humanely; medical personnel, hospitals and ambulances have specific protection; and attacks must distinguish between military and civilian targets[8]. In practice: protection is not automatic, enforcement is weak, and the law applies to the parties, not to you. Your best protections are distance, documentation and contact with humanitarian organisations.

If family members are separated, the ICRC's Restoring Family Links service can help trace and reconnect them[9]. Register names with the Red Cross or Red Crescent in your region.

2.8 Community: the strongest survival tool in a city

Across the disaster and siege accounts that humanitarian agencies document, families tend to fare better when they are connected: they shared water rotas, child care, medicine, information and warnings. Isolation is the most dangerous posture. Organise a building or street group with a list of who is vulnerable (elderly, pregnant, disabled, children), who has medical training, who has water and who has power.

PART III. Production and new ideas for safety

Surviving is not only avoiding loss. It is producing the things you need, safely, repeatedly and with the people around you.

3.1 Rain catchment (produce water)

Rainfall is measured in millimetres. One millimetre of rain over one square metre of roof collects about 1 litre. A 50 m² roof therefore yields roughly 50 litres per 1 mm of rain, and 20 mm of rain can give about 1,000 litres. Use gutters, a first-flush diverter that discards the first few litres (which carry roof dirt and bird droppings), a food-grade tank with a lid and a mosquito screen, and disinfect before drinking.

Calculator: litres = roof area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × 0.8 (typical collection efficiency for corrugated roofs). Example: 60 m² × 15 mm × 0.8 = 720 litres.

3.2 Solar and low-power light

A small 50 to 100 W solar panel with a charge controller and a 12 V battery powers LED lights, a radio and phone charging. Size the battery with: watt-hours needed per day ÷ battery voltage = amp-hours. Use lithium-iron-phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries where safe, or sealed lead-acid batteries that are well-ventilated. Never charge lithium cells in a sealed room, and never leave them in a hot car.

Good practice: passive cooling and a solar oven. A solar box oven built from cardboard, foil and glass can reach 120 to 150 °C in full sun and pasteurise water and cook beans. Passive cooling (shade, cross-ventilation, wet cloth on windows, bedding at night) cuts heat stress without power.

3.3 Food and micronutrients

Sprouting, container gardening, seed saving and fermentation keep your family fed with few inputs. Combine staples with fats and protein: beans and rice, oats with nuts, eggs or fish where available. Rotate crops, keep compost, and track yield per square metre so you know how much land feeds your household.

3.4 New ideas to stay safe

Neighbourhood mesh

Pair a Meshtastic node with a neighbour's node and a shared hall or mosque, church or school radio. Test it weekly; a dead network is a rumour machine.

Cooperative water bank

Each household stores 40 litres. A shared tank in the courtyard covers people who cannot store water, managed by a rota and logged in a notebook.

Medical kit sharing

Pool tourniquets, dressings, oral rehydration salts, antibiotics prescribed for the household, and a trained first-aider on each block.

Digital offline library

Keep Kiwix with medical and repair guides, offline maps and language dictionaries on two devices plus a printed copy of the essentials.

Family plan and safe words

Pick two meeting points (one near, one far), an out-of-area contact everyone calls, and a check-in time. Teach children their home address and the contact number by heart.

Bike or hand-crank power

A bicycle with a dynamo or alternator can charge a battery in a few hours of pedalling and doubles as transport and exercise for the whole family.

Bad decision: stockpiling weapons instead of water and medicine. Weapons do not solve dehydration, infection, cold or disease, and they make your family a target and a risk. Most survival deaths come from the first three rows of the Rule of Threes, not from crime.
Bad decision: "I'll buy it when it happens". Supply chains close first. Prices spike, shops empty and transport stops. Buy water, rice, medicines and batteries in small amounts continuously, and rotate stock.
Bad decision: refusing to leave when officials order evacuation. Evacuation orders often come after the risk has grown beyond the ability to rescue. Leave early, with the go-bag, and with your neighbours.
