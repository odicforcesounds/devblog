1. The grammar of a good cause

The sentence is older than any of the states that use it: the cause is good, therefore the act is permitted. Every entry in this dossier is a version of that sentence, and the seven nouns that most often fill the first slot are these.

God, faith, the sacredConversion, purification of belief, holy warCrusades, inquisitions, witch-hunting, forced baptism

Two things must be said clearly, because this is the point where readers usually stop listening.

First: none of these names is evil in itself. Faith, nation, order, health, justice, progress and peace are real goods, held honestly by billions of people, including the people in this file who refused to use them as a warrant. The dossier is not an argument against good causes. It is an argument against the conversion of a good cause into a permission — the moment the noun stops being a direction to walk in and becomes a reason why this particular body does not count.

Second: the mechanics are identical whether the good name is religious or secular. The crusader, the commissioner of a colony, the officer of a dictatorship, the interrogator of a democracy and the commander of a "humanitarian" war are not doing the same thing for the same reason, but they are using the same three instruments — an unexamined cause, a distance from the victim, and a word that hides the act while it happens.

When a cause cannot be questioned, the act does not need to be justified. That is what a licence looks like.

2. The anatomy of the act

History is not written by the cause. It is written, or buried, by the institution and the word.

A cause needs an institution before it can kill

A mob can murder; only an institution can organise a genocide. The organisational ingredients are boringly consistent:

  • Division of labour — the person who makes the list is not the person who knocks on the door, and the person who drives is not the person who shoots.
  • Written criteria — a form, a quota, a category, a register. A paper trail is how you can later prove what a nation will spend decades denying.
  • Distance — an office, a screen, a telephone, a border, an altitude. Distance is the cheapest anaesthetic ever invented.
  • Euphemism — a word that hides the act while the act happens: pacification, resettlement, collateral damage, enhanced interrogation, Year Zero, final solution.
  • Career and comradeship — transfer, promotion, the locker room, the group that must not be let down.

The four floors of responsibility

Every atrocity has four floors, and only two of them are usually on trial
FloorWho stands thereTheir sentence
The causePriests, ideologues, intellectuals, editorial writersAlmost never prosecuted; sometimes honoured later

6. The wound in the one who obeys

Moral injury is different from PTSD. It comes from perpetrating, failing to prevent, or witnessing acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs.

  • Perpetration-based moral injury — shame that does not respond to medals or "it was your job".
  • Betrayal-based moral injury — when the institution turns on those who question orders.
  • Numbing — the officer who feels nothing, calling it maturity.
  • Cynicism — everyone's corrupt anyway protects the speaker from their own decisions.
  • Suicide risk — veterans and police die by their own hand more often than by external violence.
The wound is produced by the orders, and then the institution treats the wound as a personal failure.

7. How it ends: courts and commissions

The law answered the excuse of "just following orders" in 1946 and never took it back.

  • Nuremberg Principles (1950) — Acting under orders does not relieve responsibility if a moral choice was possible.
  • Rome Statute, Article 33 (1998) — Superior orders are no defence for genocide or crimes against humanity; manifestly unlawful orders are never a defence.
  • International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY), International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR), International Criminal Court (ICC) — attempt to hold individuals accountable.
  • Truth and Reconciliation Commissions — South Africa, Canada, Guatemala, Chile and others documented the truth when courts could not prosecute everyone.

8. Denial, memory and the archive

Every genocide has its denial. The archive is the answer.

  • Holocaust denial — despite overwhelming evidence, Nazi documents, photographs, and survivor testimony.
  • Armenian Genocide denial — remains state policy in Turkey.
  • Rwandan denial — continues despite ICTR records.
  • The record matters — the Khmer Rouge photographed every prisoner; the police filed shooting reports; colonial administrations kept ledgers. Paper trails outlive lies.

9. Holding a good cause without a licence

Six questions to ask when someone says "in the name of the good".

  1. Who counts? Does this cause require some group to be less human to achieve it?
  2. Who decides? Is there room to say no, or is questioning the cause itself treated as treason?
  3. What language? Are we using euphemisms to make an act sayable that would otherwise be unspeakable?
  4. Where is the distance? Would I do this face to face, with a name?
  5. Who pays? Who bears the cost, and do they have a say?
  6. What will the archive say? If this were written down honestly, would I still want it done in my name?
Never hand your conscience to someone else to hold.

A frase é mais velha que qualquer Estado que a use: a causa é boa, portanto o acto é permitido. Todos os casos deste dossier são versões dessa frase.

Nenhum destes nomes é mau por si só. Mas quando uma boa causa se transforma em licença para desumanizar, torna-se o gatilho do mal.

Quando uma causa não pode ser questionada, o acto não precisa de ser justificado. É isso que é uma licença.

2. A anatomia do acto

A história não é escrita pela causa. É escrita, ou enterrada, pela instituição e pela palavra.

  • Divisão do trabalho — quem faz a lista não é quem bate à porta, nem quem conduz é quem dispara.
  • Critérios escritos — formulários, quotas, registos. O papel trai quem nega.
  • Distância — escritório, ecrã, telefone, fronteira. O anestésico mais barato.
  • Eufemismo — «pacificação», «dano colateral», «interrogação reforçada», «Solução Final».
  • Carreira e camaradagem — promoção, grupo, «não deixar os colegas mal».

6. A ferida de quem obedece

A lesão moral (moral injury) é distinta do TEPT. Vem de cometer, não impedir, ou testemunhar actos que violam os valores mais profundos.

  • Vergonha e culpa — não se curam com medalhas nem com «era o teu dever».
  • Traição — quando a instituição vira costas a quem questiona.
  • Entorpecimento e cinismo — defesas que isolam e destroem.
  • Risco de suicídio — veteranos e polícias morrem mais por suicídio que em serviço.
A ferida é produzida pelas ordens, e depois a instituição trata-a como falha pessoal.

7. Como termina: tribunais e comissões

  • Princípios de Nuremberga (1950) — Obedecer ordens não desculpa se havia escolha moral.
  • Estatuto de Roma, Artigo 33 (1998) — Ordens superiores não são defesa para genocídio ou crimes contra a humanidade.
  • TPI, TPIJ, TPIY — Tentativas de responsabilizar indivíduos.
  • Comissões da Verdade e Reconciliação — Documentam a verdade quando os tribunais não conseguem julgar todos.

8. Negação, memória e arquivo

Todo o genocídio tem a sua negação. O arquivo é a resposta.

  • Negação do Holocausto — apesar de documentos nazis, fotografias e testemunhos.
  • Negação do genocídio arménio — continua como política de Estado na Turquia.
  • Os registos sobrevivem — fotografias do Khmer Vermelho, relatórios da polícia, arquivos coloniais.

9. Sustentar uma boa causa sem licença

Seis perguntas a fazer quando dizem «em nome do bem».

  1. Quem conta? Exige esta causa que algum grupo seja menos humano?
  2. Quem decide? Há espaço para dizer não ou questionar é traição?
  3. Que linguagem? Usamos eufemismos para tornar dizível o indescritível?
  4. Onde está a distância? Faria isto cara a cara, com um nome?
  5. Quem paga? Quem suporta o custo e tem voz?
  6. O que dirá o arquivo? Se fosse escrito honestamente, ainda quereríamos que fosse feito em nosso nome?
Nunca entregues a tua consciência nas mãos de outro para a guardar.
