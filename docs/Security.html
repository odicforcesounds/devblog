Security field guide · defensive framing

How hacking actually happens.

Films show a hooded figure, a green terminal, and fifteen seconds of typing that opens a bank. Reality is duller and far more effective: an unlocked door, a stolen password, an unpatched server nobody remembered was facing the internet. This guide walks the eight levels of real intrusion technique, with a documented case file, a code sample and the defense that would have stopped it at each one.

Reality vs. the movies Start here

Real intrusions are not spells. They are a sequence of unremarkable steps, each one cheap, each one avoidable. The industry has a shared vocabulary for those steps — MITRE ATT&CK, which currently catalogues 15 tactics, 227 techniques and 479 sub-techniques for enterprise environments alone [1][2]. This page follows that shape, level by level.

Myth: hackers break cryptography

Modern encryption is not the weak point. They log in with someone's valid password instead.

Reality

Valid accounts and stolen credentials recur in nearly every case below. In the 2024 Snowflake intrusions, every incident investigated traced back to compromised customer credentials [37].

Myth: it takes a genius

Attack tooling is commoditised, sold as a subscription, and often operated by people who understand the business poorly and the tooling not at all.

Reality

The MGM Resorts intrusion was not won with an exploit. It was won by persuading a help desk to reset a password [19]. The victim's filing put the impact at roughly $100 million [18].

Myth: zero-days are required

Zero-days are expensive, perishable ammunition. Most intrusions use a vulnerability the vendor patched months ago — or no vulnerability at all.

Reality

Change Healthcare in 2024 was not a novel attack: a system simply wasn't using multifactor authentication [20].

Myth: it's over in seconds

Dwell time is long. SUNBURST, the SolarWinds backdoor, deliberately waited roughly two weeks before even phoning home.

Reality

Detection rarely fails because the attacker was fast. It fails because nobody was looking at the logs that already said what happened.

The ladder, at a glance

Levels 0–4 are about getting in. Levels 5–6 are about staying in and getting around. Levels 7–8 are about what the access is worth. An attacker rarely needs all eight — most intrusions end at level 2 or 3 and still work.

Read the ladder as a budget. Every rung is a trade: sophistication costs money and risks attention; an unlocked door costs nothing and often works. That is why the boring rungs — 1, 2 and 3 — carry most of the real intrusions, and why the exotic rung 8 gets the headlines.

Level 0 · Reconnaissance

0

Seeing what you left facing the internet

MITRE ATT&CK TA0043 Reconnaissance · “Hacking” begins with reading, not breaking.

Before anything is attacked, it is measured. Public-facing IP ranges are scanned continuously and indexed: which hosts, which ports, which software versions. Subdomains leak in certificate transparency logs. Job adverts name the exact products a company runs. Source-code repositories leak far more than code — sometimes live credentials.

None of this is illegal in most jurisdictions, and all of it is automated. The attacker's real question at this level is simple: what is exposed, and who can log into it?

Case file Uber, 2016 — the key that was sitting in Git

The access chain behind Uber's 2016 breach is a perfect illustration of levels 0, 2 and 3 stacked together. According to the US Federal Trade Commission, an engineer had placed an AWS access key in plain text inside a private GitHub repository, and the intruders reached those repositories using engineer passwords exposed in other large data breaches — Uber did not require MFA for repository access and did not prohibit password reuse [25].

  • The FTC's figures: over one month, intruders downloaded 25.6 million names and email addresses, 22.1 million names and mobile numbers, and 607,000 driver's-licence numbers [25].
  • California's Attorney General described the exposure as data of 57 million users; Uber later settled nationwide for $148 million over the breach and the delayed disclosure [26].
  • Two mundane controls — mandatory MFA on the code host, and never storing long-lived keys in a repository — would each have broken the chain.

Why “private repository” is not a security control

A private repo is an authorisation decision made by one vendor, protecting a secret you do not control, sitting behind a password that a human chose and may reuse. It is one factor. Once an attacker holds a valid engineer session — as in the Uber case — private and public look identical. Treat every repository as eventually public and every key as eventually leaked: that is why short-lived, scoped credentials beat long-lived ones regardless of where they live.

What stops it

  • Secret scanning in the pipeline, plus history scanning — catch the key before a human forgets it.
  • No long-lived cloud keys. Use SSO, OIDC and workload identity so there is nothing durable to steal.
  • Cut your internet surface. Inventory what is actually reachable; kill forgotten endpoints and test environments.
  • Mandatory MFA on the code host — this alone broke the Uber chain at the first step.

Level 1 · The unlocked front door

1

Devices still using the factory password

MITRE ATT&CK T1078 Valid Accounts · T1110 Brute Force · Default and weak credentials

The cheapest possible intrusion: a device that ships with admin/admin, is never changed, and is reachable from the internet on an administrative port. No exploit, no cleverness — just a login form left as the vendor designed it.

Case file Mirai, 2016 — 62 default passwords, one botnet

Mirai scanned the internet continuously for internet-of-things devices — routers, cameras, DVRs — and logged in with a list of 62 common default usernames and passwords. Each compromised device joined a botnet used for distributed denial-of-service attacks. CISA advised network defenders to watch Telnet ports 23 and 2323 for unauthorised access attempts [29].

  • CISA attributed a DDoS exceeding 620 Gbps against KrebsOnSecurity to Mirai, and a separate Mirai attack on OVH of at least 1.1 Tbps [29].
  • CISA cited the Mirai author's claim of more than 380,000 IoT devices enlisted for the Krebs attack [29].
  • Mirai was also associated with the October 2016 Dyn DNS outage, which made major sites briefly unreachable for many users; the SEI's write-up describes Mirai's role cautiously, as one factor among several [30].
  • No CVE was needed for the original mechanism. The weakness was a known default credential on an exposed port.

What stops it

  • Change defaults at onboarding, on a process that fails closed if the step is skipped.
  • Turn off what you don't use: Telnet, UPnP, vendor cloud bridges, debug ports.
  • Segment the device class. Cameras and printers should not reach the finance VLAN or the internet freely.
  • Rate-limit and alert on authentication failures per source, and block or tarpit on threshold.
  • Inventory the unowned. Most default-credential casualties are devices no one knows they own.

Level 2 · Password reuse & credential stuffing

2

Your password was valid here because it was valid somewhere else

MITRE ATT&CK T1110.004 Credential Stuffing · T1078 Valid Accounts · T1555 Credentials from Password Stores

Credential stuffing takes username/password pairs leaked from one service and tries them on another, exploiting the fact that people reuse passwords. MITRE describes the technique as gaining access through “credential overlap” — users reusing passwords across personal and business accounts [36].

Add infostealer malware, which harvests saved credentials and session data from a victim's own machine and uploads them to a marketplace, and the attacker never needs to break anything at all. They are simply logging in as an employee.

Case file The 2024 Snowflake campaign — AT&T, Ticketmaster, Santander

This is the reference example of level 2 at scale. Mandiant tracked the financially motivated actor as UNC5537 and reported that every incident it investigated was traced back to compromised customer credentials, with no evidence the activity came from a breach of Snowflake's own environment. The credentials had previously been stolen by infostealer malware on systems that were not Snowflake's; at least 79.7% of the accounts the actor used had prior credential exposure. In the incidents analysed, MFA was not enabled, valid credentials had sometimes gone unrotated for years, and network allow lists were absent. Mandiant and Snowflake had notified approximately 165 potentially exposed organisations [37].

  • AT&T told the SEC that an attacker accessed a workspace on a third-party cloud platform and exfiltrated files between April 14 and April 25, 2024. The data covered call and text-interaction records for roughly May–October 2022 and one day in January 2023, and the filing says it covered “nearly all of AT&T's wireless customers.” The filing itself does not state a numeric total [38]. The widely reported figure of roughly 109 million US customer accounts comes from contemporaneous reporting and describes accounts, not individual call records [39][40].
  • Ticketmaster confirmed “unauthorized activity on an isolated cloud database hosted by a third-party data services provider” and did not name the provider or confirm the frequently repeated 560-million figure [41].
  • Santander confirmed unauthorised access to a third-party-hosted database affecting certain customers in Chile, Spain and Uruguay, while stating that no credentials allowing transactions were contained in that database [42].

Case file 23andMe, 2023 — 14,000 logins, 6.9 million profiles

A useful lesson in reading breach numbers carefully. 23andMe said an actor accessed a select number of individual accounts using credential stuffing — credentials reused from other compromised websites. The confirmed figure was roughly 14,000 accounts, under 0.1% of its customers. Through those accounts, the actor reached approximately 5.5 million DNA Relatives profiles and 1.4 million Family Tree profiles — about 6.9 million profiles in total, which is not the same as 6.9 million compromised logins [43].

  • Fourteen thousand reused passwords produced a headline about nearly seven million people: the blast radius of one login is every other account it can see.
  • Vendor measurement of the underlying behaviour: SpyCloud reported a 74% reuse rate among users exposed in two or more breaches during the prior year, and a 61% historical reuse rate for previously exposed passwords through 2023. These describe SpyCloud's analysed exposure population, not a survey of all internet users [44][45].
login.py — the version that feeds credential stuffingpython
# ✖ Fast hash, no rate limit, verbose errors, no MFA gate.
import hashlib

def login(db, email, password):
    row = db.execute(
        "SELECT pw_hash FROM users WHERE email = ?", (email,)
    ).fetchone()
    if row is None:
        return "No account with that email"   # enumeration: tells the attacker who exists
    if row["pw_hash"] == hashlib.md5(password.encode()).hexdigest():
        return "ok"                            # MD5: billions of guesses per second on a GPU
    return "Wrong password"                     # and nothing limits how often this runs
login.py — hardened for exactly this attackpython
# ✓ Memory-hard hash, per-source throttle, uniform responses, MFA continuation.
from argon2 import PasswordHasher
from argon2.exceptions import VerifyMismatchError

ph = PasswordHasher()

def login(db, cache, email, password, ip, device):
    # 1. Throttle by source *and* by account: stuffing sprays one account
    #    across thousands of IPs, and thousands of accounts from one IP.
    for key in (f"rl:ip:{ip}", f"rl:acct:{email}"):
        if cache.incr(key) == 1:
            cache.expire(key, 900)
        if int(cache.get(key) or 0) > 10:
            return generic_failure()           # same shape as a wrong password

    row = db.execute(
        "SELECT id, pw_hash, totp_secret FROM users WHERE email = ?", (email,)
    ).fetchone()

    try:
        if row and ph.verify(row["pw_hash"], password):
            if ph.check_needs_rehash(row["pw_hash"]):
                db.execute("UPDATE users SET pw_hash = ? WHERE id = ?",
                           (ph.hash(password), row["id"]))
            return require_mfa(row["id"], device)  # password alone is not a session
    except VerifyMismatchError:
        pass

    return generic_failure()                    # no enumeration, no detail, no oracle

The MFA line is the whole defense. In the Snowflake campaign, credentials were valid but MFA was simply not enabled, and in some cases the same credential had gone unrotated for years [37]. A stolen password plus a second factor the attacker does not have is just a failed login.

What stops it

  • MFA everywhere, then make it phishing-resistant (see level 3) — this defeats stolen passwords outright.
  • Argon2id or bcrypt for storage, so a stolen hash is not a cheap offline crack.
  • Screen passwords against breach corpora at set-password and login time — a reused password is a known password.
  • Throttle per account and per IP, with uniform error messages to avoid account enumeration.
  • Rotate and scope service credentials, and set network allow lists where the vendor supports them.
  • Assume endpoint compromise. Infostealers make credential theft trivial; endpoint hardening is a credential control too.

Level 3 · Social engineering

3

Hacking the people who legitimately have access

MITRE ATT&CK T1566 Phishing · T1078 Valid Accounts · MFA fatigue / help-desk impersonation

Social engineering is not a lesser form of hacking; it is the highest-yield one, because it targets the component that is hardest to patch and most willing to be helpful. Three variants dominate real incidents:

  • Phishing — a message that convinces a person to enter credentials on a page the attacker controls, or to open an attachment.
  • MFA fatigue / push bombing — the attacker already holds the password and repeatedly triggers push prompts until the victim approves one out of irritation or confusion.
  • Help-desk impersonation — the attacker calls the service desk as an employee, and asks them to reset the password or enrol a new authenticator.

Case file MGM Resorts, 2023 — a help desk, not an exploit

Public reporting attributes the intrusion to the Scattered Spider group using help-desk social engineering. CISA's advisory on the group describes precisely this tactic: persuading help-desk staff to reset passwords or reissue MFA tokens. MGM's own SEC filing does not state the initial-access method or name the actors, so the specific reported impersonation sequence is press attribution rather than a company confirmation [18][19].

  • MGM estimated a negative impact of approximately $100 million to Adjusted Property EBITDAR and reported less than $10 million in one-time expenses at that point [18].
  • The filing says personal information was obtained for some customers who had transacted before March 2019, and that bank-account and payment-card information were not obtained [18].
  • No CVE. Nothing was exploited — the account-recovery process was.

Case file Target, 2013 — a contractor's credentials, and 40 million cards

Attackers entered Target's network using network credentials stolen from Fazio Mechanical Services, Target's HVAC and refrigeration contractor. They then placed card-stealing malware on point-of-sale systems and moved the stolen data out to external drop servers. Reporting attributes the credential theft to a malware-bearing phishing email; the readable account confirms the stolen vendor credentials as the entry, and establishes phishing less firmly [21][22].

  • Approximately 40 million debit and credit card accounts were exposed [21].
  • Target's 2015 filing is reported to show $252 million in cumulative breach expenses, offset by $90 million of insurance, for a net $162 million [23].
  • The control failure was not a missing patch. It was a third party's credentials reaching payment systems that should have been unreachable from that network path.

On MFA fatigue, the guidance is specific. CISA's October 2022 guidance strongly urges organisations to implement phishing-resistant MFA, and says that where an organisation cannot yet do that and uses push notifications, it should enable number matching as an interim mitigation. CISA is explicit that number matching is not as strong as phishing-resistant MFA, and identifies FIDO/WebAuthn as the only widely available phishing-resistant option [16][17].

Why “just train users” is not a control

Training helps, but it competes against a professional whose entire job is this one message, delivered at scale, often during a real business process — an invoice, a delivery notice, an unexpected sign-in alert. The failure rate only needs to be non-zero once. Treat user judgement as a detection signal, never as the barrier: report-phishing buttons, a culture where reporting is rewarded, and above all technical controls — phishing-resistant MFA, email authentication enforcement, and verification procedures on the help desk — are what actually close the path.

What stops it

  • Phishing-resistant MFA — FIDO2/WebAuthn security keys and passkeys. A password typed into a fake page is useless when the factor is bound to the real origin.
  • Number matching as an interim step if push is still in use [16].
  • Help-desk identity verification with out-of-band challenge questions and a callback to a directory-listed number — and hard limits on who can reset an administrator.
  • DMARC at p=reject, SPF and DKIM aligned on your own domains, and rules that quarantine inbound failures.
  • Conditional access that considers device posture and location, so a stolen session from an unusual context is challenged.
  • Separate the third party. Vendor and contractor access should be its own identity plane, least-privilege, with no path to the payment environment — the Target lesson [21].

Level 4 · Exploiting an exposed service

4

When the software itself can be turned against you

MITRE ATT&CK T1190 Exploit Public-Facing Application · Remote code execution, injection, deserialisation

This is the level most people picture. It is also the one where the discipline of patching and inventory decides the outcome, because the flaw is almost always public: a vendor advisory, a CVE, and a fix that was available before the attack. The 2024 Verizon DBIR recorded exploitation of vulnerabilities as the initial point of entry in 14% of breaches, up 180% year over year [4].

Case file Equifax, 2017 — one unpatched Apache Struts CVE-2017-5638

Equifax's own statement is unambiguous about the vector: the attack “occurred through a vulnerability in Apache Struts (CVE-2017-5638),” affecting the web application supporting its US online dispute portal [8]. The flaw allowed remote code execution on the server.

  • Equifax initially reported information affecting 143 million US consumers [8]. The FTC later described the breach as affecting approximately 147 million people, including 145.5 million Social Security numbers and 209,000 payment-card numbers [9].
  • The FTC settlement was at least $575 million, and potentially up to $700 million — a settlement figure, not a measure of total economic loss [9].
  • A frequently repeated detail — an expired TLS inspection certificate that hid outbound exfiltration traffic — is a monitoring and visibility failure, not the initial-access vector. The vector was the Struts flaw. The expired certificate is why months of stolen traffic may not have been inspected [10].

#   • the database user's privileges (make it least-privilege too)

Log4Shell was this same idea, everywhere at once. Log4j 2 could resolve JNDI lookups while substituting values into a log message, so any attacker-controlled string that the application happened to log could be evaluated. CISA's description: “The vulnerability exists in the action the Java Naming and Directory Interface (JNDI) takes to resolve variables,” in versions that did “not protect against adversary-controlled” JNDI endpoints. Affected range: Log4j 2.0-beta9 through 2.14.1, disclosed 10 December 2021 [31]. CISA said Java's ubiquity, ease of exploitation and the cost of mitigation made it uniquely severe, noting that Log4j had been incorporated into “thousands of products worldwide” [31][32].

Case file WannaCry, 2017 — a worm that needed one missing patch MS17-010

WannaCry's dropper spread through SMBv1 using the EternalBlue exploit associated with MS17-010. CISA's alert describes the dropper “propagating via the MS17-010/EternalBlue SMBv1.0 exploit”: it scanned local-network ranges for SMB on TCP 445, exploited vulnerable Windows systems, and dropped the ransomware component. It also checked a hard-coded kill-switch domain — a successful connection stopped the dropper, while failure to connect let infection continue [27][28].

  • MS17-010 is Microsoft's security bulletin, not a CVE. It covers CVE-2017-0143 through CVE-2017-0148; EternalBlue is commonly associated with CVE-2017-0144 [28].
  • CISA reported infections in more than 150 countries [27].
  • In England, the National Audit Office found at least 81 of 236 NHS trusts affected, along with 603 other NHS organisations. 6,912 cancelled appointments were identified, and NHS England estimated more than 19,000 were cancelled in total. The NAO said it believed no patient data were compromised or stolen [29b].
  • The patch existed. The failure was that a worm-reachable, unpatched protocol was still enabled on systems nobody had finished updating.
remove the reachable surface, not just the bugpowershell
# Disable SMBv1 entirely — the protocol EternalBlue depended on.
Disable-WindowsOptionalFeature -Online -FeatureName smb1protocol -NoRestart

# And make the old exploit surface unreachable from where it does not belong.
Set-SmbServerConfiguration -EnableSMB1Protocol $false -Force

# Verify it is actually gone:
Get-SmbServerConfiguration | Select-Object EnableSMB1Protocol
How bad was Log4Shell, in numbers you can actually cite

There is no authoritative count of every vulnerable system worldwide. The most-quoted figures are dated scan snapshots, not a census. Shadowserver, scanning from a seed list of roughly 300 million known responsive IPs, reported:

  • 13,294 vulnerable internet-facing unique IP addresses across 109 countries, from data collected 11–13 December 2021 [33].
  • A follow-up from data collected 16–22 December found 8,472 unique internet-facing IP addresses hosting 10,951 instances — one IP could expose more than one instance [34].

Those are detected internet-facing hosts. They say nothing about internal systems, embedded copies, or organisations that never appeared in a scan — which is exactly why the patch took months across large estates.

What stops it

  • Know what you run. Software bills of materials and dependency inventory turn a surprise CVE into a query you can already answer.
  • Patch on a clock, not on a mood: internet-facing criticals on an hours-to-days SLA, with a hard exception process.
  • Fix the class, not the instance. Parameterised queries, safe deserialisation, no shell=True on user input — so the next injection cannot land.
  • Reduce the reachable surface. Retire SMBv1, close unused ports, keep management planes off the internet.
  • Egress filtering. Most of these exploits need to call out afterwards; a network that cannot reach arbitrary hosts breaks the chain.

Level 5 · Escalate and move

5

One foothold is not the goal — the domain is

MITRE ATT&CK T1003 OS Credential Dumping · T1550.002 Pass the Hash · TA0008 Lateral Movement

A single compromised workstation is worth little. The next stage is to turn it into the keys to everything: steal credentials from memory and disk, find accounts with more privilege, and move between systems using valid logins rather than exploits. This is why defenders describe lateral movement as the quiet part of an intrusion — a legitimate credential and a native administration tool look almost exactly like an administrator working late.

Pass-the-hash, in one sentence. MITRE defines it as authenticating without the user's cleartext password: the attacker uses a stolen password hash directly, skipping every step that would require the plaintext. It is classified as T1550.002, under Lateral Movement [46].

MITRE defines lateral movement broadly — entering and controlling remote systems on a network — and notes that attackers explore and pivot through multiple systems or accounts to reach their objective, and that legitimate credentials and native tools can be stealthier than installing new remote-access software [47]. In practice the tooling is often already on the victim's machine: management interfaces, remote shells, scheduled tasks and administration consoles.

The escalation chain, described generically

Once on a host, the standard progression is: situational awareness (who is logged in, what groups they hold, which systems trust this one), local privilege escalation (misconfigured services, over-permissive directories, unquoted paths, missing kernel hardening), credential access (memory, cached logons, configuration files, secrets managers, browser stores — MITRE's T1003 OS Credential Dumping [11]), then lateral movement using those credentials. Each step is chosen to need no malware on disk, which is precisely what makes endpoint-only defences insufficient.

What stops it

  • Assume breach, segment hard. Tiered administration with separate hosts and credentials for domain control — no administrator browsing the web from a domain controller.
  • Protect credential material at the endpoint: Credential Guard, LSA protection, and removal of cached administrator credentials from workstations.
  • Prefer Kerberos; make NTLM an exception you can explain, and monitor for the exceptions.
  • Privileged Access Management with just-in-time, time-boxed elevation instead of standing admin rights.
  • Build the detection. The Sigma rule above is a starting point; the real value is a baseline that knows what your normal looks like.
  • Restrict lateral paths: host firewalls, SMB/RPC scoping, and eliminating “everyone can reach everything.”

Level 6 · Persist, beacon, exfiltrate

6

Blending in, and calling home on a schedule

MITRE ATT&CK T1071 Application Layer Protocol · T1219 Remote Access Software · T1195 Supply Chain Compromise

A compromised host periodically contacts an attacker-controlled server — beaconing — to check in or receive instructions. The traffic frequently rides ordinary protocols such as HTTPS, DNS or email so that it looks like normal business traffic, and MITRE notes that commands and often their results can be embedded in application-layer protocol traffic. Malware commonly uses a “reverse connection or back-connect” to an adversary-controlled system [48][49][50].

hunting the heartbeat — regularity is the tellsql
-- Over Zeek/Suricata connection logs: find hosts that talk to the same
-- peer on a machine-regular cadence, in modest volume, for a long time.
SELECT id_orig_h, id_resp_h,
       count(*)                  AS sessions,
       stddev(interval_seconds) AS jitter_seconds
FROM (
  SELECT id_orig_h, id_resp_h, ts,
         ts - lag(ts) OVER (
              PARTITION BY id_orig_h, id_resp_h ORDER BY ts
         ) AS interval_seconds
  FROM conn
) t
WHERE interval_seconds IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY id_orig_h, id_resp_h
HAVING sessions > 200
   AND jitter_seconds < 5      -- humans do not produce this; schedulers do
ORDER BY sessions DESC;

Case file SolarWinds / SUNBURST, 2020 — the backdoor in a trusted update

The most instructive supply-chain case on record. Attackers compromised SolarWinds' software build process and inserted the SUNBURST backdoor into the signed SolarWinds.Orion.Core.BusinessLayer.dll distributed in Orion updates. CISA's advisory describes the adversary adding “a malicious version of the binary … into the SolarWinds software lifecycle,” which was “signed by the legitimate SolarWinds code signing certificate” [12].

  • SUNBURST then waited roughly 12–14 days before doing anything, generated victim-specific DNS requests under its own domain, and used the DNS responses to direct selected victims to command-and-control infrastructure — disguising traffic as legitimate Orion activity [14].
  • SolarWinds later identified SUNSPOT as the malware that injected SUNBURST into the build environment; follow-on tooling included TEARDROP, a memory-only dropper, and Cobalt Strike BEACON [14].
  • GAO reported that nearly 18,000 customers received a compromised update, while the actor targeted a smaller subset of high-value victims, including government organisations. CISA documented compromises across US government, critical-infrastructure and private-sector organisations; the US government attributed the activity to Russia's SVR [13][12].
  • Note what is absent: no CVE identifies the backdoor itself. This was malicious code inserted into a build, not exploitation of one specific Orion flaw — which is exactly why a patch-and-scan approach does not answer it.

Supply chain is now a mainstream problem, not an exotic one. The 2025 Verizon DBIR put third-party involvement at 30% of breaches — double the prior year's 15% [3]. You can be perfect and still be breached through a vendor you trust.

What stops it

  • Egress visibility and control. Know what your servers call, where, and on what schedule. Beaconing needs a destination.
  • Baseline first, alerts second. Detection rules that cannot say what normal is produce noise, not findings.
  • Watch the build pipeline as production. Signing infrastructure, build agents and release artefacts deserve the same rigour as the data centre.
  • Vendor risk with teeth: least-privilege integration, ability to revoke, and a plan for “what if this update is hostile?” — including staged deployment.
  • DNS as a sensor. Long or high-entropy queries and brand-new domains in outbound traffic are cheap, high-signal detections.

Level 7 · Impact and payday

7

What the access is actually worth — and to whom

MITRE ATT&CK TA0040 Impact · Ransomware, double and triple extortion, fraud, data sale

Access is a means, not the end. The business models that convert it are fairly few, and they shape the attacker's behaviour throughout the earlier levels: a ransomware operator wants breadth (many hosts, backups, domain control), a data broker wants volume and uniqueness, and a fraud crew wants quiet persistence — the longer nobody notices, the more the access pays.

Case file Colonial Pipeline, 2021 — one unused login, a fuel shortage

A US congressional hearing record states plainly: “We know hackers exploited an unprotected VPN account that was no longer in use” to reach Colonial Pipeline's network, and that the pipeline was shut down for nearly a week [11b]. The account did not require a one-time passcode. The network was hit with DarkSide ransomware. The claim that the password specifically came from a leaked password dump is reported rather than established in the official record [11b].

  • The 5,500-mile pipeline was shut down for nearly a week [11b].
  • Colonial paid a ransom reported as $4.4 million; the Department of Justice later announced it had seized a substantial portion of the cryptocurrency paid to the DarkSide extortionists [11b][11c].
  • No CVE. The documented weaknesses were a legacy, inactive VPN account and the absence of a second factor — levels 1 and 2, not level 4.

On ransoms, the data is counter-intuitive. The 2025 DBIR put the median amount actually paid to ransomware groups at $115,000, down from $150,000 the prior year, and reported that 64% of victim organisations did not pay at all [3]. Paying is also not a data-protection measure: in double extortion, the leak threat survives the payment.

Case file Code Spaces, 2014 — the company that did not survive

A short and brutal case about where the real single point of failure lives. After a DDoS attack began, an unauthorised person gained access to Code Spaces' Amazon EC2 control panel and attempted extortion. Code Spaces reported that when it tried to regain control by changing passwords, the attacker used backup logins already created for the AWS panel and deleted cloud resources — EBS snapshots, S3 buckets, AMIs, instances and backups. The company said it could not continue operating and shut down [24].

  • Most customer repositories and backups, and all virtual machines, were lost; Code Spaces concluded it had “no alternative but to cease trading” [24].
  • The contemporaneous account does not establish how the attacker first obtained AWS access — so this page does not claim it either [24].
  • The lesson: backups held in the same control plane as the systems they protect are not backups. An attacker who owns the console owns the recovery too.
restore.sh — the only control that reliably answers ransomwarebash
#!/usr/bin/env bash
set -euo pipefail

# 3-2-1-1-0: three copies, two media, one offsite, one IMMUTABLE,
# and ZERO unverified restores.
# Object Lock in COMPLIANCE mode cannot be shortened or deleted —
# not even by an administrator, and therefore not by the attacker
# who is now an administrator. That is the entire point.
aws s3api put-object-retention \
  --bucket acme-backups \
  --key "db/nightly-${STAMP}.dump.gz" \
  --retention "Mode=COMPLIANCE,RetainUntilDate=${LOCK_UNTIL}"

# A backup you have not restored is a hope, not a backup.
# Prove it on a timer, into a scratch database, and record the result.
pg_restore --clean --if-exists -d "$SCRATCH_DB" "/restore/nightly-${STAMP}.dump"
psql "$SCRATCH_DB" -c "SELECT count(*) FROM orders;" > /var/log/restore-proof.txt

What stops it

  • Immutable, offline backups with retention that an attacker holding domain admin rights cannot alter — and tested restores with a target recovery time.
  • Network segmentation for OT: business IT and industrial control should not share a fate, as Colonial's shutdown demonstrated.
  • Kill dormant access. The Colonial VPN account was inactive. Inactive accounts are pure liability.
  • Payment-process controls — out-of-band verification for changes to bank details, and dual approval for large transfers.
  • A rehearsed incident response plan, including legal, communications and regulator notification, decided before the incident rather than during it.

Level 8 · Nation-state operations

8

Zero-days, supply chains, and crossing the air gap

MITRE ATT&CK T1195 Supply Chain Compromise · T1190 · Purpose-built tooling with nation-state resources

At the top of the ladder the techniques stop being commoditised and start being bespoke: multiple unpublished vulnerabilities in one operation, custom malware for a single industrial target, and patience measured in years. And yet the most important lesson from these cases is that they still end up relying on something mundane — a signed build, a USB stick, a misconfiguration — to reach the target.

Case file Stuxnet — reaching a network that had no internet

Stuxnet targeted Siemens SIMATIC WinCC / STEP 7 engineering environments. On a matching system it could alter the logic controlling industrial equipment; its centrifuge-related payload manipulated frequency-converter commands — and therefore motor speeds — while attempting to conceal the changes from operators [15].

  • CISA: “USB drives appear to be a primary infection mechanism,” though Stuxnet could also spread via network shares and SQL databases [15]. Removable media was the bridge across the air gap.
  • CISA describes four Windows zero-days plus one previously patched Windows flaw: CVE-2010-2568 (Windows Shell shortcut/LNK handling, enabling propagation via a crafted LNK on removable media), CVE-2010-2729 (Print Spooler), CVE-2010-2743 (kernel keyboard layout, local privilege escalation) and CVE-2010-2744 (kernel window class, local privilege escalation). The previously patched flaw was MS08-067 / CVE-2008-4250 [15].
  • Keep the numbers separate: CVE-2010-2772, a hard-coded password in Siemens WinCC/PCS 7, is not one of the four Windows zero-days [15].
  • On impact, be precise. The Institute for Science and International Security reported that Iran decommissioned and replaced about 1,000 IR-1 centrifuges at Natanz in late 2009 or early 2010 and found the timing and Stuxnet's sequences consistent with an effect on centrifuge operations — but also stated that Stuxnet's exact purpose and overall effect remain hard to assess. The ~1,000 figure is not proof that Stuxnet caused every replacement. CISA's own 2010 advisory reported that Siemens knew of infections but that, in those reported cases, none had adversely affected automation systems [15b][15].
  • ISIS on the route: “Because the Natanz control systems are not connected to the internet, Stuxnet would have needed to travel on a removable drive” [15b].

Counter-case Change Healthcare, 2024 — level 8 impact, level 2 cause

A useful anti-climax. UnitedHealth's chief executive told Congress the attack succeeded because a critical system wasn't using multifactor authentication; the reported route was compromised credentials used to remotely access a Change Healthcare Citrix portal [20]. The Citrix Bleed vulnerability (CVE-2023-4966) is frequently attached to this incident in commentary, but it is not established as the attack vector by the sources examined here — using Citrix remote access is not the same as exploiting Citrix Bleed.

  • UnitedHealth paid $22 million in Bitcoin, per the chief executive's testimony [20].
  • The affected-individual figure identified by HHS's Office for Civil Rights is approximately 192.7 million individuals [20b] — an early estimate of roughly a third of Americans given at the hearing was superseded by this larger figure.
  • The intrusion disrupted healthcare payment and services nationwide. The documented control failure, again, was missing MFA — not a zero-day.
“Air gap” is not a security boundary

An air gap removes one path, not the concept of attack. Removable media, maintenance laptops, vendor-supplied software updates, wireless peripherals and supply-chain firmware all cross isolation boundaries in ordinary operation. If a system is genuinely isolated, the honest questions are: what physically enters it, who carries it, and how do we know that carrier is clean? Those are process controls, and they fail the way process controls fail — because someone was in a hurry. Treat isolation as a reduction in likelihood with a documented residual risk, not as an axiom of safety.

What stops it

  • OT-specific defences: unidirectional gateways, network monitoring inside the control network, and strictly controlled removable media with scanning kiosks.
  • Vendor and build integrity: verify supply-chain provenance, pin and attest artefacts, and isolate build infrastructure.
  • Detect the exotic with the fundamentals. SUNBURST was found by noticing unusual outbound activity, not by antivirus [14].
  • Tabletop the doomsday case. The organisations that responded well to nation-state intrusions had already planned for them.
  • Back to basics, deliberately. As Change Healthcare shows, the expensive threat frequently arrives through the cheapest door — so MFA on every remote access path is still the highest-yield control on this page.

Anatomy of one campaign Composite

Each level above is real, but intrusions arrive as a chain. Below is a composite walkthrough built from the documented mechanics in these case files — a realistic sequence, with the detection points a defender could have used at each step.

  • Day 0 — level 0 The estate is mapped from public sources Subdomains, certificate logs, job adverts naming products, and a stale staging host still reachable from the internet. Nothing is broken; everything is measured.
  • Day 9 — level 2 A valid password arrives from an infostealer log A contractor's personal laptop was infected months earlier. Credentials harvested from that machine included one that is still valid here — the Snowflake pattern [37].
  • Day 9 — level 3 MFA is satisfied, not defeated Push prompts are triggered repeatedly until the victim approves one to make them stop. Number matching would have broken this; a FIDO2 key would have made it impossible [16].
  • Days 10–24 — level 5 Quiet escalation and lateral movement The foothold is a workstation. Credentials are collected from memory and configuration files, and logons move server-to-server using those credentials — which is why the log evidence looks like administration, not intrusion [46][47].
  • Days 12–40 — level 6 A heartbeat, and a slow exfiltration One host contacts one address on a machine-regular cadence. Data leaves in modest, unremarkable chunks. That regularity is the single strongest signal available to the defender.
  • Day 41 — level 7 Backups are found and destroyed, then the extortion begins This is where immutable, tested backups stop being a policy line and become the difference between an outage and an existential event [3].

Notice where the failures were. Not one step in this chain required a zero-day. The breaches in this guide that did involve exotic capability — Stuxnet, SolarWinds — were nation-state operations. Every case with ordinary criminal or hacktivist motivation was won with a reused password, a prompt approved out of fatigue, an unpatched service, or a system with no second factor.

Defenses that actually hold

Read across the eight levels and a pattern appears: the controls that end incidents early are unglamorous, and almost all of them are identity and exposure management. NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, published 26 February 2024, is a useful way to organise them. Its six functions are Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover — Govern being the addition in version 2.0 — and the framework was broadened from critical infrastructure to apply to all organisations [5][6].

FunctionThe controls that map to the case files above
Govern Named risk ownership; a patch SLA with teeth; third-party risk treated as your risk (Target [21]); supply-chain expectations for vendors (SolarWinds [12]).
Identify An inventory of every internet-facing asset and every software dependency (Log4Shell [31]); a register of privileged and dormant accounts — the Colonial VPN account was inactive [11b].
Protect Phishing-resistant MFA on every path, including remote access (Change Healthcare [20]); least privilege and tiered administration; no long-lived secrets (Uber [25]); network segmentation, especially around payment and OT systems.
Detect Authentication logging that is actually monitored (level 1); egress and DNS visibility for beaconing (level 6); privileged-logon baselines for lateral movement (level 5); integrity monitoring on build pipelines.
Respond A rehearsed plan with pre-agreed legal, communications and regulator steps; a decision framework for ransom that assumes double extortion (level 7).
Recover Immutable, offline backups; tested restores with a defined recovery time; an understanding of which processes are manual and which simply stop.

The six controls that would have ended most of this guide

Highest yield per unit of effort

  • Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/WebAuthn) on remote access, admin consoles, code hosts and cloud. Number matching as the interim step. This alone breaks levels 2 and 3 [16][17].
  • Eliminate dormant and standing privilege. Kill inactive accounts, remove standing admin rights, rotate long-lived credentials, and scope service accounts.
  • Short patch SLAs for internet-facing systems, supported by real inventory and an SBOM so you know what you are running when the next Log4Shell lands.
  • Immutable, offline, tested backups. Not configured — restored, on a timer, into a scratch environment, with the result recorded.
  • Write software that fails safe. Parameterised queries, no shell interpolation of user input, safe deserialisation, secure defaults, and secrets that are never long-lived. CISA's Secure by Design guidance frames this as shifting the burden off the customer [7].
  • Log and look. Almost every case here left evidence. Detection rarely failed for lack of data; it failed for want of attention to data that already existed.

A note on reading breach numbers. Headline figures describe different things. 23andMe's 6.9 million was profiles reachable through about 14,000 compromised accounts, not 6.9 million logins [43]. AT&T's filing says “nearly all wireless customers” and gives no total; the ~109 million figure is a reported count of accounts, not call records [38][39]. Equifax's $575–700 million was a settlement, not total loss [9]. Precision matters, because imprecise numbers produce imprecise risk decisions.

Scope & intent. This page is defensive security education. It explains how real intrusions work so that systems can be built and operated to resist them — it deliberately contains no working exploits, no target lists, and no operational instructions against anyone. Code appearing here is either illustrative pseudocode describing a mechanism, a detection rule for defenders, or a vulnerable-versus-fixed pair used to teach a class of bug. The AWS key values shown are AWS's own published documentation examples.

Caveats, stated plainly. Where a detail is reported by the press rather than confirmed by an official filing, advisory or company statement, the text says so — including the Colonial Pipeline password-dump origin, MGM's help-desk sequence, and Target's phishing-to-vendor chain. Citrix Bleed (CVE-2023-4966) is not established as the Change Healthcare vector. Stuxnet's ~1,000 centrifuge figure is an inference from timing, not proof of causation. Several figures (AT&T's ~109 million, Equifax's 147 million, Change Healthcare's 192.7 million) come from sources that could not be fully re-read during preparation and are attributed rather than quoted. Breach statistics describe reported datasets and are not a census of all incidents.
