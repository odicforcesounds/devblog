01The rule that changes everything

Waste is not a property of an object. It is the moment someone stops imagining a use for it. That is good news, because imagination is cheap — and the order in which you apply it decides whether you are solving a problem or just moving it.

  1. Refuse — the only free option

    The bag, the straw, the second plastic tray, the free T-shirt at an event. Every object refused costs nothing to store, sort, clean or feel guilty about. This is the only rung with no downstream work.

  2. Reduce — use less of it, for longer

    A bigger jar of yoghurt, a refill bottle, a repair instead of a replacement. Reduction is boring and it is where most of the kilos actually disappear.

  3. Reuse and repair — the interesting middle

    Jars, crates, clothes, furniture, electronics. This manual lives here: the second life that keeps the material in service without melting it down.

  4. Rot — let biology do the work

    Food scraps and garden clippings are not waste, they are next year's soil. Composting is the cheapest transformation available to a household, and the one most often skipped.

  5. Recycle — the last resort that looks like the first

    Recycling destroys the object to recover the material. It needs energy, clean streams and a market. It is good, and it is fourth best — which is why a page about it should not start there.

What you gain

  • Money that stays in the house: a jar, a crate and a repaired lamp are things you no longer buy.
  • Better bins: less smell, fewer flies, no liquid at the bottom, half the volume.
  • Skills that transfer — cutting, sewing, composting, wiring a plug are the same skills at 60 as at 6.
  • A garden that feeds itself instead of importing bags of soil.
  • A reason to know your neighbours: the person with pallets, the café with buckets, the man who fixes toasters.

What it costs

  • Time, and the honest admission that ten minutes of labour can be worth less than a €2 object.
  • Storage: the fatal temptation is a garage full of "someday". Every project needs a deadline or it is hoarding with better vocabulary.
  • Standards: some second lives are not safe — food contact, children's toys, electrical work, anything structural.
  • Aesthetic risk: visible mending is beautiful, a shelf made of three different woods is a shelf.
  • The uncomfortable part: recycling is an industry with losses, and much of what you sort is burned or shipped.
The scale, in four numbers. The average person in the EU generated 517 kg of municipal waste in 2024, and 48 % of it was recycled or composted. Biowaste is the single largest fraction of that bin at 28.8 % — food and garden waste, the part a household can transform without any industry at all. Globally, 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022, about 19 % of everything available to consumers, and food loss and waste produce 8–10 % of greenhouse-gas emissions. You cannot compost your way out of a supply chain. You can, however, remove a third of your own bin and half its smell this month.

The three questions before you keep anything

A house is not a warehouse. Answer all three out loud, because a "maybe" is a no.

  • Do I have a use, today? Not a use in the abstract: this shelf, this window, this person's birthday.
  • Do I have the other half? A jar without a lid, a lamp without a cable, a frame without glass — the missing part is the project.
  • Is it safe for that use? Food, children, heat, electricity and load-bearing are the five things you cannot improvise.

How to read the result

The EU average is 517 kg per person per year. Under 300 means you are already unusual; over 600 means volume, not guilt, is the place to start. But the number that matters is the third one: the organic share is the only stream a household can process at home, without markets, trucks or a single kilowatt-hour.

if organic wins

Build the compost, then the garden

A balcony bokashi bin handles 15 litres a month and takes no space. A worm bin handles a family's peels, and gives back the only fertiliser you will need for pots.

if packaging wins

Change the shop, not the bin

Packaging is decided at the shelf. Two refill stops, one bigger format and a market with loose produce do more than a year of folding cartons perfectly.

if textiles win

Open a repair basket

A box with needle, thread, patches and a spare button turns "I have nothing to wear" into twenty minutes. The rest becomes rags, then wicks, then compost-adjacent dust.

04The kitchen: five lives before the bin

A kitchen throws away food, water and packaging in the same gesture. These five habits catch the first two, cost nothing, and are the ones people keep doing after the novelty wears off.

20 minutes + 2 weeks · €0

Citrus-peel vinegar cleaner

The peels you were going to bin become a degreaser strong enough for the stove.

  • Peels of 3–4 oranges, lemons or limes
  • 1 L of white vinegar (the cheap one)
  • A jar with a lid
  1. Pack the peels into the jar and cover with vinegar.
  2. Leave it two weeks in a cupboard, shaking when you remember.
  3. Strain, keep the liquid, dilute half and half with water for surfaces.
Never on marble, limestone or unsealed stone — the acid eats the surface. Never mixed with bleach.
5 minutes · 1–3 weeks

Regrow from the end

Spring onion, leek, celery, lettuce hearts and carrot tops will push new leaves from a centimetre of water.

  • The root end, 2–3 cm, still attached
  • A shallow glass or a saucer
  • Light, but not direct summer sun
  1. Stand the end in 1 cm of water, cut side up.
  2. Change the water every day — stagnant water is what rots it.
  3. Cut what you need, twice at most, then give the rest to the compost.
The second growth is thinner and less flavoured: this is a pleasure, not a food supply.
40 minutes · freezer

Scrap stock

Onion skins, carrot peel, celery leaves, herb stems, a parmesan rind: a litre of stock from what was already in the bin.

  • A bag of peels kept in the freezer until it is full
  • Cold water to cover, a bay leaf, ten peppercorns
  • Salt, added only at the end
  1. Cover the scraps with cold water and bring to a bare simmer.
  2. Thirty to forty minutes, never a hard boil — boiling makes it cloudy and bitter.
  3. Strain, salt, cool fast, freeze in jars with 2 cm of headspace.
No mouldy scraps, no potato leaves or green sprouts (solanine), no cabbage in quantity. Four days in the fridge, three months frozen.
10 minutes · 150 °C

Stale bread, three ways

Bread does not go off, it goes hard — and hard bread is an ingredient.

  • Croutons: cubes, oil, salt, 15 minutes at 150 °C
  • Pangrattato: blitz with garlic and parsley, toast in a pan
  • Breadcrumbs: blitz dry and freeze in a bag
  1. Cut or tear before it turns to stone; a dry loaf grates into crumbs in a minute.
  2. Dry it fully before storing, or the bag grows mould.
Mould is not a crust problem: the toxins survive toasting. Mouldy bread goes to the compost, never to the oven.
0 minutes · daily

Coffee grounds

A café gives away kilos of them. They are a green compost input, a mild abrasive and a deodoriser — in that order of usefulness.

  • Compost: a thin layer, never a solid cake — they mat and go anaerobic
  • Scrub: with oil and sugar, for hands and sinks
  • Fridge: a cup in the door absorbs smells for a week
Skip the old myth about acid-loving plants: spent grounds are nearly neutral. Do not dump them down the sink.
Food safety, in one line each. Cool leftovers fast and shallow. Reheat once. Anything mouldy goes to compost, not to "cut off the bad bit". Rice, pasta and reheated meat are the usual suspects, not the leftovers you can smell.

05The garden: compost is chemistry with a smell

Composting fails for one of three reasons: too wet, too dry, or the wrong balance of carbon to nitrogen. The first two are visible. The third is the one this calculator fixes.

Browns : greens

The target is about 25–30 parts carbon to one part nitrogen, which in practice is two to three volumes of browns for one of greens. Greens are wet and Nitrogen-rich: peels, coffee, grass, fresh leaves. Browns are dry and Carbon-rich: cardboard, egg boxes, dry leaves, straw, sawdust from untreated wood, toilet-roll tubes.

fast

Hot compost, one cubic metre

Build the pile in one go, three parts browns to one of greens, damp as a wrung sponge. It reaches 55–65 °C in three days; turn it when it cools, and it is finished in six to ten weeks.

small space

Bokashi, on a balcony

A sealed bucket with bran inoculated with lactic bacteria ferments everything, including fish and cheese, in two weeks. It is not compost: bury the result 20 cm deep, or feed it to a worm bin.

with worms

Worm bin, indoors

A plastic box, shredded cardboard, a handful of composting worms, and peels buried under the bedding. No smell when it is balanced; citrus, onion and cooked food in quantity upset it.

What never goes in a home compost: meat, fish and dairy in an open pile (bokashi is the exception), pet faeces, glossy or thermal paper, treated wood, "compostable" plastic unless your council accepts it, and anything that has been in contact with pesticide. A compost heap is not a furnace: it does not sterilise.

The free soil amendments you already own

Leaf mould is the slowest and best thing in this section: a wire ring, wet leaves, a year, and you have a seed-sowing compost that costs nothing. Eggshells need crushing and years to break down — they are a slow calcium bank, not a quick fix. Wood ash is potash, useful in small amounts on soil, never on blueberries or acid-lovers, and never from painted or treated wood.

06Water, used twice

A litre that has washed a vegetable is not dirty, it is displaced. The trick is to move it once, by hand or by gravity, and to know what must not go with it.

garden

Greywater to a mulch basin

Bath or shower water, still warm, poured into a shallow basin dug around a tree and covered with wood chips. The soil filters it; the plant drinks it. No tank, no pump, no smell.

kitchen

Vegetable washing water

The bowl you rinsed the lettuce in goes on the houseplants or the balcony pots. One bowl a day is 100 litres a year that never reaches the drain.

cooking

Pasta, rice and egg water

Cooled, unsalted cooking water carries starch that feeds soil life. Salted water goes to the drain, not the pots — a few years of it turns a pot salty.

free

Air-conditioner condensate

A split unit in a Portuguese summer makes litres a day at almost zero conductivity. Perfect for the garden, not for drinking, and not for misting anything you breathe near.

Greywater rules that are not negotiable:

  • No kitchen sink water with grease, no dishwasher water, no water from nappies or toilets.
  • No bleach, borax or fabric softener — they kill the soil you are trying to feed.
  • Use it the same day, below the surface, and never on leaves you will eat raw.
  • Not on root vegetables, not on seedlings, not in a drip system that will clog with soap.

07Textiles: repair first, then remake

Clothes are the most emotionally loaded object in the bin. They are also the easiest to keep: a needle, a patch and twenty minutes restore most of what people throw away. Less than 1 % of the material used to make clothes is recycled into new clothes, so the second life you give a shirt is not a small thing.

20 minutes · €0

The repair basket

One box by the sofa turns mending from a project into a habit.

  • Needles, two threads (dark and light), small scissors
  • A tin of spare buttons, saved from old shirts
  • Iron-on patches, or denim offcuts for knee repairs
  1. Mend while the hole is small: a stitch now, a replacement later.
  2. Reinforce the corners of a pocket with a triangle of stitches before they tear.
  3. Visible mending is a style, not a confession — sashiko lines look deliberate because they are.
15 minutes · no sewing

The T-shirt bag

A single shirt becomes a strong, washable bag for the market.

  • One old T-shirt, the thicker the better
  • Scissors
  1. Cut off the sleeves and widen the neck into handles.
  2. Cut a fringe along the hem, 8 cm deep, front and back.
  3. Tie each front fringe to the matching back fringe, two knots, and pull tight.
It holds 5 kg of shopping. It does not hold glass bottles without a lining.
a weekend · a craft

The rag rug

Every torn sheet and worn shirt becomes weft. The Portuguese version — trapos — is older than the recycling bin.

  • Old fabric cut into 3 cm strips
  • A hessian sack or a cheap rug base
  • A crochet hook or a prodding tool
  1. Work from the centre out, rows 3 cm apart, pushing each strip through and knotting it.
  2. Mix colours deliberately: two tones of the same family read as a design.
Use cotton and linen; synthetic fleece sheds fibres every wash and does not felt.
When a textile is truly finished: cut it into rags first (kitchen, workshop, paint), then use the rags as plant ties or wicks, then take what is left to a textile bank — not the paper bin. Never put textiles in a home compost unless they are pure wool or cotton cut into small pieces; a polyester label will still be there in a decade.

08Paper and cardboard: the quiet material

Cardboard is the most useful free material in a city and the least respected. It is a carbon source for compost, a weed barrier, a seed pot, a firelighter and a prototype — and it is recyclable at the end of all of that.

propagation

Seed pots from tubes

Toilet-roll tubes and newspaper rolls make deep pots for beans, sweet peas and tomatoes, which hate root disturbance. Plant the whole tube: it rots in the soil.

weed control

Sheet mulch

Plain brown cardboard, tape and staples removed, overlapped 15 cm, wetted, covered with 10 cm of wood chips or compost. It smothers grass in a season and feeds worms underneath.

fire

Fire starters

Rolls stuffed with shredded paper and dipped in melted candle ends. One per fire, and the firelighters you stop buying are €3 a box.

office

Notebooks from misprints

Print on one side only and the other side becomes a notebook: fold, staple at the spine, cut the edges. A5 from A4, sixty pages, ten minutes.

Paper that must not be composted or mulched: thermal receipts (they are coated and often contain bisphenols), glossy magazines, pizza boxes soaked in grease, anything with plastic tape or laminate, and shredded paper from an office with a confidential-waste contract. Plain brown card, newspaper and office paper are fine.

09Glass and metal: containers that outlive us

Glass is endlessly recyclable and effectively eternal in a cupboard. Metal is the same, with sharper edges. Both reward a little patience and punish improvisation around food and heat.

pantry

Jars that match their lids

A shelf of identical jars with matching lids is better storage than anything you can buy: visible, airtight, stackable. Collect one brand's jars until the lids all fit, then stop collecting.

light

The jar lantern

A clean jar, a length of copper wire as a handle, a candle stub or a solar garden light inside. Twenty minutes, and it is the best-looking thing on a summer table.

garden

Tin-can planters

A large tin, holes punched in the base with a nail, a coat of paint outside: herbs on a wall. Rust is the only maintenance, and herbs do not mind it.

protection

Bottle cloches

The bottom half of a plastic bottle over a seedling is a mini greenhouse for €0. Lift it by day or the plant cooks; the cap off means ventilation.

Sharp things, honestly:

  • Cut glass with a proper cutter, oil, and gloves; fire-polish edges or sand them wet. A cut bottle is a blade.
  • Tin cans: file the inside rim, and never use a can that held paint or chemicals for food or for a planter with edible roots.
  • Do not store acidic food in a rusty can or in aluminium that has been cut. Glass is for food; metal is for tools, planters and light.
  • Aerosol cans, gas cartridges and batteries are not craft material. They go to the ecocentro, closed.

10Wood and furniture: the strong second life

Wood is the one material where salvage beats buying on quality, not just on principle. Old pine from a drawer bottom is denser and drier than anything in a DIY shed.

structure

The pallet bench

One pallet, four castors, a cushion: a bench for a balcony in an afternoon. Sand the top, oil it, and check every board for cracks before it takes weight.

storage

Crates as shelving

Wine and fruit crates stack into a shelf that needs no tools, moves with you and can go back to the market. Fix them to the wall if children are in the house.

garden

Drawers as planters

A drawer with holes drilled in the base, lined with cardboard and filled with compost, is a salad bed for a wall or a windowsill. It lasts two or three seasons.

repair

The wobbly chair

Ninety per cent of wobbles are a loose joint: clean the old glue with a knife, warm the joint, add wood glue and clamp for a day. Stronger than new, and free.

The wood you must not take:

  • Pallets stamped MB (methyl bromide, a fumigant) — take only HT, heat-treated. The stamp is on the block, not the board.
  • Painted or varnished wood of unknown age: old paint can carry lead. Sanding it is the risk, not keeping it.
  • Railway sleepers, telegraph poles and anything green-tinted from pressure treatment — creosote and arsenic salts do not belong near food or compost.
  • MDF and chipboard that has been wet: it crumbles and the dust is not worth breathing.

11Electronics: the box you must not open

The most valuable waste in your house is also the most dangerous. E-waste contains copper, gold, aluminium and cobalt — and capacitors that hold a lethal charge long after the plug is out. The second life of a device is repair, resale, or a certified recycler. Not a screwdriver and a YouTube video, unless you know which part is which.

62 million tonnes. That is the world's e-waste in 2022, growing five times faster than the recycling that is documented — only about a fifth is formally collected and recycled. A phone in a drawer is not "saved"; it is a mine that has not been opened yet.
first

Repair before replacement

A repair café is the cheapest technician in Europe: volunteers, tools, coffee, and a good chance the toaster only needs a €2 switch. Thousands of them exist; the map is the first link in the sources.

cables

The cable cull

Every house has a bag of chargers for devices it no longer owns. Keep one 5 V USB supply, one USB-C cable, one micro-USB, label them, and take the rest to the ecocentro — or to a repair café's parts box.

second life

An old phone as a camera

A phone with a dead battery but a working camera becomes a time-lapse camera for seedlings, a kitchen recipe screen, or a baby monitor on the local network. Charge it through a timer; never leave a swollen battery charging overnight.

end

The correct exit

Retailers that sell electronics must take old ones back in Portugal. Batteries go to a pilhão; bulbs, cables and small appliances to the ecocentro. Never the mixed bin, never the garden, never a fire.

Never, under any circumstance:

  • Open a microwave, a CRT television or a power supply. The capacitors can hold a fatal charge for hours or days.
  • Puncture, bend or charge a swollen lithium battery. It is a fire in a plastic wrapper.
  • Burn cables to recover copper. The smoke is the poison; the copper is worth less than the hospital.
  • Try to recover gold at home with acids. It is an industrial process, and the waste needs an industrial route.

light

The bottle lamp

A clean bottle, a cord set with a switch, a bulb whose warmth suits the plastic — an E14 LED, never an incandescent. The lamp is the easy part; hiding the cable is the design.

mosaic

Broken tiles, new table

Construction skips give away broken tiles by the bag. Wrap them in a cloth and break with a hammer, set them in tile adhesive, grout, seal. Wear glasses.

gift

Kintsugi, the visible repair

The Japanese art of making the repair the feature: join broken pottery with lacquer and gold powder. True kintsugi is slow and expensive; the modern epoxy version is honest if you say so.

wall

Textile wall hanging

A branch, a dozen strips of worn fabric, one knot per strip. It absorbs sound, hides a bad wall and costs a walk to the park.

The one rule: a second life is decoration only if you would still like it in a year. Paint it, sand it, or leave it alone — but decide before you fill the house. Three upcycled objects look curated; thirty look like a skip with a rug.

Three rules for a business built on waste

  1. Charge for the transformation, not the material

    A pallet is free and a pallet bench is €120. Customers pay for the design, the finish and the certainty that it will not collapse. Never apologise for the material; never call it "just junk" on the label either.

  2. Keep the legal line visible

    Food contact, cosmetics, children's toys and electrical work are regulated. A soap made from used oil is a chemical product; a repaired lamp is an electrical appliance you are putting your name on. Invoice, declare, and insure what needs insuring.

  3. Sell the story once, the service always

    The "made from waste" line sells the first object. What brings people back is that the drawer closes, the bench holds, the compost does not smell. Reputation is the only durable asset in this trade.

Start with one service, not one product. A monthly repair morning at a neighbours' association needs a table, a sign and two hours. A compost collection for ten flats needs a bike, a bucket and a scale. Products need stock, photography and shipping; services need a calendar. The service is almost always the cheaper way to find out whether anyone wants what you can do.

14Sorting, without the guesswork

Portuguese bins are colour-coded and the rules are simple, but the mistakes are always the same six. One wrong item can spoil a whole lorry load — which is what "when in doubt, out" means in practice.

The streams at a Portuguese ecoponto, and what breaks them
Stream Goes in Never

Three habits that do more than sorting

  • Empty and rinse, do not wash. A yoghurt pot with a spoonful left in it is contamination; a pot with a trace of yoghurt is fine.
  • Flatten and cap. Cartons flattened, bottles with the cap on — a cap is recyclable and a loose cap is litter.
  • Never bag the recyclables. A bag inside a bin is treated as residual waste, even if it is full of clean plastic.
Deposit schemes are spreading across Europe, and the direction is clear: the container you return is worth money, the one you bin is not. Until a scheme reaches your street, the ecoponto is the best route — and the shop that sells the drink is often obliged to take the packaging back.

15Limits, dangers, and the hoarding trap

The failure mode of this whole manual is not a bad jar. It is a house that slowly becomes a warehouse of good intentions. These are the rules that keep a second life from becoming a first problem.

Do not

  • Burn plastic, treated wood, cables or rubbish in a garden fire. Dioxins are not a country smell.
  • Dump anything in a storm drain, a river bank or a empty lot. It comes back in the water.
  • Use food containers that held chemicals, whatever the label says after washing.
  • Store food in a can that is rusty or cut, or in unknown plastic.
  • Put batteries, aerosols or gas cartridges in any bin or fire.
  • Compost meat, fish, dairy or pet waste in an open pile — bokashi indoors is the controlled exception.

Before you keep it

  • The 30-day rule: if it has not been used in thirty days, it goes. Write the date on the box.
  • One box, one shelf, no more. When the box is full, the oldest thing in it leaves.
  • No "someday" materials without a named project and a weekend.
  • Never store flammable-soaked rags (oil, linseed, paint) indoors: they self-heat and ignite.
  • If a repair needs a tool you do not have, that is a repair café visit, not a purchase.
The invisible cost. Washing a synthetic fleece releases thousands of microfibres; the wastewater plant catches most, not all. Wash cold, less often, with a full load, and prefer natural fibres where the garment will be washed hard. Nobody is asking for a hair shirt — a cotton T-shirt worn two hundred times beats five recycled ones worn twenty.

When it is not a craft project:

  • Anything structural, load-bearing or holding a child: use rated, new material.
  • Anything mains-electrical beyond a plug or a lamp cord: a licensed electrician, not a tutorial.
  • Anything that touches food, lips or skin: glass, stainless steel, or food-grade plastic with a known history.
  • Anything with an unknown chemical history: the ecocentro, not the garden.
