Orientation: how this manual works

Read this before touching a single command. The manual is deliberately sequential: each chapter ends with a gate, and a gate passed is the only key to the next chapter.

Most networking study material is a pile of disconnected chapters. You watch a video about OSPF, read an article about VLANs, and three weeks later you cannot explain why a trunk failed. This manual is built the other way around: it is a dependency graph disguised as a book. Layer 1 problems are taught before Spanning Tree, Spanning Tree before trunk troubleshooting, and subnetting before routing. You do not get to jump to BGP because BGP is interesting.

Reading contract

Every chapter has three parts: the briefing (why the topic exists), the field procedure (how it is configured and verified on real Cisco equipment), and the knowledge gate (three questions, two must be correct). The gate is not a formality. If you cannot pass it, the next chapter will assume knowledge you do not have.

Who this is for

Three readers, one manual. The beginner has never configured a switch and needs the physical layer explained without hand-waving. The CCNA candidate needs the 200-301 blueprint covered with exam-accurate terminology and verification commands. The CCNP candidate needs the same topics at design depth: why a particular spanning-tree flavour, which First Hop Redundancy Protocol fits a pair of distribution switches, when a route reflector replaces a full iBGP mesh.

Every reader starts at Chapter 00. The early chapters are short. That is deliberate: they install the vocabulary the rest of the manual uses.

How to use the gates

  1. Read the chapter completely. Skim the code blocks even if you are not labbing yet; the commands become familiar before they become necessary.
  2. Answer without scrolling back. The gate is a retrieval test, not an open-book exercise. Retrieval is what builds durable memory.
  3. Read every explanation. After submission, each question shows why the right answer is right and, more usefully, why the distractors are wrong.
  4. Retry on failure. Nothing is locked permanently. The retry is the lesson.
  5. Do the field drill. Most chapters include a short lab or verification exercise. If you have no lab yet, Chapter 02 fixes that for free.
Field note · the 20-minute rule

When a lab breaks, spend twenty minutes diagnosing before searching for the answer. Write down what you expected, what you observed, and the first command that disagreed. This single habit is worth more than memorising a hundred show commands, because it is exactly what an interview or an outage will ask of you.

Conventions used in this manual

  • R1(config)# is a prompt, not something you type. ! begins a comment inside a configuration block.
  • Addresses use the reserved documentation ranges: 10.0.0.0/8, 192.0.2.0/24, 198.51.100.0/24, 203.0.113.0/24 and 2001:db8::/32. Public-looking examples never point at a real organisation.
  • BAD DECISION boxes describe a choice that is common, tempting, and wrong — followed by why it fails and what to do instead. These are the most valuable pages in the manual.
  • Links marked ↗ leave the manual. They are references, not required reading, and the page loads nothing from them.

What this manual is not

It is not a brain dump, and it does not contain live exam questions. Cisco changes exam content and does not publish passing scores. Anything on the internet that promises "the exact questions" is either stale, illegal under the candidate agreement, or both. What this manual gives you is the underlying model, so that an unfamiliar question is still answerable.

It is also not a substitute for configuration practice. Reading about OSPF is like reading about swimming. The gates check that you understand the water; only the lab gets you wet.

Version currency

The certification ladder, decoded

What Cisco actually tests, what the codes mean, and which pieces of paper are worth your evenings. Exam facts here are checked against Cisco's 2026 catalogue.

Cisco certification is a ladder with three rungs, and each rung answers a different question. CCNA asks "can you operate a small-to-medium network?" CCNP asks "can you design, secure, automate and troubleshoot an enterprise network?" CCIE asks "can you do it under pressure, for eight hours, with no safety net?" The exams are not progressively harder versions of the same quiz; they test different behaviour.

The order that actually works

  1. CCNA, then work. A CCNA with six months of helpdesk or NOC experience beats a CCNP with none. The certificate opens the interview; the operational scars pass it.
  2. Pick a concentration for a reason. ENARSI if you troubleshoot routing. ENSDWI if your employer runs Catalyst SD-WAN. ENAUTO if your team owns infrastructure as code. ENSLD if you want architecture.
  3. Only then chase CCIE. The lab is a lifestyle commitment, not a next checkbox. Most engineers are better served by depth in one domain plus real project ownership.
Bad decisionCollecting certificates instead of building judgement

Three associate badges in twelve months looks impressive on LinkedIn and collapses in the first design review. Certificates are proxies for knowledge; they do not replace the ability to look at a broken network and form a hypothesis.

Why it is tempting

Each pass gives a dopamine hit and a shareable badge. Study guides are structured, while real operations are messy.

Do this instead

Pair every certificate with one artefact: a lab you built, an outage you diagnosed, or a design document you wrote. One CCNA plus a documented VLAN redesign beats three badges and no stories.

Bad decisionUsing brain dumps to "prepare"

Dumps are copyrighted exam content. Using them violates the Cisco candidate agreement, can void every certification you hold, and — practically — teaches you the answer to a question that will not appear again. The exam is sampled from a large pool; memorised answers do not transfer to the lab or the job.

Why it is tempting

It is faster, cheaper than a course, and the score feels like proof.

Do this instead

Use official exam topics as a checklist, a lab for muscle memory, and practice tests only to find weak domains. If a question surprises you, learn the underlying mechanism — that is what the next question will test.

Exam lens

The CCNA does not reward trivia. It rewards the ability to read a topology, predict the forwarding path, and choose the verification command. Every chapter in this manual ends with commands for exactly that reason.

Models, encapsulation, and the life of a packet

OSI is not trivia. It is a diagnostic coordinate system: every fault lives at a layer, and every command interrogates one.

Two models describe the same journey. The OSI model has seven layers and is the vocabulary engineers use to argue about where a problem lives. The TCP/IP model has four (or five) and describes what actually ships in software. You need both: OSI to communicate, TCP/IP to implement.

Encapsulation is a series of envelopes

When PC-USER sends an ICMP echo to 10.10.30.10, the host builds downward. The ICMP payload gets a TCP/UDP header (not for ICMP, which sits directly on IP), then an IP header with source and destination, then an Ethernet header with the next-hop MAC and a trailer with a frame check sequence. Each layer treats everything above it as opaque payload. That is the whole trick: the router does not care about your HTTP session, and the switch does not care about your IP address.

At the receiving side, the process reverses. The NIC checks the FCS, the host checks the destination MAC, IP checks the destination address and TTL, and the transport layer reassembles the stream. A failure at any step produces a different symptom — which is why "the network is down" is never a useful sentence.

The life of one ping

  1. Routing decision. PC-USER compares 10.10.30.10 with its own 10.10.10.0/24. It is remote, so the host sends to its default gateway, 10.10.10.1.
  2. ARP for the gateway. The host broadcasts "who has 10.10.10.1?" The router replies with its MAC. The host caches the mapping for a few minutes.
  3. Frame to the switch. Destination MAC is the router. ACCESS-01 looks up the MAC table, finds the port toward HQ-CORE-01, and forwards. Unknown unicast would be flooded; broadcast is always flooded within the VLAN.
  4. Router decapsulates. HQ-R1 strips the Ethernet frame, checks the IP header, decrements TTL, and looks up 10.10.30.0/24 in its routing table.
  5. New frame, new MACs. The router builds a fresh Layer 2 frame for the next hop. Source and destination MAC change at every routed hop; IP addresses do not (unless NAT is involved).
  6. The return path. The echo reply follows the reverse route. Asymmetry is normal; a broken return route is the classic reason a ping "works one way".
Memory hook

Layer 2 is hop-by-hop; Layer 3 is end-to-end; Layer 4 is process-to-process. If the MAC changes and the IP does not, you are crossing a router. If the port changes, you are crossing a socket boundary.

Domains, bandwidth and delay

A collision domain is a set of interfaces that can collide; modern full-duplex switched Ethernet makes each port its own collision domain, which is why CSMA/CD is history. A broadcast domain is a set of devices that receive each other's broadcasts; on a switched network, one VLAN equals one broadcast domain unless you route. Design is largely the art of deciding how big those domains should be.

Cabling, optics, and Power over Ethernet

Most outages begin with a physical fact: the wrong optic, a dirty connector, a PoE budget that was never calculated, or a duplex setting that "looks fine".

The physical layer is not glamorous, but it is where an astonishing share of real incidents live. An interface can be up/up and still be unusable: CRC errors climbing, a one-way fibre strand, or a speed mismatch negotiated into half duplex. Learn the numbers and the counters, and you will diagnose in minutes what otherwise takes a truck roll.

Power over Ethernet

PoE delivers DC power over the same pairs as data. The standards matter because the budget does: a switch rated for 740 W cannot power forty 30 W access points. 802.3af (PoE) provides up to 15.4 W at the port, 802.3at (PoE+) up to 30 W, and 802.3bt (PoE++ / 4PPoE) up to 60 W (Type 3) or 90 W (Type 4) at the source, with less at the device after cable loss.

Read that output like a clinician. Up/up means the physical link and the line protocol are established. Rising CRC counts with clean input errors usually point to a bad cable, a dirty optic, or a duplex mismatch. runts suggest collisions or a faulty NIC; giants suggest MTU or a jabbering device. late collisions on a modern full-duplex link almost always mean one side is hard-set to half duplex. Ethernet switching and VLANs

A switch is a table, a set of flooding rules, and a segmentation decision. Everything else is implementation.

An Ethernet switch learns source MAC addresses, maps them to ports and VLANs, and forwards frames by destination MAC. If the destination is known, it forwards to one port. If unknown, it floods within the VLAN. Broadcasts and multicasts are flooded too. That is the entire forwarding model — and most Layer 2 incidents are a consequence of forgetting the flooding part.

What the MAC table actually holds

The MAC address table is keyed by VLAN, MAC address and port, with an ageing timer (300 seconds by default on IOS). A MAC that moves between ports repeatedly causes MAC flapping, which is usually a loop, a duplicate address, or a misconfigured virtual host. A table with thousands of entries on an access port is a symptom of a MAC flooding attack or a lab gone wrong.

VTP: convenient, and dangerous

VLAN Trunking Protocol propagates VLAN definitions between switches. In server mode, one switch can overwrite the VLAN database of the entire domain. A switch with a higher revision number and an empty database will happily delete your VLANs — including the management VLAN — across the campus. If you use VTP, use transparent mode unless you have a very controlled reason not to, and always set a domain password.

Bad decisionPutting everything in VLAN 1 and calling it "simple"

A flat Layer 2 domain means every broadcast, every ARP, every multicast and every unknown unicast reaches every port. One loop takes down the whole building, one compromised laptop can ARP-spoof the finance team, and a broadcast storm has no boundary to stop it.

Why it is tempting

No inter-VLAN routing to configure, no trunk allow-lists, no subnet plan. It works in a ten-person office.

Do this instead

Segment by function and trust level, not by floor. Keep user, voice, server, guest and management traffic in separate VLANs with explicit routing and policy between them. Limit each VLAN to a size you can reason about.

Bad decisionTrunking all VLANs everywhere

switchport trunk allowed vlan all is the default. It means a user-access switch receives broadcast traffic for the server, voice and management VLANs it has no business carrying, and a VLAN-hopping attack has a much larger surface.

Why it is tempting

New VLANs work immediately without touching the trunk. Nobody has to think.

Do this instead

Use explicit allow-lists and prune at the access layer. Adopt a naming and numbering convention, and document which VLANs each wiring closet needs.

Spanning Tree: loops, root bridges and guard rails

Redundant links without STP are a broadcast storm waiting for a maintenance window. With STP, redundancy is safe — but only if the root bridge and the edge are designed.

Ethernet has no TTL. A frame that loops loops forever, and every copy generates more copies. Within seconds, a redundant Layer 2 topology without Spanning Tree saturates links, melts switch CPUs, and takes down the building. STP builds a loop-free logical tree by blocking redundant ports, while keeping the physical links available for failover.

The election, in one paragraph

Switches exchange BPDUs. The switch with the lowest bridge ID — priority (default 32768) followed by MAC address — becomes the root. Every non-root switch selects one root port: the port with the lowest cost path to the root. Each segment selects a designated port; everything else is blocked. In classic 802.1D, ports move through blocking, listening, learning and forwarding, which can take 30–50 seconds. RSTP (802.1w) converges in seconds and adds edge, point-to-point and alternate port roles. MST (802.1s) maps multiple VLANs to a smaller number of instances.

The four guard rails

  • PortFast skips listening/learning on edge ports. Use it only where a single host or phone connects.
  • BPDU Guard err-disables a PortFast port if a BPDU arrives. This stops a user from plugging in a switch and becoming part of the topology.
  • Root Guard prevents a downstream switch from becoming root on that port. Use it on access-facing distribution links.
  • Loop Guard prevents a blocked port from moving to forwarding when BPDUs stop arriving — the classic unidirectional-link loop.
Bad decisionLeaving the root bridge to chance

With default priorities, the switch with the lowest MAC wins. That is often the oldest access switch in the noisiest closet, so the entire campus forwards through a 1G uplink and a single power supply. The network "works" until it does not.

Why it is tempting

STP is on by default and appears to handle everything. Nobody notices until a link fails and convergence takes 50 seconds.

Do this instead

Explicitly set primary and secondary roots per VLAN on the core pair, align the root with the default gateway (FHRP), and monitor show spanning-tree for unexpected root changes.

Bad decisionDisabling STP because "we have no loops"

Every desk is a potential loop. A user plugs a £15 switch into two wall ports, or a conference room cable is patched back into the same access switch. Without STP, that is a broadcast storm in seconds; with it, the port blocks and the network survives.

Why it is tempting

STP convergence caused an outage once, so the protocol takes the blame rather than the design.

Do this instead

Keep RSTP, design the root, enable BPDU Guard on every edge port, and use Loop Guard on fibre where a one-way failure is possible. Fix convergence with design, not by removing the safety net.

Symptom · MAC flapping

Logs repeat %SW_MATM-4-MACFLAP_NOTIF and the network stutters. That is a loop or a duplicate MAC, not a "slow switch". Trace the flapping MAC to its two ports, then follow the physical path. Do not clear the table and hope.

EtherChannel, stacking and physical redundancy

Bundling links buys bandwidth and sub-second failover — but only when both ends agree on the same rules. Redundancy you never test is decoration.

Spanning Tree blocks redundant links to prevent loops, which means half your expensive uplink capacity sits idle. An EtherChannel (link aggregation) solves this by making several physical links look like one logical link. STP sees one port, so nothing is blocked; the switch hashes flows across members; and if a member fails, traffic redistributes in milliseconds instead of seconds.

The rules of the bundle

  • Same speed and duplex on every member.
  • Same Layer 2 configuration: access VLAN or trunk mode, allowed VLANs, native VLAN (on some platforms).
  • Same EtherChannel protocol and mode on both ends. LACP (active/active or active/passive) is the standards-based choice; PAgP is Cisco-proprietary; on is static and offers no negotiation.
  • Members must be contiguous or supported by the platform's grouping rules; check the switch's channel-group limits.

Wireless foundations: RF, 802.11 and WPA3

Wi-Fi is a shared, half-duplex, interference-prone medium. Treat it like Ethernet with an invisible, unreliable cable and most design questions answer themselves.

Wireless is the only access layer where the physical medium changes minute to minute. Users move, microwaves leak, neighbours transmit, and the access point must arbitrate every conversation. The engineer's job is to make the RF predictable enough that the protocols above it can do their jobs.

The RF numbers that matter

Signal strength is measured in dBm (a logarithmic ratio to 1 mW). −30 dBm is excellent, −67 dBm is the usual target for voice and real-time applications, −75 dBm is marginal for data, and −90 dBm is effectively noise. SNR — signal minus noise floor — matters more than raw strength: a −60 dBm signal with a −90 dBm noise floor yields 30 dB SNR and works; a −55 dBm signal with a −50 dBm noise floor is loud garbage.

Rules of thumb: doubling transmit power adds 3 dB; moving twice as far in free space costs about 6 dB; a 3 dB gain is a doubling of power. Every 3 dB matters because the client's transmit power is often the limiting factor, not the AP's.

Why it is tempting

APs are expensive; a visual guess lets you order fewer and promise coverage on paper.

Perform a predictive survey, then validate with an on-site survey and a post-install validation. Design for capacity (clients per AP) as well as coverage, and check the uplink and PoE budget for Wi-Fi 6E/7.

Binary, masks and subnetting from zero

If you can count to 256 in powers of two and subtract, you can subnet. Everything else is presentation.

An IPv4 address is 32 bits, written as four decimal octets. Each octet is 8 bits with place values 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. The block-size method

For any prefix shorter than /24, work in the octet that contains the boundary. The block size is 256 minus the mask value in that octet. Subnets start at multiples of the block size. This is faster and less error-prone than converting everything to binary under exam pressure.

Example A · 192.168.10.0/26

Mask 255.255.255.192, block size 64. Subnets: .0, .64, .128, .192. Broadcasts: .63, .127, .191, .255. Usable hosts per subnet: 62. The address 192.168.10.130 is in the .128 subnet, usable range .129–.190.

Example B · 10.10.10.77/27

Mask 255.255.255.224, block size 32. Subnets: .0, .32, .64, .96. The address .77 falls in .64/27. Network .64, broadcast .95, usable .65–.94. A /27 gives 30 hosts.

For a prefix longer than /24, the boundary moves left into the third octet. 172.16.5.130/25: mask 255.255.255.128, block 128 in the fourth octet. The .128 subnet runs from .128 to .255; network .128, broadcast .255, usable .129–.254. The other subnet is .0–.127.

Network, broadcast and the two addresses you lose

The all-zeros host portion is the network address; the all-ones host portion is the directed broadcast. Neither is assignable to a host in classic subnetting, which is why usable hosts = 2ⁿ − 2. A /31 is the exception: RFC 3021 allows both addresses on point-to-point links, and modern IOS supports it. A /32 is a single host route, commonly used on loopbacks.

The AND operation, demystified

Network address = IP AND mask, bit by bit. In practice: copy the masked octets exactly, and for the unmasked octet round down to the nearest multiple of the block size. 10.10.10.77/27 → keep 10.10.10, round 77 down to 64 → 10.10.10.64.

A /24 for every VLAN is not a plan; it is a habit. Variable-length subnetting turns a fixed block into a hierarchy that routes, summarises and grows.

  2. Align to the block. A /23 must start on an even third octet (0, 2, 4…). A /26 starts at a multiple of 64. Misalignment creates overlapping subnets that are painful to unwind.
  3. Reserve gaps. Leave room between categories so a new VLAN can be inserted without renumbering. Renumbering a live campus is a project, not a change ticket.
  4. Write it down in an IPAM. NetBox, Infoblox, phpIPAM, or a maintained spreadsheet. The plan is only real if the next engineer can read it.

IPv6 without fear

IPv6 is not IPv4 with longer addresses. It removes broadcast, replaces ARP with Neighbor Discovery, and makes the /64 the atomic unit of a LAN.

IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses written as eight hexadecimal groups. The rules for shortening are simple: drop leading zeros in each group, and replace one contiguous run of all-zero groups with :: — once only. 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0000:0001 becomes 2001:db8::1. A prefix is written in CIDR just like IPv4: 2001:db8:10:10::/64.

Neighbor Discovery replaces ARP

IPv6 has no ARP and no broadcast. Instead, ICMPv6 Neighbor Discovery uses multicast: a router sends Router Advertisements (type 134), hosts send Router Solicitations (133), and address resolution uses Neighbor Solicitation/Advertisement (135/136). Duplicate Address Detection uses NS before an address is used. SLAAC lets a host build its own address from the RA prefix plus an interface identifier; DHCPv6 can supply addresses and options instead.

The /64 rule

SLAAC requires a /64 interface identifier. Do not subnet a LAN smaller than /64 unless you deliberately disable SLAAC. A typical allocation: a /48 per site, a /64 per VLAN. That gives 65,536 LANs per site — enough that "running out" stops being the design driver.

DHCP, DNS, NAT and ARP

These four services are invisible until they break, and then everything breaks at once. Learn their packet flows, not just their configuration.

A host needs four things before it can use the network: an address, a mask, a default gateway, and a resolver. DHCP supplies most of them; DNS resolves names; ARP or ND finds the next-hop MAC; NAT rewrites addresses at a boundary. Each has a distinct failure signature.

DHCP: DORA and the relay

DHCP uses UDP: client port 68, server port 67. The exchange is Discover → Offer → Request → Acknowledge. Because the initial Discover is a broadcast, a DHCP server on another subnet never sees it unless a router relays it. On Cisco, ip helper-address on the client-facing interface converts the broadcast to unicast toward the server.

TCP, UDP and the transport layer

Ports decide which process receives the data. TCP decides whether it arrived intact. UDP decides nothing and gets out of the way.

IP delivers packets to a host, not to an application. The transport layer adds port numbers to identify the process, and optionally reliability, ordering and congestion control. That choice — TCP or UDP — explains most of the difference between "the application is slow" and "the application is broken".

The TCP handshake and what can go wrong

TCP establishes state with a three-way handshake: SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK. Sequence numbers establish ordering; the receive window advertises how much data the receiver can buffer; MSS negotiates the largest segment that fits the path MTU. Teardown uses FIN/ACK or a RST to abort.

  • SYN sent, no SYN-ACK: a firewall, ACL, NAT rule or missing listener is dropping the packet. The network path may be fine.
  • SYN-ACK received, ACK lost: asymmetric routing or a stateful device that did not see the return path.
  • Handshake succeeds, then stalls at large transfers: MTU/PMTUD black hole or a proxy in the path.
  • Connections in SYN_SENT: the far end is unreachable or silently filtering.

MTU, MSS and the black hole

Ethernet's standard MTU is 1500 bytes. TCP's MSS is typically 1460 (1500 minus 20 bytes IP and 20 bytes TCP). Path MTU Discovery uses ICMP "Fragmentation Needed" (IPv4) or "Packet Too Big" (ICMPv6) to tell the sender to shrink. If a firewall blocks those ICMP messages, small pings work, the TCP handshake works, and large transfers hang. This is the PMTUD black hole, and it is diagnosed by testing with a large, DF-set payload.

UDP is not "unreliable TCP"

UDP is a datagram service: no handshake, no ordering, no retransmission. That is a feature for DNS, DHCP, NTP, voice and QUIC, which implement their own reliability or prefer timeliness over completeness. Do not blame UDP for packet loss you have not measured.

Read the brackets as [AD/metric]. The O 10.20.0.0/24 is an OSPF route with metric 2; the S 10.30.0.0/16 is a static route with metric 0 (static routes do not have a real metric). A packet to 10.20.0.5 matches both the OSPF /24 and any /8 or default route; the /24 wins because it is longer. A packet to 10.30.5.5 matches the /16 static, even if a /24 for a different subnet exists elsewhere — longest match first, always.

Static routes: four flavours

  • Directly connected next hop: ip route 10.30.0.0 255.255.0.0 198.51.100.2. The router must resolve 198.51.100.2 via ARP on the egress interface.
  • Exit interface: ip route 10.30.0.0 255.255.0.0 GigabitEthernet0/0/1. Valid on point-to-point links; on multi-access links it can trigger proxy ARP for every destination.
  • Floating static: add an AD above the dynamic protocol, e.g. ip route ... 198.51.100.6 200, so it installs only if the primary disappears.
  • Discard route: ip route 10.10.0.0 255.255.248.0 Null0, used to anchor a summary and prevent loops.
Bad decisionStatic routes everywhere because "they never fail"

Static routes do not fail; they simply become wrong. A link goes down, a backup path exists, but the static route still points into the void. Someone eventually adds a floating static with an arbitrary AD, then another, and the network becomes a stack of undocumented assumptions.

Why it is tempting

They are simple, predictable, and require no protocol knowledge. They also avoid routing-protocol adjacencies.

Do this instead

Use static routes for stubs, default routes to an ISP, and deliberate traffic engineering. Use a dynamic protocol where topology can change. If you must use statics, pair them with object tracking (IP SLA) and document the intent.

Bad decisionPointing a static route at the wrong interface on a multi-access link

ip route 10.30.0.0 255.255.0.0 Gi0/0/1 on an Ethernet link tells the router the destination is "out that port". It then ARPs for every destination address, or relies on proxy ARP, and can black-hole traffic when the next hop is not the intended router. On point-to-point links, interface-only routes are fine.

Why it is tempting

It removes the need to know the next-hop IP, and it works in a simple lab.

Do this instead

Use a next-hop address on multi-access links, or both next hop and interface on platforms that require it. Verify with show ip route and a traceroute, not just a ping.

Summarisation and the Null0 anchor

Summarising 10.10.0.0/24 through 10.10.3.0/24 gives 10.10.0.0/22. If the router advertises that summary but only some components exist, traffic for the missing components can loop between the summary and a default route. A discard route to Null0 for the summary gives those packets a local, silent exit and breaks the loop. It is not a hack; it is the standard anchor for summarisation.

OSPFv2 fundamentals

Every OSPF router builds the same map. Convergence is fast because the map, not the route, is what gets exchanged.

OSPF is a link-state protocol. Each router describes its own links in a link-state advertisement, floods those advertisements within an area, and runs Dijkstra's shortest-path-first algorithm on the resulting database. The database is identical on every router in the area; the routing table is the local result of the calculation. That separation is why OSPF converges quickly and why "show ip ospf database" is often more revealing than "show ip route".

Adjacency, in the order it happens

Hellos are sent every 10 seconds on broadcast and point-to-point links (dead interval 40), and every 30 seconds on NBMA (dead 120). Hello and dead timers must match, as must the area ID, authentication and stub flags. A mismatch in any of them means the neighbour never leaves Down or Init, and the log is usually silent.

Designated routers and cost

On a multi-access segment, OSPF elects a DR and BDR to reduce adjacencies. Election is by interface priority (default 1, highest wins), then router ID (highest wins). It is not preemptive: a new router with a higher priority does not take over until the DR fails. DROthers form full adjacencies only with the DR and BDR, which is why some neighbours legitimately sit in 2-Way.

Cost is reference bandwidth ÷ interface bandwidth, with a default reference of 100 Mbps. That makes 1G, 10G and 100G all cost 1 unless you raise the reference. Set auto-cost reference-bandwidth 100000 consistently on every router in the domain — changing it on one router creates asymmetric and surprising paths.

Bad decisionOne OSPF area for the entire company

Why it is tempting

It is the simplest configuration and avoids inter-area design decisions. Labs rarely have more than ten routers.

Do this instead

Use area 0 as the backbone and a separate area per region or large site. Summarise at the ABRs. Keep the backbone contiguous and avoid routing through a non-backbone area.

Bad decisionLeaving the default reference bandwidth on a 10G/100G network

With the default 100 Mbps reference, every link of 100 Mbps or faster has cost 1. OSPF then sees a 100G core and a 1G backup as equal and may load-balance across both, or choose the slower path based on router ID.

Why it is tempting

It is the default, and the network "converges" either way.

Do this instead

Set a reference bandwidth above your fastest link (for example 100000 = 100G) on every router, or set explicit interface costs. Verify with show ip ospf interface and compare paths with show ip route.

Exam lens

If a neighbour is stuck in ExStart/Exchange, think MTU mismatch first. If it is stuck in Init, think one-way hellos or an ACL. If it is Down, check the interface, the network statement, the area and the timers, in that order.

Bad decisionRedistributing without a filter or metric

Why it is tempting

One command makes the prefix appear. Filtering feels like extra work.

Do this instead

Redistribute with a route map or prefix list, set an explicit seed metric and metric type, and tag routes so you can prevent re-redistribution. Verify with show ip ospf database external.

Bad decisionSummarising at two ABRs with different ranges

If two ABRs advertise different summaries for the same area, routers choose the more specific one unpredictably, and traffic can take a suboptimal or asymmetric path. Worse, a summary that includes a prefix not present in the area creates a black hole.

Why it is tempting

Each ABR is configured independently by a different engineer or template.

Do this instead

Standardise the summary range on all ABRs for the area, verify the component routes exist, and add a Null0 anchor where appropriate. Compare show ip ospf database summary on both ABRs.

EIGRP and the feasibility condition

EIGRP's magic is not the metric. It is the guarantee that a backup path is loop-free before it is installed.

EIGRP is an advanced distance-vector protocol. It does not flood a full topology map; it exchanges prefixes with reported distance (the neighbour's cost to the destination) and computes a feasible distance (this router's cost via that neighbour). The best path is the successor. A neighbour can be a feasible successor only if its reported distance is strictly less than the current feasible distance — the feasibility condition. That inequality is what makes EIGRP loop-free and fast.

Bad decisionRunning EIGRP as the only routing protocol in a multi-vendor network

EIGRP is Cisco-proprietary (with limited third-party support). If a merger brings in Juniper or Arista, or a cloud gateway only speaks BGP, you end up redistributing at every boundary. Redistribution is where routing loops, suboptimal paths and metric mismatches live.

Why it is tempting

It is easy to configure, converges quickly, and every Cisco-heavy shop already runs it.

Do this instead

Use OSPF or IS-IS as the open interior protocol if multi-vendor is a realistic future, and keep EIGRP where it is already stable. If you must redistribute, filter and tag aggressively.

BGP essentials: policy, not just paths

BGP is the protocol that connects organisations that do not trust each other. Every configuration is a policy statement, including the ones you forget to write.

BGP is a path-vector protocol. It carries reachability for prefixes between autonomous systems, and it chooses paths using a long list of attributes rather than a single metric. The AS path provides loop prevention: a router rejects any route whose AS path already contains its own AS. eBGP peers are in different ASes; iBGP peers are in the same AS and require a full mesh or route reflectors because iBGP does not re-advertise routes learned from one iBGP peer to another.

Do this instead

Use route reflectors (with redundant clusters) or confederations. Design a small number of RRs, peer clients to them, and keep the IGP providing loopback reachability.

A host has one default gateway. If that gateway dies, the host does not care that a second router exists — unless the two routers share a virtual address.

Endpoints are simple: they ARP for their default gateway and cache the MAC. If the gateway router fails, the host keeps sending frames to a dead MAC. First-hop redundancy protocols solve this by presenting a virtual IP and virtual MAC shared by two or more routers. One is active, the other standby, and the host never changes its configuration.

Three details determine whether this actually protects users. First, the virtual IP must be the host default gateway, not either physical address. Align FHRP with STP

Make the STP root for a VLAN the same switch that is HSRP active for that VLAN. Otherwise traffic from a host goes to the active gateway, then crosses the inter-switch link to reach the root, and then leaves — adding a hop and congesting the peer link for no reason.

Bad decisionDual-homing servers without a first-hop protocol

Two NICs, two switches, two default gateways — and the server still fails over badly, because most operating systems do not dynamically switch default gateways. The standby NIC may be idle, or failover may take minutes. The result is redundancy that only works during a controlled test.

Why it is tempting

Two cables look resilient, and the server team owns the NIC configuration.

Do this instead

Provide a shared virtual gateway with HSRP or VRRP, and use a server-side teaming/bonding mode that actively detects failure. Test by pulling the active link under load.

Bad decisionTracking the wrong object

Tracking the LAN SVI tells HSRP nothing: the SVI stays up even if the WAN uplink is dead, so the router remains active and black-holes traffic. Tracking a flapping interface without a delay causes constant role changes.

Why it is tempting

The interface exists and the command is easy. It looks like health checking.

Do this instead

Track the uplink line protocol, an IP SLA probe to a reliable target, or a specific route. Add preempt delay and hysteresis so a transient blip does not move the gateway.

QoS: congestion, marking and queuing

QoS does not create bandwidth. It decides who waits when bandwidth is scarce — and it only works if the marking boundary is trustworthy.

On a LAN with abundant capacity, QoS mostly matters for control traffic and microbursts. On a WAN uplink, it is the difference between usable voice and a meeting that sounds like a fax machine. The model is consistent: classify traffic, mark it, queue it on egress, and drop or police what exceeds policy.

The priority queue is strict: it is served before everything else, capped at 10% so it cannot starve the link. Bandwidth percent guarantees a share under congestion but allows unused capacity to be borrowed. fair-queue in class-default keeps one elephant flow from consuming all remaining bandwidth.

Policing versus shaping

Policing drops or remarks traffic that exceeds a rate; it is simple and stateless, and it is the usual choice at a provider edge. Shaping buffers excess traffic and sends it later, which smooths bursts but adds delay and requires memory. On a low-speed WAN, shape to the contracted rate on egress rather than police; on ingress from a provider, you often cannot control the rate and must police.

Bad decisionMarking voice at the core instead of the edge

If endpoints or access switches are untrusted, anything can arrive marked EF. A user with a packet generator can starve real voice calls. QoS must begin where the traffic enters, with a trust boundary that either marks it or verifies it.

Why it is tempting

The core policy is centralised and easy to see. Access switches are numerous and configured by templates.

Do this instead

Trust the phone's DSCP at the access port and mark/limit PC traffic, or use Cisco phone QoS trust. Untrust everything else at the edge, then apply queuing at the bottleneck.

Bad decisionGiving voice 100% priority "to be safe"

A strict priority queue with no cap can starve routing protocols, management and every other application during a flood or a misbehaving endpoint. Once the control plane is starved, the device stops responding and the outage becomes total.

Why it is tempting

Voice is the most latency-sensitive traffic, so more priority seems better.

Do this instead

Cap the priority queue (often 10–33% of link bandwidth), reserve bandwidth for control traffic, and monitor drops per class. QoS is a budget, not a wish.

Where QoS actually applies

Egress queuing matters at the slowest link in the path. Applying a policy to a 10G core port while the WAN is 100 Mbps changes nothing: by the time packets reach the WAN, the congestion has already happened or not. Find the bottleneck first.

A change-control sequence that survives audits

  1. State the intent and the blast radius. Which devices, which services, who is affected, and what is the rollback trigger?
  2. Capture the before state. Running config, interface counters, routing table, licence status, and a reachability test from a user perspective.
  3. Prepare the rollback. A saved config, a known-good image, console access, and a tested command sequence — not "we will figure it out".
  4. Apply during a window, in small steps. Verify after each step; never stack three unverified changes.
  5. Verify with the user's metric. A ping from the router is not proof that the application works. Test the actual flow.
  6. Record the outcome. What changed, what broke, what was learned. The next engineer will read this.
Bad decisionUpgrading production without a lab rehearsal

Release notes list caveats, but they do not list your configuration. An upgrade can change default behaviour, deprecate a command, alter an ACL's handling of fragments, or break an unsupported third-party integration. Production is a terrible place to discover that.

Why it is tempting

The window is short, the image is "just a maintenance release", and the lab is never an exact copy.

Do this instead

Rehearse on a comparable platform or CML, diff the configuration after upgrade, and check feature compatibility. Upgrade one device in a redundant pair first, verify, then proceed.

Bad decisionChanging a remote device without console access or a rollback timer

An ACL or a routing change cuts your SSH session. Without out-of-band access or a scheduled reload, the device is unreachable and someone drives to the site. The fix is cheap and boring: reload in 10 before the change, cancel it after verification.

Why it is tempting

The change is "just one line", and the site is only an hour away.

Do this instead

Use a rollback timer, verify from a second path, and keep out-of-band management (console server, cellular, separate VRF) available. Test the rollback path before you need it.

Troubleshooting as a discipline

The method is always the same: define the fault precisely, gather evidence, form a hypothesis, test the smallest thing that could disprove it, then fix the cause rather than the symptom. Work bottom-up when the scope is unknown; top-down when one application fails while everything else works; divide and conquer when you can test a midpoint. Document the timeline as you go — memory is a liar under pressure.

Before you reload

Ask three questions: Is the configuration saved? Is the image verified? Is there a way back in? If any answer is no, stop. A reload is the most destructive routine command in networking.

Security foundations and Layer 2 attacks

The perimeter is a fiction once an attacker is inside your VLAN. Layer 2 has no authentication, no TTL and no default trust — which makes it both fragile and easy to harden.

Security is the practice of controlling access while assuming some control will fail. The classic goals are confidentiality, integrity and availability, supported by authentication, authorisation, accounting and non-repudiation. The engineering principles matter more than the acronyms: least privilege, defence in depth, fail secure, separation of duties, and never trusting a network location as proof of identity.

The Layer 2 attack surface

  • MAC flooding. A tool fills the CAM table, the switch floods unknown unicast to every port, and the attacker captures traffic. Port security limits learned MACs per port.
  • VLAN hopping. Switch spoofing (DTP negotiation) or double tagging can move traffic between VLANs. Disable DTP on access ports, set the native VLAN to an unused ID, and never put user ports in VLAN 1.
  • DHCP starvation and rogue DHCP. An attacker exhausts the pool or hands out a malicious gateway. DHCP snooping with rate limits and trusted uplinks prevents it.
  • ARP spoofing. Forged ARP replies redirect traffic. Dynamic ARP Inspection validates against the snooping binding table.
  • STP manipulation. A rogue switch wins the root election and becomes a choke point. BPDU Guard and Root Guard defend the edge.

AAA, 802.1X and Cisco ISE

Authentication asks who you are. Authorisation asks what you may do. Accounting asks what you did. Port-based access control enforces all three at the wall jack.

AAA separates three questions that are often conflated. Authentication verifies identity (password, certificate, token). Authorisation decides permissions (which VLAN, which commands). Accounting records activity (login, commands, duration). RADIUS and TACACS+ both provide AAA, but they are not interchangeable.

802.1X in one page

The supplicant (laptop) asks for access. The authenticator (switch) blocks all traffic except EAP over LAN until authentication completes. Critical VLAN and guest VLAN are the difference between a security control and an outage. If ISE is unreachable and there is no critical VLAN, every port fails closed and the building loses wired access. Design the failure mode before the failure.

Bad decisionTurning on 802.1X everywhere at once

A campus-wide 802.1X cutover without monitor mode discovers the devices nobody documented: the HVAC controller, the badge reader, the legacy printer, the conference room TV. They all fail closed, and the helpdesk drowns.

Why it is tempting

Security mandates a date, and a phased rollout takes months.

Do this instead

Run monitor mode first, inventory the failures via ISE, build MAB/profiling exceptions, pilot one floor, then enforce. Keep a critical VLAN and a documented break-glass procedure.

Bad decisionTreating MAB as authentication

A MAC address is public. It appears in ARP tables, DHCP requests and switch logs. Anyone can spoof a printer's MAC and inherit its access. MAB answers "what kind of device is this", not "who is this".

Why it is tempting

It makes old devices work without a supplicant, and the ISE logs look green.

Do this instead

Use MAB only for devices that cannot authenticate, combine it with profiling and restricted authorisation, and monitor for MAC changes on those ports. Prefer EAP-TLS for managed endpoints.

TrustSec and SGT

Cisco TrustSec assigns a Security Group Tag to traffic and enforces policy based on the tag rather than IP addresses. It scales better than ACLs in a large campus, but only if the tag propagation (inline or SXP) and the policy matrix are designed end to end. A tag that is not carried is just a label.

Bad decisionApplying an ACL in the wrong direction and locking yourself out

An inbound ACL on the management SVI that permits only the server subnet will drop your SSH session before you can type no ip access-group. The implicit deny at the end is doing exactly what it was designed to do; the design forgot the management path.

Why it is tempting

The rule looks correct for users, and the engineer is working from a different subnet that "should" be allowed.

Do this instead

Add an explicit permit for the management network and the routing protocol first, test on a console or with reload in, and verify counters. Never apply a new ACL to a remote interface without an out-of-band path.

Bad decisionUsing a standard ACL to filter a whole subnet near the source

A standard ACL can only match source, so placing it near the source blocks that source from reaching everything, including DNS, the domain controller and the management network. It is a blunt instrument that creates outages far from the intended target.

Why it is tempting

Standard ACLs are simpler and older documentation uses them for access control.

Do this instead

Use extended ACLs for policy, place them near the source so unwanted traffic is dropped early, and reserve standard ACLs for simple route-filtering or VTY source restrictions.

DMVPN in one paragraph

DMVPN combines multipoint GRE, NHRP and IPsec so spokes can build tunnels to a hub and, in phase 3, directly to each other. The hub is the NHRP server; spokes register their public address and request shortcuts. It is a classic enterprise WAN design, though many organisations now prefer Catalyst SD-WAN for the same problem. The routing protocol runs over the tunnel; the underlay only needs to reach the hub's public address.

Bad decisionSharing one pre-shared key across every branch

A single PSK means any compromised branch can impersonate any other branch or the hub. It also makes rotation a coordinated outage across the estate. IPsec authenticates the peer, not just the encrypted bytes; a shared secret destroys that distinction.

Why it is tempting

One key is easy to deploy and works for every spoke. Certificates require a PKI.

Do this instead

Use per-peer keys at minimum, and certificates with a managed PKI for scale. Rotate keys on a schedule, and monitor for IKE authentication failures as a signal.

Bad decisionBuilding a flat VPN that bridges every site into one subnet

Bridging a VPN turns remote workers into local Layer 2 users: broadcasts, ARP and lateral movement all cross the tunnel. One infected home laptop can scan the entire corporate subnet. It also makes routing and MTU behaviour unpredictable.

Why it is tempting

Everything "just works" without new routes or firewall rules, especially for legacy applications that broadcast.

Do this instead

Route the VPN, assign a dedicated client subnet, and apply firewall policy and DNS controls. Use split tunnelling deliberately based on risk, not by default.

MTU inside tunnels

IPsec adds overhead; a 1500-byte packet may need fragmentation or a smaller MSS. Set ip tcp adjust-mss 1360 on tunnel interfaces, and allow ICMP fragmentation-needed through the path. "VPN connects but some sites hang" is often an MTU problem, not an encryption problem.

Firewalls, SASE and cloud-delivered security

The user is no longer inside the building. Policy has to follow identity and application, not a physical port.

A modern firewall is not a box that permits port 443. It is a pipeline: stateful inspection, application identification, intrusion prevention, malware analysis, URL categorisation, and TLS decryption where policy allows. Cisco's current family is Secure Firewall (the FTD lineage), managed by Firepower Management Center (FMC) or on-box Firepower Device Manager (FDM). The 3100 series (3105/3110/3120/3130/3140) covers branch and mid-size; the 4200 series (4215/4225/4245) covers larger data-centre and internet-edge roles. The older ASA platform still exists in many estates but is not where new deployments should start.

SASE in plain language

SASE converges network-as-a-service and security-as-a-service: traffic from a branch or a roaming laptop is steered to a cloud security edge, where identity, device posture, URL category and data-loss policy are applied before it reaches the application. Umbrella is best known for DNS-layer protection: it answers DNS queries and blocks malicious domains before a connection is made. That is cheap, fast and effective against a large class of malware — but it is not a substitute for inspection, and it is trivially bypassed if users can switch to an unmanaged resolver.

Zero Trust is the architectural principle underneath: no implicit trust from network location; authenticate and authorise every request; assume breach; log everything. ZTNA implements it for applications by brokering per-app access instead of dropping a user onto the whole LAN via a VPN.

Bad decisionAn internet edge that permits any/any outbound

Outbound any/any lets malware call home, ransomware reach its controller, and data leave without inspection. Most breaches need egress. The firewall sees the connection but has no policy to stop it, and the logs become a haystack with no needle.

Why it is tempting

Everything works. Users never file a ticket about a blocked site, and the initial deployment is simpler.

Do this instead

Default-deny egress, allow categories and applications deliberately, log denies, and use DNS security and TLS inspection where lawful and appropriate. Start in monitor mode to build the allow-list.

Bad decisionDecrypting all TLS "for visibility" without a plan

TLS inspection breaks certificate pinning, mutual TLS, some software updaters and many SaaS applications. It also creates privacy, legal and HR obligations, and it concentrates sensitive plaintext in one device. Turning it on globally produces outages and a compliance incident.

Why it is tempting

Without decryption, IPS and DLP see almost nothing. Vendors market "full visibility".

Do this instead

Decrypt selectively by category and destination, exclude privacy-sensitive and pinned traffic, document the legal basis, protect the inspection device as a high-value asset, and monitor performance.

Firewall rules are code

Treat rule changes like software: peer review, a documented owner and expiry date, hit-count review, and a rollback plan. A rule with no owner and no hits is technical debt that eventually becomes an incident.

An attacker with enable access owns the network. The management plane deserves the same rigour as the data plane — often more.

Cisco devices expose three planes. The management plane handles SSH, SNMP, syslog and APIs. The control plane runs routing protocols and processes traffic destined to the device. Disable plain HTTP, use HTTPS with AAA and an ACL if it is truly needed, restrict SNMPv3 to collectors, and put the management plane in a dedicated, firewalled VLAN.

The management VLAN is not a magic shield

A management VLAN separates traffic only if routing and firewalls enforce it. If any user VLAN can route to it, the segmentation is cosmetic. Apply an ACL on the SVI and on the VTY lines, and log denied attempts.

FlexConnect and roaming

FlexConnect lets an AP switch client traffic locally at the branch while keeping control traffic at the controller. That avoids backhauling every packet to a central site, but it changes the design: the AP's local VLAN must exist at the branch, DHCP must be reachable locally, and ACLs may be enforced in a different place. In central switching, client traffic is tunnelled to the controller, which simplifies policy but consumes WAN bandwidth.

Roaming within one controller is straightforward. Across controllers, mobility groups and inter-controller tunnels keep the client's session and IP address. Fast roaming uses 802.11r (FT), 802.11k (neighbour reports) and 802.11v (BSS transition management). Without these, a client may cling to a distant AP until the signal is unusable.

Bad decisionDeploying APs without sizing PoE and uplinks

A Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 AP can draw 30–60 W and saturate a 1G uplink with realistic client load. Install it on a 2960X with a 370 W budget, and half the APs will not power up; connect it at 1G and the radio is the bottleneck. The wireless design fails because the wired design was never checked.

Why it is tempting

The AP datasheet lists a maximum client rate that sounds like the uplink requirement, and PoE is "just power".

Do this instead

Calculate PoE budget with headroom, use 2.5G/5G/10G uplinks for high-density APs, and confirm the access switch supports the required PoE class and uplink speed. Design the wired and wireless together.

Bad decisionPutting AP management in the user VLAN

An AP is a Linux device with a management interface. Placing it in the same VLAN as users exposes it to client broadcast traffic and lateral movement, and makes ACLs between wireless and wired impossible to express cleanly. It also complicates DHCP and CAPWAP discovery.

Why it is tempting

The AP gets an address from the user pool and "it works". No new VLAN or DHCP scope is needed.

Do this instead

Use a dedicated AP management VLAN with its own DHCP scope, restrict management access, and keep client VLANs separate. Allow only CAPWAP and management flows from the AP subnet.

Time is a wireless dependency

AP join failures are frequently certificate or time related. If the AP cannot validate the controller certificate because its clock is wrong, the join fails with a vague error. NTP on the AP management network is not optional.

WLAN design: coverage, capacity and validation

Coverage is a signal problem. Capacity is a contention problem. A design that only solves the first fails in every lecture hall.

Wireless design starts with a requirements table, not an AP count. How many clients per area? What applications? What is the acceptable latency? Is voice required? Are there high-density zones (auditoriums, warehouses, clinics)? What devices are legacy? The answers set the target RSSI, SNR, channel width and AP density.

ParameterTargetWhy
RSSI (data)≥ −67 dBmReliable MCS rates and roaming headroom

Predictive, on-site and validation surveys

  1. Predictive survey. Import the floor plan, set wall attenuation, place APs, and simulate coverage and capacity. It is a starting point, not proof.
  2. On-site survey. Walk the space with a analyser, verify RSSI/SNR, identify interference and adjust AP placement. Do this before cabling if possible.
  3. Post-install validation. Test real clients and real applications at peak density, including roaming and voice. Document the results against the requirements.
  4. Baseline and monitor. Record channel utilisation, client counts and retry rates. A design degrades as neighbours, furniture and device mix change.
Capacity math, roughly

One 20 MHz 5 GHz channel has roughly 100–150 Mbps of real throughput after overhead, shared by all clients on that channel. Twenty clients streaming 4K video at 25 Mbps need 500 Mbps — more than one AP can deliver, no matter how strong the signal. Add APs and channels, not power.

Bad decisionOne SSID for employees, guests and IoT

A single SSID means one security policy, one VLAN and one broadcast domain for devices with wildly different trust. A compromised smart TV can reach the finance file share; guest devices can ARP-spoof employees; IoT broadcasts wake up every laptop.

Why it is tempting

Users only see one network, onboarding is simple, and there is less configuration.

Do this instead

Separate SSIDs for corporate, guest and IoT, each with its own VLAN, policy and security. Use 802.1X or WPA3-Enterprise for corporate, captive portal plus isolation for guest, and device onboarding for IoT.

Bad decisionUsing 80 MHz channels in a dense office

An 80 MHz channel consumes four 20 MHz channels. In a dense deployment there are not enough channels to go around, so APs overlap and co-channel interference rises. The headline rate goes up; the actual experience goes down.

Why it is tempting

The AP datasheet advertises huge per-client rates at 80 or 160 MHz.

Do this instead

Use 20 MHz in 2.4 GHz and mostly 20–40 MHz in 5 GHz for density. Reserve 80/160 MHz for low-density, high-throughput areas. Let the channel plan, not the marketing, choose width.

Bad decisionScraping CLI output with screen-scraping scripts

Python for network engineers

You do not need to become a software developer. You need to read data, make a request, check the result, and fail safely.

The useful subset of Python for network operations is small: variables, lists and dictionaries, loops, functions, exceptions, files, and a few libraries. requests handles HTTP; json parses payloads; netmiko and napalm handle SSH/CLI; nornir and Ansible handle inventory and orchestration. Use a virtual environment so versions are reproducible.

Python · read-only inventory check with Netmikocopy
import os
from netmiko import ConnectHandler, NetmikoTimeoutException

DEVICES = [
    {"name": "HQ-R1", "host": "10.10.10.1", "device_type": "cisco_ios"},
    {"name": "BR-R1", "host": "10.20.0.1", "device_type": "cisco_ios"},
]

def check(device):
    params = {
        **device,
        "username": os.environ["NET_USER"],
        "password": os.environ["NET_PASS"],
        "timeout": 10,
    }
    try:
        with ConnectHandler(**params) as conn:
            output = conn.send_command("show ip interface brief", read_timeout=15)
            down = [line for line in output.splitlines() if "down" in line.lower()]
            return {"device": device["name"], "down_interfaces": down}
    except (NetmikoTimeoutException, KeyError) as exc:
        return {"device": device["name"], "error": str(exc)}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for result in map(check, DEVICES):
        print(result)
!

Notice what the script does not do: it does not print the password, does not change configuration, and returns a structured error rather than crashing the whole run. Those three choices are the difference between a useful operational tool and a liability.

Bad decisionAutomating changes without verification or rollback

A loop that pushes a VLAN to 200 switches in ten seconds is not automation; it is a very fast outage. If the template is wrong, all 200 are wrong before anyone notices. The operator has replaced a slow human error with a fast systematic one.

Why it is tempting

Speed is the visible benefit, and the script "worked in the lab".

Do this instead

Canary one device, verify with a post-change check, then proceed in batches. Capture the before state, support rollback, and stop the run on the first failure.

Bad decisionUsing a personal account for automation

When the script runs under an engineer's account, the audit trail says the engineer made every change at 3 a.m., the account cannot be disabled without breaking automation, and permissions are broader than the task requires.

Why it is tempting

The account already exists and has the needed rights. Creating a service account requires a ticket.

Do this instead

Use a dedicated service identity with least privilege, rotate its credentials, log its actions, and document ownership. Human and machine actions should be distinguishable.

Orchestration is not a script with more loops. It is a repeatable pipeline with a source of truth, a test, an approval and a rollback.

Ansible is agentless: it connects over SSH or HTTP and applies modules. Its value is not YAML syntax; it is idempotence. A well-written task says "ensure this VLAN exists", not "type this command". Run it twice and the second run changes nothing.

CI/CD for a network

  1. Commit the intent (YAML, Jinja2 templates, policy) to Git. The repository is the source of truth.
  2. Lint and render templates, validate YANG/JSON schemas, and run unit tests on the generated configuration.
  3. Test in a virtual lab (CML, containerlab) and run automated reachability and policy checks.
  4. Approve through a pull request with a named reviewer and a change record.
  5. Deploy to a canary, verify, then to batches. Stop on the first failure.
  6. Observe and reconcile drift. The pipeline is not finished at deployment.

VRFs, MPLS and VXLAN overlays

Segmentation at scale is not more VLANs. It is separate routing tables, a label or a tunnel, and a control plane that distributes reachability.

A VRF (Virtual Routing and Forwarding) is an independent routing table inside one router. Interfaces and routes are assigned to a VRF; overlapping addresses can coexist because each table is separate. VRF-lite is the hop-by-hop version, used for management separation, customer segmentation or a simple multi-tenant campus. It is not a tunnel: every device in the path must know the VRF.

IOS-XE · VRF definition and route leakingcopy
HQ-R1(config)# vrf definition MGMT
HQ-R1(config-vrf)# rd 65010:99
HQ-R1(config-vrf)# address-family ipv4
HQ-R1(config-vrf-af)# exit
HQ-R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0.99
HQ-R1(config-subif)# encapsulation dot1Q 99
HQ-R1(config-subif)# vrf forwarding MGMT
HQ-R1(config-subif)# ip address 10.10.99.1 255.255.255.0

! A route in the global table is not visible inside the VRF
HQ-R1(config)# ip route vrf MGMT 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 10.10.99.254

! Controlled leaking: export/import with route targets
HQ-R1(config)# ip vrf MGMT
HQ-R1(config-vrf)# route-target export 65010:99
HQ-R1(config-vrf)# route-target import 65010:99
!

MPLS L3VPN in one page

An MPLS provider network forwards using labels, not IP lookups. The PE router holds customer VRFs; the P router switches labels; the CE router belongs to the customer. A route distinguisher (RD) makes a customer prefix unique in MP-BGP, and route targets (RT) control which VRFs import which routes. The label stack can be two or more deep: an outer transport label to reach the egress PE, and an inner VPN label to identify the customer VRF. Penultimate-hop popping removes the outer label before the PE, which then uses the inner label.

VXLAN encapsulates Layer 2 frames in UDP, with a 24-bit VNI that supports 16 million segments. A VTEP adds and removes the encapsulation. The underlay is a routed, ECMP-capable IP network; the overlay control plane is usually BGP EVPN, which distributes MAC and IP reachability and avoids flood-and-learn. Bad decisionCreating VRFs without a route-leaking and DNS plan

VRFs isolate by default, including the DNS server, the jump host and the monitoring system. Users authenticate but cannot resolve; backups fail; SNMP polls time out. The segmentation is technically correct and operationally unusable.

Why it is tempting

Isolation looks like security, and the VRF command is easy. The shared services are discovered later.

Do this instead

List shared services first (DNS, AD, NTP, syslog, backup, monitoring), design controlled leaking or a services VRF, and test every flow before migrating users.

Bad decisionBuilding VXLAN on an underlay with no MTU or ECMP plan

VXLAN adds 50 bytes or more. If the underlay is 1500 bytes end to end and ICMP is filtered, large frames black-hole. If the underlay has a single path, all the overlay's advertised benefits disappear under load.

Why it is tempting

The overlay is logical; the physical network is "someone else's problem".

Do this instead

Raise the underlay MTU (often 9000 on data-centre links), enable ECMP, verify with large pings, and monitor per-path utilisation. Test failure scenarios, not just the happy path.

Advanced routing design: policy, reflectors and redistribution

At scale, routing is less about making prefixes appear and more about controlling where they are allowed to go.

Three mechanisms do most of the work in a mature routing design. Hierarchy limits the size of the topology each router must compute. Summarisation hides detail and contains churn at a boundary. Policy decides which prefixes are trusted, preferred or forbidden. Get these right and the network is predictable; get them wrong and every change is an adventure.

iBGP scaling: route reflectors and confederations

A full iBGP mesh does not scale. A route reflector (RR) relaxes the split-horizon rule for its clients: routes learned from one client can be reflected to other clients and to non-clients. Redundancy requires at least two RRs in a cluster, with clients peering to both. Confederations divide one AS into sub-ASes, reducing the mesh inside each sub-AS; they are less common now that RR design is well understood.

IOS-XE · route reflector and policy attributescopy
! On the route reflector
RR1(config)# router bgp 65010
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.2 remote-as 65010
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.2 update-source Loopback0
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.2 route-reflector-client
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.3 remote-as 65010
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.3 update-source Loopback0
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.3 route-reflector-client

! Prefer a path within the AS, and mark it so it is never exported
RR1(config)# ip community-list standard NO-EXPORT permit 65010:100
RR1(config)# route-map RM-PREFER permit 10
RR1(config-route-map)# set local-preference 200
RR1(config-route-map)# set community 65010:100 additive
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 10.255.255.2 route-map RM-PREFER in

! Cap what an external peer can send, or risk memory exhaustion
RR1(config-router)# neighbor 198.51.100.2 maximum-prefix 500000 90 restart 15
!

Local preference is the main outbound path control inside an AS; it is carried in iBGP and not sent to eBGP peers. MED is a hint to a neighbouring AS about which entrance you prefer; it is compared only between paths from the same AS (by default). Communities are tags that carry policy across AS boundaries: no-export, no-advertise, or a provider's own customer/peer/transit tags. If you do not set them deliberately, you inherit whatever your provider assumes.

Redistribution without loops

Every redistribution boundary is a potential loop. A route learned from OSPF and redistributed into EIGRP can be redistributed back into OSPF by another router, each time with a worse metric. The standard defences are a route tag, a route map that denies the tag on the way back, and a seed metric that makes the path unattractive.

IOS-XE · tagging and filtering at the boundarycopy
! Tag routes coming from OSPF so they are never redistributed back into OSPF
HQ-R1(config)# route-map RM-OSPF-TO-EIGRP permit 10
HQ-R1(config-route-map)# set tag 110
HQ-R1(config-route-map)# set metric 100000 10 255 1 1500
HQ-R1(config)# router eigrp 100
HQ-R1(config-router)# redistribute ospf 10 route-map RM-OSPF-TO-EIGRP

! Refuse anything already tagged as OSPF when redistributing the other way
HQ-R1(config)# route-map RM-EIGRP-TO-OSPF deny 10
HQ-R1(config-route-map)# match tag 110
HQ-R1(config)# route-map RM-EIGRP-TO-OSPF permit 20
HQ-R1(config-route-map)# set tag 90
HQ-R1(config-router)# router ospf 10
HQ-R1(config-router)# redistribute eigrp 100 subnets route-map RM-EIGRP-TO-OSPF

! OSPF area filtering: keep a prefix out of an area entirely
HQ-R1(config-router)# area 1 filter-list prefix PL-BLOCK out
!
Bad decisionComparing MED values from different ASes

MED is not a global preference. Cisco compares it only among paths from the same neighbouring AS unless bgp always-compare-med is enabled. Enabling that globally can produce path selection that contradicts your providers' traffic engineering, especially in a multi-homed design.

Why it is tempting

The lower MED looks like the better path, and the knob exists.

Do this instead

Use local preference for outbound control, AS-path prepending or communities for inbound influence, and leave MED comparisons scoped to a single AS unless you have a documented reason.

Bad decisionMutual redistribution without tags or filters

Two routers redistributing in both directions create a feedback loop. Prefixes bounce between protocols, metrics inflate, and the routing table oscillates. The logs look fine because each individual redistribution is "working".

Why it is tempting

Each direction is a single command, and the prefixes do appear.

Do this instead

Tag at the boundary, deny tagged routes on re-entry, set explicit seed metrics, and prefer a single redistribution point (or a deliberate pair with consistent policy). Test with a topology change.

BGP is a trust system

Every eBGP session is a business relationship. Use prefix filters, maximum-prefix limits, RPKI origin validation where available, and explicit communities. "The ISP will only send us the right routes" is not a control.

Field drill 37

Build two routing domains (OSPF and EIGRP) with two redistribution points. Without tags, demonstrate the feedback loop by observing metric changes. Add tags and deny rules, then prove the loop stops with show ip route and traceroute.

  1. Write your dump sheet first. Subnet blocks, AD values, OSPF states, BGP order, port numbers. Offload memory before the timer pressures you.
  2. Budget time. CCNA and ENCOR are 120 minutes; concentrations are 90. Aim for about a minute per multiple-choice question and reserve time for simulations or drag-and-drop items.
  3. Read the last line first. "Which command verifies…" changes how you read the scenario. Underline the actual question in your mind.
  4. Eliminate, then commit. Two answers are usually clearly wrong. If you are stuck after 90 seconds, flag it, choose your best answer, and move on.
  5. Do not fight a simulation. If a lab item will not converge, check the basics (interface state, addressing, routing) and use the available partial credit rather than burning ten minutes.
  6. Review flagged items only if time remains. Changing answers without new evidence usually lowers scores.

Bad decisionStudying only the domains you enjoy

Engineers who like routing skip wireless; engineers who like security skip subnetting. The exam samples every domain, and the weakest domain sets the floor. A 90% in routing does not compensate for a 40% in automation.

Why it is tempting

Competence feels good, and the weak topic is uncomfortable.

Do this instead

Use practice results to rank domains, then spend the next week on the bottom two. Track accuracy by domain, not just overall score.

Bad decisionCramming the night before

Sleep is when memory consolidates. A late night of flashcards trades durable recall for a short-term feeling of readiness, and the exam is a marathon of attention. Arrive rested, fed and early.

Why it is tempting

There is always one more fact, and anxiety demands action.

Do this instead

Stop intensive study 24 hours before. Review your dump sheet and one summary page, sleep, and treat the exam as a performance you have already rehearsed.

Exam lens

When two answers both look correct, the exam is usually testing a qualifier: "first", "most likely", "best practice", "verifies". Re-read the qualifier. The longest answer is not automatically right, and "all of the above" is rare.

Field drill 41

Build a 20-question practice set from your weakest three chapters, set a 25-minute timer, and take it without notes. Mark every wrong answer with the reason (knowledge gap, misread, or guess), then schedule the gap for tomorrow.

Table A-3 · Terms used without apology throughout the manual
TermMeaning in this manual
ABRArea Border Router: an OSPF router attached to two or more areas, injecting summaries.
ADAdministrative distance: trust ranking between routing sources (connected 0, static 1, OSPF 110, iBGP 200).
ASBRAutonomous System Boundary Router: injects external routes into OSPF.
BFDBidirectional Forwarding Detection: fast liveness check used by routing protocols and SD-WAN.
CAM / MAC tableThe switch's map of MAC address and VLAN to port; overflow causes flooding.
CAPWAPControl and provisioning protocol between lightweight APs and a WLC (UDP 5246/5247).
DSCP6-bit QoS marking in the IP header; EF 46 for voice, AF classes for assured forwarding.
FHRPFirst-hop redundancy protocol: HSRP, VRRP or GLBP providing a shared default gateway.
FIBForwarding Information Base: the hardware-optimised forwarding table derived from the RIB.
LSALink-State Advertisement: the unit of OSPF topology data.
MABMAC Authentication Bypass: authenticating a device by its MAC; convenient, spoofable.
MQCModular QoS CLI: class-map, policy-map and service-policy configuration model.
OMPOverlay Management Protocol: SD-WAN control plane for routes, TLOCs and policy.
PDUProtocol Data Unit: the unit at a given layer (frame, packet, segment).
RIBRouting Information Base: the routing table built by the control plane.
SGTSecurity Group Tag: a scalable policy identifier used by TrustSec/SD-Access.
SLAService Level Agreement: the measurable commitment (latency, availability) a design must meet.
STPSpanning Tree Protocol: builds a loop-free Layer 2 topology by blocking redundant ports.
TLOCTransport Locator: an SD-WAN path identifier (address, colour, encapsulation).
VNIVXLAN Network Identifier: 24-bit segment ID, far larger than a 12-bit VLAN ID.
VRFVirtual Routing and Forwarding: an independent routing table inside a device.
