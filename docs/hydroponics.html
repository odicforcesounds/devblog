01What a plant actually needs

A plant does not eat soil. Soil holds the roots still, keeps them damp, lets air reach them and slowly releases minerals. Do those four jobs another way and the soil becomes optional — not magic, just one supplier among several.

job 1

Support

Net pots, clay pebbles, rockwool cubes or a foam collar hold the stem upright. A heavy fruit needs a string or a stake, not the pot.

job 2

Water

The solution reaches the roots continuously or in cycles. What soil did by capillarity, here a pump, a wick or a falling film does.

job 3

Oxygen

Roots respire. Wet roots without air suffocate in days, which is why every system that works either moves the water or bubbles air through it.

job 4

Minerals

Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and the trace elements arrive dissolved and measured, instead of waiting to be released by soil life.

The honest ledger

Hydroponics is a tool for space, control and clean water — not an automatic claim to sustainability. The trade is specific, and it is worth knowing before you buy anything.

What you gain

  • 70–90 % less water than the same crop in open soil, because nothing drains away.
  • No weeds, no soil-borne pests, no digging, no backache.
  • Growth is faster when light and oxygen are right — often two or three weeks per cycle.
  • The garden moves with you: indoors in November, balcony in April.
  • You see the plant's food. EC and pH are a dashboard no soil grower gets.

What you pay for it

  • A pump or power failure is fatal within hours in warm weather — the roots have no reservoir of soil.
  • Water chemistry becomes your job, and mistakes show up as leaf edges two weeks later.
  • Energy: a controlled-environment study found lettuce yields 11× higher per area but 82× the energy per head, mostly lighting.
  • Plastic, salts and meters are consumables; a system is a small appliance, not a closed loop.
  • You buy the nutrients. Compost does not dissolve, and manure tea does not belong in a recirculating reservoir.
The one-line version: hydroponics replaces soil with plumbing, and plumbing fails loudly. It is at its best where soil is impossible — a fourth-floor flat, a dark January, a balcony with 0.4 m² — and at its worst as a promise that it will save money.

02Six systems, one ladder

Six ways to keep roots wet and breathing. They are ordered by how much can go wrong, not by how impressive they look: start at the top and move down only when a plant — not boredom — gives you the reason.

  1. Start here: Kratky, or a wick

    No electricity, no timer, no noise. Lettuce, basil, rocket, pak choi. If this fails, the cause is temperature or light, and you learn it for €5.

  2. Then: one deep-water bucket

    Add an air pump and a 20 L reservoir and you can grow a tomato. The pump is the first thing that can break, and the first thing worth learning to hear.

  3. Then: drip or a channel

    A timer, emitters, a return line: this is where a home system starts to behave like a small machine and needs a weekly inspection, not a glance.

  4. Only if you must: aeroponics

    The fastest growth and the shortest grace period. A clogged nozzle kills a crop before lunch. Run it beside a bucket, never instead of one.

best first system

Kratky — passive, no pump, no electricity

A jar, a net pot, nutrient solution. The roots drink, the level drops, an air gap forms and the upper roots breathe it. Nothing moves, nothing hums. Lettuce, basil, rocket, pak choi: one plant per 1–3 litre jar, 4–6 weeks.

watch out

Limits

Water temperature decides everything: above 24 °C the solution runs out of oxygen and root rot starts. Not for tomatoes or anything that lives four months. Change the solution every two weeks; never top up to the brim once roots hang in the air gap.

the workhorse

Deep water culture — bucket, lid, air stone

10–20 litres in a bucket, an aquarium air pump with a stone, roots permanently submerged and constantly oxygenated. Fast growth, forgiving of a missed top-up, and the cheapest way to grow a real tomato plant indoors.

watch out

Details

Keep the water at 18–22 °C — a black bucket in the sun will cook it; wrap it in foil or paint it white. One 3–5 W pump per two buckets is enough. If the pump stops for a day in summer, the plant may not survive.

for many small plants

Nutrient film technique — a shallow stream in a channel

A pump lifts solution to one end of a tilted pipe; a 2–3 mm film runs past the roots and drains back into the tank. Excellent for 20 lettuce heads on a shelf, poor for fruit: the channel is too small for a big root ball.

watch out

Details

A blocked channel starves a whole row in an hour. Slope 1–2 %, never more. Filter the return, and check flow daily in hot weather.

for fruit

Drip — a pot of pebbles, watered on a timer

Each plant sits in clay pebbles; a small pump drips solution onto the pot three to six times a day and it drains back to the tank. The standard way to grow tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, strawberries and herbs from one shared reservoir.

watch out

Details

Emitters clog with nutrient salts — clean them monthly in warm water. Because the reservoir is shared, one sick plant spreads to all: inspect roots weekly.

advanced

Aeroponics — roots in air, misted

Roots hang in a closed box; nozzles spray a fine mist every few minutes. The highest oxygen levels of any method and the fastest growth — and the least tolerant of failure: a clogged nozzle means dead roots in hours.

watch out

Details

Needs a timer with seconds, a filter, and a spare pump. Do not start here. If you want the experiment, run it beside a DWC so one crop survives.

simplest possible

Wick — a cord does the pumping

A cotton or nylon cord runs from a reservoir into a pot of pebbles or coco coir and pulls solution up by capillarity. Good for herbs, lettuce and a windowsill, and it cannot flood the roots.

watch out

Details

Weak flow limits big plants, and the cord clogs with salts after a month or two — replace it. Coco coir buffers the pH and hides the water, so check the reservoir weekly rather than the surface.

03Build it from the city's waste

Every reservoir in this manual can be bought new for forty euros, or found for nothing. A city produces exactly the containers a hydroponic garden needs — food-grade drums, bakery crates, pipes, shelving — and throws most of them away after one use. The skill is not finding them. It is knowing which ones are safe.

Why reuse beats recycling. A kilo of new HDPE carries roughly 1.8–2 kg of CO₂e before it holds a drop of water; a 20-litre drum weighs about a kilo. Reusing it saves the material, the moulding energy and the €6 — while recycling it saves only part of the first. The EU's own hierarchy puts prevention and reuse above recycling, and the plastics strategy is built on that order. A garden that buys new plastic every season is not circular, whatever the bucket is made of.

The rule that matters more than the number

The little triangle tells you the polymer. It does not tell you what the container held, who made it, or what was added to it. History beats code: a #2 drum that carried screen wash is scrap, and a #5 tub that carried yoghurt is a seedling pot after one wash. Buy nothing that ever held a biocide, a solvent, a fuel, a paint or anything whose label you cannot read.

Clean it like a vessel, not like a dish

A used container carries two things you cannot see: the residue of what it held, and a biofilm that will feed algae and root rot. Five steps, forty minutes, once per container.

  1. Strip the label and the glue

    Warm water with a spoon of bicarbonate, or a little cooking oil on the adhesive. Solvents are not worth the risk on something that will hold plant food.

  2. Scrub, do not soak

    Hot water, washing-up liquid, a brush that reaches the corners. Rinse until the water runs clear and no foam remains.

    Never use a scouring pad on the inside of a reservoir: the scratches become a permanent home for biofilm.

  3. Sanitise with dilute bleach

    One part thin household bleach (about 5 %) to nine parts water, ten minutes of contact, then two full rinses. Three per cent hydrogen peroxide works too, at 3 ml per litre, with no rinse needed.

    Never mix bleach with an acid — including pH-down. Bleach plus acid releases chlorine gas.

  4. Dry it in the sun

    Ultraviolet light is free disinfectant. An hour upside down on a balcony does more than a second wash. Store dry, lid off, so it cannot grow a smell.

  5. Test the rinse water

    Fill with clean water, stir, read the EC. If it climbs above 0.1 mS/cm, something is still dissolving out of the plastic. Rinse again, or demote the container to holding pebbles.

Five builds, from a bottle to a tote

Each is a complete, working system. The dimensions are the ones that fit a windowsill or a balcony in a Portuguese flat; scale them, do not shrink them — a reservoir that is too small is the most common reason a build fails in August.

build 1 · €0–3 · 20 minutes

The 5-litre bottle Kratky

One bottle, one net pot, one lettuce. The cheapest honest test of whether you enjoy this.

  • A 5 L PET water bottle, label off, top cut at the shoulder
  • A net pot that sits in the neck, or a plastic cup with holes
  • Clay pebbles, or washed gravel
  • A seedling in a rockwool cube or a plug
  1. Wrap the bottle in paper or paint it: clear plastic grows algae in a week.
  2. Fill with solution to about a third, EC 0.8, pH 5.8.
  3. Seat the plant so the cube's bottom just touches the liquid.
  4. Never top up to the brim. The air gap that forms as the level drops is what the upper roots breathe.
One crop per bottle: PET softens in sun and holds smells. Refill once, then recycle it and start a new one.
build 2 · €12 new, €0 found · one afternoon

The 20-litre drum DWC

The workhorse. Two lettuce, or one tomato, with an air stone and a lid you can cut.

  • A 20 L HDPE drum: a food bucket from a bakery, or a jerry can from a car wash
  • An aquarium air pump, 2–3 W, with stone and 1 m of tube
  • Two 8 cm net pots and 3 L of clay pebbles
  • A lid, or a piece of rigid foam board cut to size
  1. Cut two holes in the lid, 8 cm, spaced as far apart as the drum allows.
  2. Drill a 6 mm notch for the air tube at the rim, not through the lid's middle.
  3. Fill to 2 cm below the pots, start the pump, and check for bubbles before planting.
  4. Wrap the drum in an old rice sack: it keeps light out and heat down for nothing.
Food buckets from bakeries held mayonnaise, olives or pickles — safe after the wash. A drum that held engine oil never becomes a reservoir, whatever you clean it with.
build 3 · €15 new, €5 found · for 6–10 plants

The gutter or crate NFT

A length of gutter, a bakery crate with a liner, or a plastic cable tray: anything that holds a 2 mm film and slopes.

  • 2 m of PVC or PP gutter, with two end caps
  • A 10 L tank and a 5 W submersible pump
  • Net pots, 5 cm, one per 20 cm
  • A lid or black membrane to keep light off the film
  1. Set the channel on two bricks: 2 cm of fall over 2 m is 1 %, which is enough.
  2. Feed at the high end through a 4 mm tube; drain at the low end through a bulkhead or a drilled cap.
  3. Cut pot holes 20 cm apart and cover the rest of the channel with a lid.
  4. Run the pump continuously for lettuce; five minutes on, fifteen off for herbs.
Old PVC gutters can contain lead stabilisers and are a poor choice for edible crops. Prefer new PP, or a food crate with a food-grade liner.
build 4 · €20–40 found · a balcony or a yard

The IBC tote, cut in half

A 1000 L IBC that carried juice or syrup is the best free reservoir a city offers: food-grade HDPE, a steel cage, and a tap already fitted.

  • One 1000 L IBC, food-grade, with its cage and pallet
  • A saw, a file, and gloves
  • A submersible pump and 13 mm hose
  • Net pots and pebbles for the raft, or six pots for drip
  1. Cut it a third from the top: the shallow part becomes a raft bed, the deep part a reservoir.
  2. File every edge — a cut IBC is a blade. Wear gloves, not sandals.
  3. Drill an overflow 3 cm below the raft's rim and plumb it back to the reservoir.
  4. Float a foam board with 5 cm holes: lettuce and basil on top, water underneath.
A full 1000 L tote weighs a tonne. Put it on a ground-floor terrace or over a load-bearing wall, never on a balcony slab without checking what it was designed for.
build 5 · €0 · five minutes

The wick tub, for herbs and a north window

Two tubs stacked, one wick, no electricity. The one system that survives a fortnight away.

  • A 1 L yoghurt tub with its lid, plus a second tub as the reservoir
  • A strip of old cotton T-shirt, 3 cm wide and 20 cm long
  • Coco coir or pebbles, and a herb seedling
  1. Cut a slot in the upper tub's base and thread the cotton through, 5 cm inside.
  2. Fill the lower tub with solution, set the upper tub inside so the wick dips.
  3. Plant into the coir, water once from above, and leave it.
  4. Replace the wick every two months: it clogs with salts and stops lifting water.
Wicks suit basil, mint, parsley and lettuce. They cannot deliver enough water for a tomato in July.

Where a city keeps its parts

Ask once, in person, and most of these become a weekly supply. Nobody at a bakery is attached to the bucket the mayonnaise came in.

Twelve sources, what to ask for, and the catch
Source Ask for Becomes Catch

Never in a reservoir, whatever the label says:

  • Anything that held oil, fuel, antifreeze, screen wash, paint, solvent, pesticide or a cleaning product. Plastic absorbs; rinsing does not undo it.
  • Black recycled plastic from electronics, for anything that touches solution. It is often e-waste derived and can carry flame retardants.
  • Galvanised steel, copper, brass or zinc fittings in contact with the solution — zinc and copper are toxic to roots at surprisingly low levels.
  • Old PVC pipe or vinyl hose for edible crops; historic lead stabilisers are the reason it is avoided in drinking-water plumbing.
  • Treated, painted or unknown timber, and pallets stamped MB (methyl bromide). HT, heat-treated, is the one to take.
  • Tyres, polystyrene fish boxes and anything that smells of its last contents after two washes.
About microplastics, honestly. Every plastic reservoir sheds a little, and warm, slightly acidic nutrient solution is a mildly aggressive environment. Choose HDPE or PP over PET, keep the tank out of the sun, do not scrub the inside, and replace anything cracked or chalky. The World Health Organization found no evidence of harm from microplastics in drinking water at current levels, and your lettuce is not drinking it — but "no evidence of harm" is not the same as "no plastic", and a glass jar is still the better choice for a windowsill.

04Water is the medium

In soil, water is an ingredient. Here it is the room the roots live in, so its temperature, its hardness and what your municipality put in it all become part of the recipe. Four sources, in the order a city dweller should consider them.

the best one

Rain from your own roof

Nearly zero EC, no chlorine, free. One millimetre of rain on one square metre of roof is one litre; forty square metres of roof in Lisbon collect about 25 000 litres a year, which is a hundred times what a small system drinks.

the default

Tap water

Perfectly usable. Let it stand 12–24 hours for chlorine, or neutralise chloramine with 50 mg of ascorbic acid per 10 litres. Read its EC before you add anything: that number is your floor.

the free bonus

Condensate from an air conditioner

A working split unit in a Portuguese summer produces litres a day at almost zero EC. Fine for topping up a reservoir; not for drinking, and not for an aeroponic mister, where the aerosol is inhaled.

with a caveat

Water from an aquarium

Mild nutrients and living microbes — excellent for a wick pot or the soil garden, risky in a sterile recirculating system where you cannot see what is blooming. Never pour it into a shared reservoir.

Catching rain without making a mess

A downpipe, a first-flush diverter, a filter and an opaque tank with a mosquito screen. The first two litres off a dry roof carry bird droppings and dust — discard them. Keep the tank dark: light grows algae, and an algae tank costs you more in pH swings than the water saved. On a balcony, a 100–200 L barrel under a downpipe is enough for a small system and a few pots, and it weighs 200 kg when full, so put it over a wall, not over the neighbour.

Hard water. If your tap starts above EC 0.6, part of the reading is calcium and magnesium you did not add. Use rain or filtered water, or subtract the tap reading from the target and feed the difference. Never chase EC 3.0 with water that already sits at 1.0 without watching the leaf tips for burn.

Temperature is oxygen

Warm water holds less oxygen, and roots burn through oxygen faster when it is warm. This is the single number that decides whether a passive system lives through August. Keep the solution under 24 °C, and treat 26 °C as an alarm.

Oxygen saturation of fresh water at sea level, and what it means for roots
Water °C O₂ saturation mg/L What happens
15 10.1 Comfortable. Lettuce grows without complaint.
20 9.1 The target range for most home systems.
24 8.4 The line. Add air, shade the tank, or both.
28 7.8 Warm, oxygen-poor, and where root rot begins.
30 7.6 Emergency: chill, shade, increase flow, or harvest early.

Roots want at least 5 mg/L of dissolved oxygen, which is why an air stone is not optional in deep water culture. A frozen 1.5 L bottle dropped into the tank in the evening buys a hot afternoon; a white or foil-wrapped tank buys the whole summer; a pump that runs at night buys the margin between the two.

05Nutrients and the only two numbers

pH and EC. One says whether the food is available, the other says how much food is there. Two meters cost about €30 together, and they replace every guess in this manual.

Target ranges by crop (mS/cm = EC; 1 mS/cm ≈ 500–700 ppm depending on the meter's scale)
Crop pH EC mS/cm Days to harvest Litres / plant

pH: the lock on the cupboard

Most crops want pH 5.5–6.5. Below 5.5 calcium and magnesium lock up; above 6.5 iron does. Adjust downwards with phosphoric or citric acid, upwards with potassium bicarbonate — a few drops at a time, stirring between each, because the response is not linear. Then measure again 24 hours later: drift is normal, a jump of more than 0.5 in a day means too little water or too much plant.

EC: the strength of the soup

EC measures total dissolved salts, not a nutrient. It cannot tell nitrogen from sodium. That is why you measure it, note it, and correct it — rather than trusting a bottle's label. Dose by EC, start at half strength, and work up over two weeks.

Mix in the right order, always

  1. Water first, chlorine gone

    Fill the reservoir, let it stand or treat it. Everything that follows is measured against that water, hard or soft.

  2. Part A, stir, wait a minute

    Calcium sits in A. Add it to the full volume, never to a concentrate, and stir until the water is clear again.

  3. Part B, stir, wait a minute

    Sulphates and phosphates are in B. Poured together as concentrates, A and B make a white precipitate the plant cannot absorb — and that sludge is the most common beginner's mistake.

  4. pH last, then re-read EC

    Acid changes conductivity slightly. Adjust pH, stir, read EC, and write both numbers in the logbook.

vegetative

Grow formula

More nitrogen, less potassium. Lettuce and herbs stay here for life; tomatoes and peppers stay here until the first flower opens.

fruiting

Bloom formula

More potassium and phosphorus once fruit has set. Dropping EC in the last week before harvest reduces the nitrate taste in leaves.

Micro-elements are already in the bottle. Iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper and molybdenum arrive chelated. Adding more is not generosity — it is how people poison a crop, and iron in particular becomes unavailable the moment pH drifts above 6.5.

Can you mix your own nutrients?

Yes, in principle: calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, monopotassium phosphate, magnesium sulphate and a chelated trace mix will make a solution that grows lettuce. In practice, at home scale, the savings are a few euros a year and the errors are measured in dead plants. A two-part hobby nutrient is cheaper than the mistake. The honest exceptions are rain water (you may need to add magnesium and calcium) and long-term cropping, where a water analysis from a lab is worth more than any bottle.

06Seed to plate, in twelve steps

The order matters as much as the steps. Nearly every failed first attempt skipped light, or oxygen, or stirred the two nutrient bottles together.

  1. Choose the site before buying anything

    A south-facing window gives four to six hours of weak winter light in Portugal — enough for lettuce, not for tomatoes. A shelf with a 30–50 W LED bar 20–30 cm above the canopy, 14–16 hours a day, is the reliable answer. Keep the reservoir out of direct sun: light on water grows algae.

    Measure it: a phone light-meter app at canopy height should read 15 000–25 000 lux for leaves, more for fruit. If you want the real number, think in DLI — lettuce wants roughly 12–17 mol/m²/day.

  2. Start the seeds in inert media

    Rockwool cubes, peat-free plugs or a cup of perlite. Soak rockwool in pH 5.5 water for 30 minutes first — it arrives alkaline. Sow two seeds per cube, keep at 18–22 °C, and give light the moment the first green shows; a seedling that stretches thin on a windowsill never recovers.

    No nutrients for the first 7–10 days. The seed carries its own food; feeding too early burns the root tip.

  3. Mix the solution, in the right order

    Fill the reservoir and let it sit 12–24 hours so the chlorine leaves (or neutralise chloramine with 50 mg of ascorbic acid per 10 L). Add part A, stir, add part B, stir. Never pour the two concentrates together.

    Dose by EC, not by the label. Start at half strength and work up over two weeks.

  4. Set pH, then verify EC

    Aim for pH 5.5–6.5, adjusting a few drops at a time and stirring between each. Then read EC — the total salt strength — and write both numbers down. The logbook is the only way to see a drift before the plant shows it.

    Check the same solution again 24 hours later. Drift is normal; a jump of more than 0.5 pH in a day means too little water or too much plant.

  5. Transplant when the roots leave the cube

    Two or three true leaves, roots visible at the sides. Rinse the medium of loose crumbs, seat the cube in the net pot, fill with clay pebbles so the stem is supported and the cube's bottom touches the water or the film. Never bury the crown — it rots.

  6. Keep the roots breathing

    Air stone in DWC, film movement in NFT, drain cycles in drip. Water above 24 °C holds less oxygen: shade the tank, float a frozen bottle on hot days, keep the pump running at night. Roots should be white or cream; brown and slimy is rot.

    Root rot smells. If the reservoir smells like a pond, empty it, rinse the roots in clean water, and restart with fresh solution and 3 ml/L of 3 % hydrogen peroxide.

  7. Keep a weekly rhythm

    Top up with plain water to the mark, then measure EC and pH and correct. Remove dead leaves. Look at the underside of leaves for pests. Every two weeks, in small systems, empty and remix — the ratios drift even when the numbers look right.

  8. Feed by stage, not by calendar

    Grow formula until flowers, bloom formula after fruit set. Lettuce stays on grow formula its whole life. A plant that is pale and slow wants more light or warmer roots far more often than it wants more fertiliser.

  9. Pollinate by hand indoors

    No wind, no bees. Shake tomato canes daily at midday, or touch each flower with a soft brush. Peppers self-pollinate with a light shake. Strawberries need a brush, flower by flower. Cucumbers are the fussiest: open the windows, or accept a low set.

  10. Harvest small and often

    Cut lettuce 2 cm above the crown and it regrows once. Take basil from the top, above a node. Pick outer leaves before they yellow: a plant that is harvested keeps producing, and one left to "get big" usually bolts instead.

  11. Sow the next before you clear the last

    Start the next batch two weeks before the current one ends. A garden that is always 80 % full beats a garden that is empty every second month — and succession is the only way a home system produces anything like a supply rather than an event.

  12. Clean, store, reset

    Empty, scrub, sanitise, dry. Store the pump dry and the pebbles in a sack, not in the tank. Note what worked in the logbook while you still remember it, and change one thing next cycle — not five.

    One part bleach to nine of water for the tank and pots, two rinses. An infection reused is an infection repeated.

07What grows, and what refuses

Hydroponics favours anything harvested above the water line and fast to mature. Roots and tubers — carrot, potato, bulb onion, beetroot — deform or rot in a liquid medium, because there is nothing to push against.

excellent

Leafy greens & herbs

Lettuce, rocket, spinach, kale, chard, pak choi, mizuna, watercress, basil, mint, parsley, coriander, dill, chives, thyme, oregano, microgreens.

excellent

Fruit from a small plant

Strawberries (the best hydroponic fruit by a wide margin), alpine strawberries, cherry tomatoes, chillies and small sweet peppers, cucumbers, and — with summer light — melon and cape gooseberry.

good with care

Worth trying

Radish (fast but needs a deeper medium), spring onion, dwarf peas, French beans, blueberry (acid water, pH 4.5–5.5 — advanced), aubergine and full-size tomato (big reservoir, support, four months).

avoid

Poor fit

Carrot, potato, beetroot, turnip, parsnip, garlic and bulb onion (they need soil structure to size up), sweetcorn (too tall), cabbage and cauliflower (huge, slow), and anything that needs a cold period to flower.

The ten that forgive a beginner

  • Loose-leaf lettuce
  • Rocket
  • Basil
  • Mint
  • Pak choi
  • Watercress
  • Parsley
  • Microgreens
  • Spring onion
  • Strawberry crowns

All ten tolerate EC around 1.0–1.6, germinate at room temperature, and are harvested within six weeks. A first season built only from this list will produce something to eat; a first season built from tomatoes and melons will produce a lesson.

Fruit trees in a bucket are a myth worth naming: a lemon or a fig in hydroponics needs hundreds of litres of reservoir and years of pruning. For fruit at home, strawberries and cherry tomatoes are the honest answer.

08What to sow right now

Late October on the Atlantic coast: the outdoor garden is closing, which is exactly when an indoor system earns its space. Pick a month below — it opens on the current one.

    Why these and not the others

    Cool-season leaves germinate at 15–20 °C, tolerate 10 °C nights and bolt only when spring heat arrives — they are the crops that fit a windowsill in November. Warm-season fruit sown now will germinate and then stall: the light is falling by two minutes a day, and no nutrient fixes a light deficit. Either overwinter a chilli plant under a lamp at 14 hours, or wait until March.

    October–November, the short version
    Crop Where Harvest Note
    The one thing that changes everything indoors: light hours. Below 12 hours of decent light, lettuce still grows but slowly and thin. A 15 W LED bar at 20 cm for 14 hours a day turns a dark November windowsill into a productive shelf for about €25 of electricity a year.

    11Failures, in order of likelihood

    Almost every symptom in a home system traces back to four things: not enough oxygen, wrong pH, wrong strength, not enough light. Read the leaves before you open a bottle.

    The diagnosis order

    1. Smell and touch the water

      A pond smell or slimy roots means stop: fix oxygen and temperature before anything else.

    2. Read pH, then EC

      A meter that has not been calibrated in a month is a random number generator. Calibrate, then trust it.

    3. Take the temperature

      Water above 24 °C explains more yellowing than any missing nutrient.

    4. Look at the light

      Pale, stretched, small-leaved: too little. Bleached or scorched top leaves: too much, too close.

    5. Lift the lid

      Roots white or cream? Then the problem is above the water. Brown and mushy? Then it is the water.

    6. Only then the nutrients

      Change one thing, wait four days, look again. Two changes at once teach you nothing.

    Symptoms and the first move
    Symptom Most likely First move
    Lower leaves yellow, upper fine Nitrogen short, or old leaves dying naturally Raise EC by 0.2; if it is only the bottom pair, nothing is wrong
    Yellow between veins, veins stay green Magnesium or iron lockout — usually pH Check pH first, correct to 5.8–6.2 before adding anything
    Brown, crisp leaf edges Too strong a solution, or dry air Drop EC 0.3, top up with water, raise humidity
    Roots brown, slimy, smell Root rot from warm, oxygen-poor water Empty, rinse, H₂O₂ 3 ml/L, cool the tank below 22 °C
    Green film on water and pots Algae — light reaching the solution Opaque cover, black tubing, new solution
    pH climbs every day Algae, or too little water for the plant mass Bigger reservoir; check for light leaks
    Lettuce bolts early Heat and long days Cooler spot, shade the tank, choose slow-bolt varieties
    Flowers but no fruit No pollination indoors Shake the plant at midday; brush tomato and strawberry flowers
    Tiny black flies around the pots Fungus gnats in wet medium Let the surface dry, yellow sticky traps, Bti granules
    White crust on pebbles Salt residue — normal Rinse at each reservoir change; it is not a disease
    New leaves bleached white at the top Light burn — lamp too close Raise it 10 cm; recovery is slow but the plant survives
    Seedlings tall, thin, falling over Light too far or too weak from day one Lamp at 10–15 cm, 16 hours; restart the batch if they have stretched badly
    pH crashes overnight Anaerobic root zone — the water has gone sour Empty, clean, add air and cool it; do not pH-up a rotting tank
    Tomatoes split near the stem Watering that swings between dry and full Keep the level steady; a bigger reservoir fixes most of it

    Pests, and the honest first move

    Indoors there are no predators, so a small infestation is an infestation
    Pest Sign First move
    Aphids Clusters on new tips, sticky leaves, ants Blast with water, then soap spray 5 ml/L; repeat in five days
    Whitefly White specks that fly when you touch the plant Yellow sticky traps plus vacuuming the undersides; check the whole shelf
    Thrips Silvery streaks, black dots, distorted growth Blue traps, remove affected leaves, repeat every four days
    Spider mite Fine webbing, pale stippling in dry heat Raise humidity, wash the leaves, use a mite spray; they hate wet air
    Fungus gnat Small dark flies walking on the medium Dry the surface, sticky traps, Bti drench — the larvae eat roots
    Shore fly / algae fly Flies near a green reservoir lid Kill the algae, not the fly: opaque lid and a clean tank
    Deficiency by leaf position, in one line: old leaves first means mobile nutrients — nitrogen, magnesium, potassium. New leaves first means immobile ones — iron, calcium, boron. In hydroponics, nine times out of ten the deficiency is not missing from the bottle; it is locked out by pH, cold roots, or a reservoir that has been topped up with water for three weeks and never re-dosed.

    12Safety: water, mains, and eating the result

    Three separate risks, three separate habits. None of them is difficult; all of them are boring until the day they are not.

    electricity

    Water and 230 V

    • Everything on a socket protected by an RCD (interruptor diferencial) — no exceptions, not even for a 3 W pump.
    • Pumps and lights above the water line, cables with a drip loop, no joint inside the growing area.
    • 12 V air pumps are safer than 230 V ones; a small aquarium pump is enough for 20 L.
    • Never leave a DIY 230 V build running while you are away for a week.
    • Outdoors, use an IP44 box and a cable made for outside; a socket on a wet balcony is a trap.
    food

    Salad from a warm reservoir

    • Keep the solution below 24 °C: warm, nutrient-rich water is a bacterial incubator, and leafy greens are the crop most often linked to outbreaks.
    • Never use manure, compost tea or fish waste in a recirculating edible system. Compost belongs in soil, not in the tank.
    • Wash harvest in clean running water; do not eat leaves that sat in the solution.
    • Sanitise the tank and pots between crops; a biofilm carries the last crop's problems into the next.
    • People who are pregnant or immunocompromised should cook home-grown leafy greens rather than eat them raw.
    • Keep pets and children out of the reservoir; the nutrient bottles look like juice and taste like salt.

    Chemicals and structure

    • Acid into water, never water into acid. Add pH-down drop by drop, with the jug away from your face.
    • Never mix bleach and acid, and never store them side by side — the gas they make is the dangerous part, not the splash.
    • Label every bottle with a marker. "A" and "B" is not a label six months later.
    • A full 20 L bucket weighs 20 kg, a full IBC a tonne. Check the shelf, the bracket and the balcony before you fill anything.
    • Keep lights away from anything that can drip, and never hang a lamp by its own cable.

    14Keep a logbook, even a bad one

    Drift is invisible for two weeks and then obvious for one day. Six columns, one line a week, and the pattern does the diagnosing for you.

    A page from a working log — copy the shape, not the numbers
    Date pH EC Water °C Did Next time
    12 Oct 5.9 0.8 21 Mixed first solution at half strength, 2 lettuce in —
    19 Oct 6.4 1.1 22 Topped up 3 L, pH down 3 drops Reservoir too small for six plants
    26 Oct 6.0 1.0 24 Full change, added frozen bottle at noon Shade the tank before June
    2 Nov 5.8 1.3 19 First cut of outer leaves, sowed next batch Sow 10 days earlier

    Write the numbers even when they are fine, especially then. The week the EC climbs while the pH falls is the week the plant mass outgrew the reservoir, and you can only see it if the previous four weeks are on the page. A notebook by the bucket beats an app you open twice.

    15Sources, and what each one is good for

    Every number in this manual that is not arithmetic comes from one of these. They are public, free, and mostly boring in the way that reliable documents are.

    • FAO — Small-scale aquaponic food production (Technical Paper 589) The reference for dissolved oxygen, biofilters and water quality in small recirculating systems. Free PDF; the fish chapters are optional, the water chapters are not.
    • FAO — Good Agricultural Practices for greenhouse vegetable crops Soilless culture at commercial scale: substrates, nutrient solutions, climate control. Where the hobby numbers come from.
    • Virginia Tech Extension — Hydroponic production of edible crops: media systems Clear, practical, university-written: systems, media, and the plumbing that makes them work.
    • Penn State Extension — Hydroponics systems and principles of plant nutrition The best short explanation of what each nutrient does and what its absence looks like.
    • University of New Hampshire Extension — Hydroponics at Home A household-scale guide with sane equipment lists — useful for checking whether a plan is over-built.
    • Royal Horticultural Society — Hydroponics The conservative British view: what works in a cold conservatory, and why some crops are a waste of a pump.
    • Virginia Tech Extension — Calculating and using daily light integral How to turn hours and watts into the only lighting number plants care about.
    • Utah State University Extension — Which plastics are safe for food storage A short, sober guide to resin codes and reuse. This is the source behind the material table in section 03.
    • European Commission — Plastics strategy The policy frame: prevention, reuse, recycling, in that order, and what Europe actually requires.
    • European Commission — Microplastics What is restricted, what is measured, and how little of it is settled science.
    • World Health Organization — Microplastics in drinking-water The careful answer: no evidence of harm at current levels, and a clear call for more data. Read it before repeating either extreme.
    • Barbosa et al. (2015) — Land, water and energy of hydroponic vs conventional lettuce The 11× yield and 82× energy figures quoted in section 01. A US greenhouse, not a windowsill — but the trade-off is real.
    • Texas A&M AgriLife — Rainwater catchment area How to size a roof catchment and a tank, with the arithmetic behind the calculator in section 10.
    • Precious Plastic Open-source machines and a global map of small recyclers, if you want to go past reuse into shredding and moulding.
    • Área Metropolitana de Lisboa — home and community composting Lisbon's composting programme: the municipal route for the scraps a hydroponic garden cannot use.
    • Ivey et al. (2025) — Food safety in hydroponic food crop production A review of the outbreaks and the practices behind them. The reason section 12 exists.
    • US EPA — Recycling basics What actually happens to a bottle after the bin, and why "recyclable" is not the same as "recycled".
    • Ellen MacArthur Foundation — Plastics The circular-economy argument in one place: why reuse beats recycling, and what a system designed for it would look like.
