October 2026: what the tents tested

A chronology of the month that made a 52-year-old decree front-page news, and the split it exposed.

  1. 12 Oct

    Porto, Praça D. João I. Tents set up in the housing protest are removed by the authorities; the city council says the camp cannot stay in a public square. The protest continues without them.

    RTP, 12 Oct 2026 ↗
  2. 20 Oct

    Lisbon, Rossio. The PSP removes the camp at dawn. 55 people are detained for disobedience, then constituted arguidos and released with summonses. The camp had become the visible centre of the housing movement.

    RTP, 20 Oct 2026 ↗
  3. 22 Oct

    The legal argument goes public. Vital Moreira argues that the acampadas are unlawful and the intervention justified, provided the means are proportionate. A petition asks the Provedor de Justiça to clarify the 00h30 limit.

    DNOTICIAS, 26 Oct 2026 ↗
  4. 24 Oct

    The camp returns. People come back to Rossio and put tents up again, testing whether the removal had settled the legal question or only the pavement.

  5. 26 Oct

    The PGR declines, and asks parliament. Amadeu Guerra refuses to request a constitutional review of the 1974 decree and says parliament should revise it. The speaker of the Assembly says the Constitutional Court is the body to decide. Jorge Reis Novais and Teresa Violante call the police response a gross violation of the right to demonstrate.

    DNOTICIAS, 26 Oct 2026 ↗
  6. 30 Oct

    No new law. The decree remains in force, unamended, with its 00h30 limit and its article 12 about buildings — and with the argument about what those words mean now firmly in the open.

Why the month matters. Nothing in the statute book changed in October 2026. What changed was the interpretation: a rule that had been read loosely for decades was applied strictly, and the gap between the text and the constitutional right became visible. That is the moment a legal system is tested — not when the law is clear, but when it is old, ambiguous and inconvenient.

The argument the camp was making is set out in the housing proposal. The night it was cleared, and what the tents were replacing, is in Tendas e Templos. How to run a demonstration that survives contact with the police is in Manifestações, do zero ao herói.

If you are sleeping in the square

Eight things that decide whether the night ends with a licence, a fine or a detention. Practical, not legal advice.

  1. Talk to the council before the camp exists. Ask which service licenses occasional camps and which licenses occupation of public space. Ask in writing; keep the reply.
  2. File the notice two working days ahead. Three promoters, names, professions and addresses, the hour, the place, the purpose and the route. Get the receipt — it is the proof that you did your part.
  3. Ask for the camp licence, not just the demonstration. The owner or the entity managing the square must authorise it; the health delegate and the local police commander must give opinions. A licensed camp changes the whole conversation.
  4. Keep it small, quiet and passable. Clear fire lanes, no gas cylinders, no open flames, no generators at 3 a.m. The proportionality argument is won with facts about how little you disturbed.
  5. Never occupy a building. Article 12 of the decree and article 215 of the Penal Code are about buildings, and they are a different league from a tent on a square.
  6. Know the 00h30 argument in advance. If the camp is silent, say so, and be ready to explain why the rule's purpose — other people's sleep — is not engaged. If it is not silent, fix that before the police arrive.
  7. When the order comes, record it and ask for the auto. Who gave it, at what time, under which article, with what warning. Article 5.º, n.º 2 requires a written record and a copy for the promoters.
  8. Do not fight, and do not vanish. Disobedience is the offence that sticks. A lawyer, a complaint to the Provedor and, for promoters, an appeal within fifteen days are slower and stronger than a shove.

The asymmetry to keep in mind. The council can license the camp; the police can only react to it. Every document you have before the first tent goes up is a document the other side has to answer. Every document you do not have is a decision someone else makes about you at six in the morning.

Twelve words

The vocabulary of the argument, in the order it usually appears.

Acampada
A camp set up as a protest — tents in a square, not a holiday. The word the movement uses for itself.
Comunicação prévia
Prior notice. A duty to inform the council, not a request for permission.
Promotor
One of the three people who sign the notice and answer for the gathering — and who receive the heavier penalty for disobeying a dispersal order.
Auto
The written record an authority must draw up when it interrupts a gathering, stating its grounds. A copy goes to the promoters.
Contraordenação
An administrative offence. A fine, not a crime; no criminal record. Camping without a licence is one.
Desobediência
Disobedience to a legitimate order — the offence behind most of the October detentions.
Dispersão
The order to break up and leave. It must be legitimate, communicated and accompanied by the legal warning.
Domínio público
Public domain: the squares, streets, gardens and beaches owned by the public and administered by the council.
Flagrante delito
Caught in the act. The constitutional basis for detention without a prior court order.
Proporcionalidade
The test that a restriction must be suitable, necessary and not excessive for the right it protects.
Título de ocupação
The licence or authorisation that allows private use of public space — an esplanade, a stall, a stage, a camp.
Usurpação
Occupying someone else's immovable as if you had a right to it. Since 2025 it no longer requires violence, and it depends on a complaint.
