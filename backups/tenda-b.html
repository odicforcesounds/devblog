03

The 1974 decree, article by article

Decreto-Lei n.º 406/74 was written four months after the 25 April revolution and before the 1976 Constitution. It is still the law of assembly in Portugal. Open the articles people actually argue about.

art. 1.º The guarantee, and its old limits ›

“A todos os cidadãos é garantido o livre exercício do direito de se reunirem pacificamente em lugares públicos, abertos ao público e particulares, independentemente de autorizações…”

Peaceful assembly is guaranteed in public places, places open to the public and private places, “regardless of authorisations”, provided the purpose is not contrary to law, morals, the rights of persons, or public order and tranquillity.

Paragraph 2 also bans meetings whose object offends the honour of the sovereign bodies and the armed forces. It is a pre-constitutional clause that no one has used for decades and that is widely read as incompatible with the 1976 Constitution; it is quoted here because it is still printed in the text.

art. 2.º Notice, not permission ›

“…deverão avisar por escrito e com a antecedência mínima de dois dias úteis o governador civil do distrito ou o presidente da câmara municipal…”

Anyone planning a meeting, rally, demonstration or parade in a public place must give written notice at least two working days in advance to the mayor of the municipality where the gathering will happen. The notice must be signed by three promoters, identified by name, profession and address — or by the board, for an association. The council must issue a receipt.

The reference to the civil governor is a leftover: civil governors were abolished in 2011, and Lei Orgânica n.º 1/2011 replaced them with the mayor in this article. Notice is not a request for authorisation, and the receipt is evidence, not a permit.

art. 3.º What the notice must contain — and silence ›

The notice must state the hour, the place and the object of the meeting, and, for demonstrations or parades, the route. Authorities may only prevent gatherings whose object or purpose contradicts article 1. If they raise no objection within 24 hours, at the addresses the promoters gave, the gathering is treated as unopposed.

Read with the Constitution, this is the narrow gate: a council cannot ban a demonstration because of its message, only because of the specific harms article 1 lists.

Read the article ↗
art. 5.º The only grounds for stopping it ›

“As autoridades só poderão interromper… quando forem afastados da sua finalidade pela prática de actos contrários à lei ou à moral ou que perturbem grave e efectivamente a ordem e a tranquilidade públicas…”

Police may interrupt a gathering only when it has been diverted from its purpose by unlawful or immoral acts, or by acts that seriously and effectively disturb public order and tranquillity, the free exercise of other people's rights, or paragraph 2 of article 1. In that case they must draw up a record describing the grounds and give a copy to the promoters.

“Seriously and effectively” is the operative phrase. Annoyance, noise, inconvenience or a message the government dislikes are not enough. The written record is not a formality: it is the document a court will read.

art. 11.º The 00h30 rule ›

“As reuniões de outros ajuntamentos objectos deste diploma não poderão prolongar-se para além das 0,30 horas…”

Gatherings covered by the decree may not run past 00h30, except in a closed venue, in theatres, in buildings without residents, or in buildings whose residents are the promoters or have consented in writing.

The wording is awkward — “as reuniões de outros ajuntamentos” — and that is the centre of the 2026 dispute. Some jurists read the limit as applying to meetings and rallies, not to demonstrations; others read it as applying to every gathering in a public place, and the PSP has used it that way. The article's own rationale is other people's sleep, which matters when a vigil is silent.

art. 12.º Buildings, not squares ›

“Não é permitida a realização de reuniões, comícios ou manifestações com ocupação abusiva de edifícios públicos ou particulares.”

Meetings, rallies or demonstrations that involve the abusive occupation of public or private buildings are not permitted.

The word is edifícios — buildings. A tent in a square is not a building, and this article does not say what the police sometimes suggest it says. Occupying a building is a different act, with different consequences, including article 215 of the Penal Code.

art. 13.º The 100-metre ring ›

For security reasons, gatherings may be prevented within 100 metres of the seats of sovereign bodies, military installations and camps, militarised forces, prisons, diplomatic and consular missions, and party headquarters.

This is the article behind the familiar images of police lines pushing crowds away from the Assembly of the Republic. It is a distance rule, not a ban on criticising what happens inside the building.

Read the article ↗
art. 14.º–15.º Appeal, and who answers for a bad order ›

Decisions taken in breach of the decree can be appealed to the ordinary courts within fifteen days, by the promoters. Authorities that prevent or try to prevent the free exercise of assembly outside the legal conditions are liable under the Penal Code of the time and to disciplinary proceedings. Those who hold a gathering contrary to the decree commit qualified disobedience.

The cross-references are to the 1886 Penal Code (articles 291 and 329), which no longer exists. The conduct now maps onto abuse of power and the offences against authority, but the mismatch is a good sign of how old this text is.

04

The camping licence nobody mentions

This is the part that turns a symbolic tent into an administrative offence. It comes from 2002, not 1974, and it is enforced by the council, not the police.

Decreto-Lei 310/2002

Article 18.º — the licence

“A realização de acampamentos ocasionais fora dos locais adequados à prática do campismo e caravanismo fica sujeita à obtenção de licença da câmara municipal.” The request is made by the person responsible for the camp, and the licence depends on the owner's express authorisation.

Before granting it, the council needs a favourable opinion from the health delegate and from the commander of the PSP or the GNR. The licence is for a fixed period, never longer than the owner's authorisation, and can be revoked at any time.

Read the article ↗
Article 47.º, n.º 1, g)

The fine

Running an occasional camp without a licence is an administrative offence (contraordenação) punishable with a fine of €150 to €200. The council instructs the case and the mayor decides; the money is municipal revenue.

This is not a crime, it does not create a criminal record, and it is not enforced by the PSP. That is precisely why the police rarely rely on it — and why a council, not a police officer, is the authority that can licence the camp in advance.

Read the article ↗

The practical consequence. If the camp is planned, the licence is obtainable: the organiser asks the council, the owner of the land or the authority managing the square signs, the health delegate and the local police commander give their opinions, and the camp becomes a licensed occasional camp. If the camp is a surprise, none of that exists, and the only legal question left is whether the tent is protected expression or an unlicensed occupation. Both arguments can be true at once.

The camp in Rossio was not a camping holiday; it was an argument about rent. The proposal it was answering is on the housing page, and the wider question of who gets to stay in a city is in A Ponte.

05

Symbol or campsite?

The same tent can be three things at once: a message, an occupation and a place to sleep. Portuguese jurists disagree about which one the law sees first.

Reading one

The tent is the argument

Jorge Reis Novais and Teresa Violante argued in October 2026 that a tent in a housing protest is not a camping issue at all: it is the most literal way of saying “I have no house”. On that reading, applying camping regulations to it is a category mistake, and the 00h30 limit — whose purpose is other people's sleep — cannot justify removing a silent camp at dawn.

They also insist the problem is interpretation, not the age of the text: the decree has coexisted with the Constitution for fifty years, and the Constitution requires restrictions to be necessary and proportional.

DNOTICIAS, 26 Oct 2026 ↗
Reading two

A camp is a camp

Vital Moreira reached the opposite conclusion: the acampadas are unlawful, and the police are justified in ending them, provided the means used are proportionate. On this reading, the right to demonstrate protects the gathering, the placards and the march — not an indefinite takeover of a square with structures, generators and overnight stays.

This is the reading behind the operations: the camp is treated as an unlicensed occupation of public space, with the assembly decree supplying the order to disperse and the camping law supplying the administrative offence.

DNOTICIAS, 26 Oct 2026 ↗

What the European court adds. Article 11 of the European Convention protects assembly, and the Strasbourg court has repeatedly held that a demonstration may annoy, inconvenience and disturb. But the protection has limits: in Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania (2015), farmers blockaded highways with tractors and vehicles for two days, and the Grand Chamber held that their conviction did not violate Article 11 — the disruption was severe and the sanctions were not excessive. The question is rarely “is there a rule?”; it is “was this response necessary and proportionate to the specific interest at stake?”. That question can vindicate the camp or the police, depending on the facts.

The practical side — how to organise, negotiate and keep a demonstration legal — is in Manifestações, do zero ao herói (Portuguese) and Manifestations, from zero to hero (English). The night the tents were destroyed is told in Tendas e Templos.

06

“You must leave”: the order and the offence

Most of the 55 detentions in Rossio were for disobedience, not for camping. The distinction matters, because the two have different authors, different courts and different defences.

Código Penal, art. 304.º

Disobeying a dispersal order

“Quem não obedecer a ordem legítima de se retirar de ajuntamento ou reunião pública, dada por autoridade competente, com advertência de que a desobediência constitui crime…”

The penalty is up to one year in prison or 120 days' fine. If the person who disobeys is the promoter of the gathering, the penalty rises to up to two years or 240 days' fine.

Read the article ↗
Código Penal, art. 348.º

Disobedience, simple and qualified

“Quem faltar à obediência devida a ordem ou a mandado legítimos, regularmente comunicados e emanados de autoridade ou funcionário competente…”

One year or 120 days' fine when a legal provision punishes simple disobedience, or when the authority warns that disobedience is a crime. Two years or 240 days when the law provides for qualified disobedience — which is the case of article 15.º of the 1974 decree.

Read the article ↗

The four conditions an order needs

  1. Legitimate. It must have a legal basis — the dispersal of a gathering that has met the article 5.º conditions, or an occupation without title. “Because I say so” is not a legal basis.
  2. From a competent authority. A police commander acting within his powers, not a private security guard and not an officer improvising.
  3. Regularly communicated. Clearly, to the people concerned, in a way they can understand, with enough time to comply.
  4. With the warning. Article 304 requires the explicit warning that disobedience is a crime; article 348, n.º 1, b) requires the authority to make the corresponding warning when no statute already provides for the penalty.

Detention is not a sentence. The Constitution allows detention in flagrante delito (art. 27.º, n.º 3, a) and requires it to be reviewed by a judge within 48 hours (art. 28.º). Being detained, constituted an arguido and released with a summons is the beginning of a case, not the end of one; in October 2026 the Public Prosecutor's Office proposed suspending the proceedings against the Rossio group.

07

What the police may not do

The same decree that gives the order its force also limits it. These are the limits written into the text — not an opinion about any particular operation.

art. 5.º, n.º 2

Grounds in writing

An interruption must be recorded in an auto describing the grounds, and a copy must be handed to the promoters. An order with no record has no reasons a court can review.

art. 6.º–7.º

Routes and counter-protests

Routes may be changed only to keep traffic moving, and the change must be given in writing. Police must protect the gathering from counter-demonstrators who try to stop it.

art. 15.º

Liability for preventing it

Authorities who prevent assembly outside the legal conditions face criminal and disciplinary proceedings. The provision is old and cross-references a repealed code, but it has never been repealed itself.

Read the article ↗
CRP art. 18.º, n.º 2

Proportionality

Restrictions must be limited to what is necessary to protect another constitutional right. Removing a silent camp at 6 a.m. to protect sleep is a harder argument than removing a sound system at 3 a.m.

CRP art. 21.º

Resistance

Everyone has the right to resist an order that offends their rights, and to repel force when there is no time to call the authorities. It is a real article and a dangerous one: it is read narrowly, and it is not a licence to fight.

CRP art. 22.º–23.º

Liability and the Provedor

The state is civilly liable for violations committed by its agents, and anyone can complain to the Provedor de Justiça, who has no power to annul but can recommend and publish. In October 2026 a petition asked the Provedor to clarify the 00h30 rule.
